\chapter{Analog Modulation and the Classic Radio}
\label{ch:analog}

\begin{chapterintro}
Pick up an old tabletop radio at a flea market, plug it in, wait thirty seconds for the tubes to
glow, and turn the dial. Out of a hiss of static comes a ball game, a preacher, a weather report:
voices that left a transmitter tower as a carrier whose \emph{loudness} was being wiggled by a
microphone, and that were turned back into sound by a single diode inside your radio. That trick---and
its cousin, wiggling the carrier's \emph{pitch}---ran the world's communications for most of the
twentieth century. It is still on the air: AM and FM broadcasting, aviation voice, marine and
land-mobile radio, amateur single sideband. And its ideas are everywhere inside digital radios. The
superheterodyne, the phase-locked loop, the I/Q modulator, pre-emphasis, automatic gain control, the
capture effect and the threshold effect were all discovered by engineers armed with vacuum tubes, coils
and an oscilloscope.

This chapter tells their story the way the designers lived it: as trade-offs between power, bandwidth,
receiver cost and fidelity. We build AM from the transmitter's modulation transformer to the crystal
set's cat's whisker; derive single sideband three ways; find out why FM sounds so much better than AM,
and why it suddenly stops working; take apart the five-tube radio that sat in tens of millions of
kitchens; decode the beautiful frequency arithmetic of colour television; and finish with the analog
telephone network, the system digital transmission was invented to replace. Almost every idea in the
digital chapters that follow has an ancestor here.
\end{chapterintro}

\mdcfig{ch04_timeline}{A century of analog radio in one line. The ideas of this chapter arrived in a
rush between 1906 and 1961; the digital transition that began with T1 carrier in 1962 is still
finishing the job.}

%======================================================================
\section{Why modulate at all?}
\label{sec:ch04:why}
%======================================================================

The story of this chapter runs across a century (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_timeline}), but it starts with a
simple question. Suppose you want to broadcast your voice by radio, and you try the obvious thing: connect a microphone,
through an amplifier, straight to an antenna. Nothing useful happens. A microphone produces a
baseband signal $m(t)$ with energy from roughly 100\,Hz to a few kilohertz, and there are three reasons
it will never leave the antenna in useful shape.

\mdcside{ch04_wavelengths}{How long would the antenna be? A quarter wavelength at each frequency.
Audio would need tens of kilometres of wire; a broadcast carrier needs a tall mast; FM needs a car
whip.}

First, antennas are efficient only when their size is comparable to a wavelength, and at 1\,kHz the
wavelength is 300\,km (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_wavelengths}). Second, even if you could radiate audio,
every station in the region would occupy the same spectrum, and listeners would hear all of them at
once. Third, radio waves behave very differently at different frequencies, and an engineer wants to
choose the band that suits the service: medium waves that hug the ground by day and bounce off the
ionosphere at night, short waves that hop between continents, VHF that stays within the horizon,
microwaves that go straight up to a satellite.

\term{Modulation} solves all three problems at once by translating the message up to a \term{carrier}
frequency $f_c$ chosen by the engineer and allocated by the regulator. A fourth benefit appears once
many messages share one medium: give each its own carrier and you obtain
\term{frequency-division multiplexing}, the principle behind every broadcast band and, until the 1980s,
the long-distance telephone network (Section~\ref{sec:ch04:fdm}). Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_spectrum_map}
shows where the classic analog services ended up. Notice how the services spread across almost four
decades of frequency, each placed where propagation suits its job.

\mdcfig{ch04_spectrum_map}{Where the classic analog services live. Broadcasting began at long and
medium waves (wavelengths of hundreds of metres), moved to short waves for international reach, and to
VHF and UHF for FM and television, where antennas shrink to metres and coverage shrinks to the
horizon.}

\subsection*{Two knobs on a sine wave}

A sinusoidal carrier $A_c\cos(2\pi f_c t+\phi)$ has only two things that can be varied: its amplitude
and its angle. Every analog scheme in this chapter turns one knob or both, and every digital scheme in
Part~III does the same with a discrete alphabet.

\begin{analogy}[A singer with two knobs]
Imagine a singer holding one long, steady note: that is the carrier. To send a message she can do one
of two things. She can sing louder and softer in time with the message while keeping exactly the same
pitch: that is \emph{amplitude modulation}, the message turning a volume knob. Or she can keep her
volume rock-steady and let her pitch slide up and down with the message: that is \emph{frequency
modulation}. Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_two_knobs} draws both. The analogy has a limit: a real carrier
``sings'' at a million cycles per second or more, so the message changes very slowly compared with the
note itself---thousands of carrier cycles pass during one cycle of the voice.
\end{analogy}

\mdcfig{ch04_two_knobs}{The same message sent two ways. In AM the envelope (dashed) is a copy of the
message and the zero crossings stay evenly spaced. In FM the envelope never changes, and the message is
hidden in the spacing of the crests. (The carrier here is drawn absurdly slow so that you can see
individual cycles.)}

In the language of Chapter~\ref{ch:signals}, a modulated signal is
\begin{equation}
s(t)=\re\{\tilde{s}(t)\,e^{j2\pi f_c t}\},
\label{eq:ch04:env}
\end{equation}
and each modulation is nothing more than a rule for building the complex envelope $\tilde{s}(t)$ from
$m(t)$. Table~\ref{tab:ch04:envelopes} is worth a moment's study, because it is the whole chapter in six
lines.

\begin{table}[htbp]\centering\small
\caption{The analog modulations as rules for building the complex envelope.}
\label{tab:ch04:envelopes}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Scheme & Complex envelope $\tilde s(t)$ & Bandwidth \\
\midrule
AM (DSB-LC) & $A_c[1+\mu m(t)]$ & $2W$ \\
DSB-SC & $A_c\,m(t)$ & $2W$ \\
SSB (upper) & $A_c[m(t)+j\hat m(t)]$ & $W$ \\
VSB & $A_c[m(t)+j m_v(t)]$, $m_v$ a filtered quadrature term & $W$ to $2W$ \\
PM & $A_c e^{jk_p m(t)}$ & $\approx 2(\Delta f+W)$ \\
FM & $A_c e^{j2\pi k_f\int m(\tau)d\tau}$ & $\approx 2(\Delta f+W)$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Here $W$ is the message bandwidth, $\hat m(t)$ the Hilbert transform of $m(t)$ and $\Delta f$ the peak
frequency deviation. Read the table in two halves. The amplitude modulations have \emph{real} or
\emph{analytic} envelopes and occupy a bandwidth tied to $W$. The angle modulations have
constant-magnitude envelopes and trade bandwidth for noise immunity. The first group is \emph{linear}:
the modulated signal for $m_1+m_2$ is the sum of the signals for $m_1$ and $m_2$, so spectra simply
shift. The second group is \emph{nonlinear}, and its spectrum takes Bessel functions to describe.

\begin{history}[From spark to continuous wave: Fessenden and the first voice broadcast]
Marconi's early wireless used spark transmitters: a high-voltage discharge rang a tuned circuit, which
radiated a train of damped oscillations spread over a wide band. Sparks could send Morse code but not a
voice---imagine trying to talk by banging a gong. Reginald Fessenden (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_fessenden}), a
Canadian-born engineer who had
worked for Edison, argued from around 1900 that speech needed a \emph{continuous} wave whose amplitude
could be varied smoothly, and he coined the idea of the \emph{heterodyne}: beating a received continuous
wave against a local oscillator to produce an audible difference frequency.

To get a continuous wave of tens of kilohertz he commissioned a high-frequency alternator from General
Electric, where the young Ernst Alexanderson developed it (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_alexanderson}): a
spinning machine that generated radio frequency the way a power-station generator makes 60\,Hz.
According to Fessenden's own later account, on Christmas Eve 1906 his station at Brant Rock,
Massachusetts, transmitted speech and music that were heard by ship operators along the coast.
Historians have debated the details, since the main source is Fessenden's recollection decades later,
but his experiments with amplitude-modulated continuous waves in 1906 are well documented, and the
idea---a carrier whose amplitude follows the voice---is the subject of the next section. Practical voice
broadcasting had to wait for the triode oscillator and amplifier of the 1910s; KDKA in Pittsburgh began
regular scheduled broadcasts in November 1920.
\end{history}

\section{Amplitude modulation}
\label{sec:ch04:am}
%======================================================================

\subsection{The AM signal}

On the evening of 2 November 1920, Westinghouse's station KDKA in Pittsburgh read the
Harding--Cox presidential election returns to anyone within range who owned a receiver. KDKA grew out of
Frank Conrad's amateur station 8XK (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_8xk}), whose evening broadcasts of
gramophone records had become popular enough that a department store advertised receivers to hear
them. What those listeners heard was conventional AM, formally \emph{double sideband with large
carrier} (DSB-LC). It was the first broadcast modulation and is still used on long, medium and short
waves and for aviation voice around 118--137\,MHz.

\mdcphoto[0.72]{ch04_8xk}{Frank Conrad's station 8XK in 1920, the amateur station from which
Westinghouse's KDKA grew. A broadcast transmitter of the day was an open table of coils, tubes and
wiring.}

With the message normalised so that $|m(t)|\le 1$, the AM signal is
\begin{equation}
s_{\text{AM}}(t)=A_c\,[1+\mu\,m(t)]\cos(2\pi f_c t),
\label{eq:ch04:am}
\end{equation}
where $0<\mu\le 1$ is the \term{modulation index} (or \emph{depth}; broadcasters quote it in percent).
In words: take a steady carrier, and turn its volume up and down in proportion to the message. The
envelope of the RF waveform traces $A_c[1+\mu m(t)]$, which stays positive as long as $\mu\le1$, so a
diode, a capacitor and a resistor can recover the message.

Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_am} shows a tone-modulated carrier at three indices. At $\mu=1.4$ the envelope
would need to go negative; it cannot, so the detector output folds and the audio is badly distorted.
Broadcast transmitters limit negative peaks to prevent this \term{over-modulation}, while allowing
positive peaks somewhat above 100\% because those do not break the envelope (US rules permit positive
peaks up to 125\%).

\mdcfig{ch04_am}{AM with a 500\,Hz tone on a 10\,kHz carrier for three modulation indices. The dashed
green curve is the ideal envelope $1+\mu m(t)$; the red curve is the output of a simulated full-wave
envelope detector. Over-modulation ($\mu=1.4$) makes the envelope fold, and the detected audio is
distorted.}

\begin{pitfall}[Over-modulation does not stay in your channel]
An over-modulated transmitter does not merely distort its own audio. The sharp corners where the
envelope hits zero are rich in high-frequency components, so the signal \emph{splatters} sidebands into
the neighbouring channels. This is why every broadcast audio chain ends in a peak limiter that guards the
negative peaks, and why regulators measure out-of-channel emissions rather than trusting the station's
meters.
\end{pitfall}

On an oscilloscope the modulation index can be read directly from the envelope: if $A_{\max}$ and
$A_{\min}$ are its largest and smallest values for a tone,
\begin{equation}
\mu=\frac{A_{\max}-A_{\min}}{A_{\max}+A_{\min}}.
\label{eq:ch04:muscope}
\end{equation}
A classic bench trick is the \emph{trapezoid} display (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_trapezoid}): drive the
oscilloscope's horizontal axis with the audio and its vertical axis with the RF. A linear modulator
draws a trapezoid with straight sides; curvature reveals modulator nonlinearity, and a pointed tip with
a flat ``tail'' reveals over-modulation.

\mdcfig{ch04_trapezoid}{The trapezoid pattern, the broadcast engineer's modulation meter. Straight
sides mean a linear modulator; the trapezoid closes to a point at exactly 100\%; beyond that the carrier
is cut off for part of each audio cycle and the pattern grows a flat tail.}

\subsection{Spectrum and sidebands}

What does an AM signal look like on a spectrum analyser? Taking the Fourier transform
of~\eqref{eq:ch04:am},
\begin{equation}
S_{\text{AM}}(f)=\frac{A_c}{2}\big[\delta(f-f_c)+\delta(f+f_c)\big]
+\frac{A_c\mu}{2}\big[M(f-f_c)+M(f+f_c)\big].
\label{eq:ch04:amspec}
\end{equation}
The spectrum is the message spectrum copied to either side of the carrier: an \term{upper sideband}
and a \term{lower sideband}, each a complete copy of the information, plus an unmodulated carrier line.
For a single tone $m(t)=\cos2\pi f_m t$ the identity $\cos a\cos b=\frac12[\cos(a-b)+\cos(a+b)]$ makes
it explicit:
\begin{equation}
s_{\text{AM}}(t)=A_c\cos2\pi f_ct+\frac{\mu A_c}{2}\cos2\pi(f_c+f_m)t+\frac{\mu A_c}{2}\cos2\pi(f_c-f_m)t.
\end{equation}
Three lines, the outer two at most half the height of the carrier.

\begin{analogy}[Sidebands as echoes of the message]
Think of the carrier as a tall, silent post at $f_c$. When the message is applied, two ``echoes'' of it
appear, one on each side of the post: the upper sideband is the message spectrum shifted up, the lower
sideband is its mirror image shifted down. A 1\,kHz whistle at 1000\,kHz produces echoes at 999 and
1001\,kHz; a 5\,kHz cymbal produces echoes 5\,kHz out. The limit of the analogy: the echoes are not
weaker copies arriving later in time, they are exact copies displaced in \emph{frequency}, and either one
alone contains the whole message---a fact single sideband will exploit.
\end{analogy}

The phasor picture is illuminating (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_phasors}, left). In a frame rotating at $f_c$
the carrier is a fixed arrow, and the two sidebands are smaller arrows rotating in opposite directions
at $\pm f_m$. Their sum always lies along the carrier, lengthening and shortening it; this is why AM
changes amplitude but not phase. Keep this picture in mind: in Section~\ref{sec:ch04:nbfm} we will flip
one sideband by $180^\circ$ and get FM (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_phasors}, right).

\mdcfig{ch04_phasors}{Phasors in a frame rotating with the carrier. Left: in AM the two sidebands
rotate in opposite directions and their sum always points along the carrier, so only the length
changes. Right: flip the lower sideband and the sum points across the carrier, so the angle swings:
narrowband FM.}

The occupied bandwidth is $2W$. Medium-wave (MW) broadcast channels are spaced 10\,kHz in the Americas
(530--1700\,kHz) and 9\,kHz in Europe, Africa, Asia and Oceania (531--1602\,kHz, with some countries
extending higher). Because adjacent channels are not normally assigned in the same area, US stations may
transmit audio up to 10\,kHz under the NRSC-1 standard, but typical receivers pass only 3--5\,kHz to keep
the adjacent channels out; AM's reputation for ``telephone quality'' is largely a receiver choice.
Long-wave broadcasting (148.5--283.5\,kHz in Europe and Africa) and short-wave international
broadcasting (bands between 2.3 and 26\,MHz with a 5\,kHz raster) also use DSB-LC.

\subsection{Power and efficiency}
\label{sec:ch04:ampower}

Here is AM's dirty secret: most of the power a broadcast station pays for carries no information at all.
With a tone message, the carrier power (into a 1\,$\Omega$ load) is $P_c=A_c^2/2$ and each sideband
carries $\mu^2A_c^2/8=\mu^2P_c/4$. The total is
\begin{equation}
P_t=P_c\Big(1+\frac{\mu^2}{2}\Big),\qquad\text{or}\quad P_t=P_c(1+\mu^2P_m)
\label{eq:ch04:ampower}
\end{equation}
for a general message of normalised power $P_m=\overline{m^2(t)}$. The fraction of the transmitted
power that carries information is
\begin{equation}
\eta_{\text{AM}}=\frac{\mu^2P_m}{1+\mu^2P_m}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\eta=\frac{1}{3}\ \text{for a tone at }\mu=1.
\label{eq:ch04:ameff}
\end{equation}
In other words, even in the best case two-thirds of the power is spent on the carrier.

\mdcfig[0.5]{ch04_am_power}{Where the transmitter's power goes. Grey is carrier, red is message. Real
speech, with its pauses and peaks, leaves AM spending almost nine-tenths of its power on the carrier.}

With speech it is far worse. Speech has a high peak-to-average ratio, so with peaks held at 100\%
modulation the average $P_m$ is often below 0.1 and $\eta$ falls below 10\%
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_am_power}). Broadcasters fight back with audio processing: multiband compressors
and peak limiters (the Orban Optimod family is the best known) raise the average modulation
substantially, which is why AM stations sound ``loud'' and somewhat squashed.

A practical consequence of~\eqref{eq:ch04:ampower}: the antenna current rises with modulation as
$\sqrt{1+\mu^2/2}$, by 22.5\% for 100\% tone modulation. Generations of broadcast engineers checked
their modulation with nothing more than the antenna ammeter.

Why accept such waste? Because in 1920 the only practical receiver was an envelope detector, and a
large carrier makes envelope detection possible. The transmitter is expensive, but there is one of it;
the receivers are cheap, and there are millions. That asymmetry---\emph{put the complexity where there
are few units}---recurs throughout the history of communications, from broadcast television to GPS.

\begin{worked}[An AM broadcast transmitter power budget]
A US clear-channel station runs the maximum licensed carrier power, $P_c=50$\,kW.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Tone test at 100\%.} Each sideband carries $P_c/4=12.5$\,kW, so $P_t=75$\,kW and
$\eta=1/3$. The peak envelope is $2A_c$, so the \term{peak envelope power} (PEP) is $4P_c=200$\,kW. The
final amplifier, its tubes and the antenna system must all withstand 200\,kW peaks and twice the
carrier voltage.
\item \emph{Programme audio.} With processed audio peaking at 100\% and an average $P_m\approx0.15$, the
sideband power is $50\times0.15=7.5$\,kW and $\eta=7.5/57.5\approx13\%$. More than 85\% of the radiated
power is the carrier, which conveys nothing but makes the envelope detector work.
\item \emph{Plate modulation (next subsection).} If the class-C final amplifier is 80\% efficient, its DC
input at carrier is $50/0.8=62.5$\,kW. For a 100\% tone the modulator must add audio power equal to
$\mu^2/2$ of that DC input, $31.3$\,kW---a large audio amplifier in its own right. With programme audio it
averages about $62.5\times0.15\approx9.4$\,kW.
\item \emph{Mains.} With a 90\%-efficient high-voltage supply, the station draws roughly
$(62.5+9.4)/0.9\approx80$\,kW from the grid on programme, about 1.4 times the radiated power. Modern
solid-state transmitters (below) do better still.
\end{itemize}
\end{worked}

\subsection{Generating AM: low-level and high-level modulation}
\label{sec:ch04:amtx}

There are two places to impose the modulation. In \term{low-level modulation} the carrier is modulated
at a small signal level, for example in a transistor mixer or an I/Q modulator, and then amplified to
full power by \emph{linear} amplifiers (class A or AB). This is simple but inefficient, because linear
amplifiers waste much of their DC input as heat; it is the standard method in low-power and
software-defined transmitters.

In \term{high-level modulation} the final amplifier is run as an efficient \emph{class-C} stage that is
driven hard and behaves like a switch: its RF output amplitude is simply proportional to its supply
voltage. Modulate the supply voltage, and the RF output follows. In the classic \term{plate modulation}
circuit of Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_am_tx}, the DC supply to the anode (plate) of the final tube passes
through the secondary winding of a large \emph{modulation transformer} driven by a class-B push-pull
audio amplifier, the \emph{modulator}. The audio voltage adds to and subtracts from the DC supply; at
100\% modulation it swings the plate supply between zero and twice its resting value. Because the final
amplifier's DC input power is $V_{\text{DC}}I_{\text{DC}}$ and its output follows $V^2$, the modulator
must supply the sideband power: $\mu^2/2$ of the DC input for a tone, as in the worked example. The
modulation transformer of a 50\,kW transmitter weighed tonnes and had to carry the DC plate current
without saturating its core.

The efficiency of the high-power AM transmitter has been a design obsession for a century. Raymond
Heising's constant-current modulation (around 1919) was the first practical high-level scheme; William
Doherty's 1936 amplifier, which uses a second ``peaking'' tube that turns on only during modulation
peaks, kept efficiency high across the envelope and is now, in solid-state form, the standard power
amplifier of 4G and 5G base stations; Leonard Kahn's \emph{envelope elimination and restoration} (1952)
amplified a constant-envelope RF signal with a saturated amplifier and restored the envelope through the
supply. The output of all this ingenuity goes to an antenna like those of
Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_mw_masts}: at medium waves the whole steel mast is the radiator.

\mdcphoto[0.8]{ch04_mw_masts}{A medium-wave transmitter site at Szombathely, Hungary. At 1\,MHz a
quarter wavelength is 75\,m, so the antenna is the mast itself: a self-radiating steel tower, fed at its
base or, in one of these designs, partway up, standing on a buried network of ground radials.}

\begin{inpractice}[title=Modern AM transmitters]
A modern high-power AM transmitter does not use a modulation transformer or a linear amplifier.
Pulse-duration modulation (PDM) transmitters of the 1970s replaced the class-B modulator with a
switching (class-D) amplifier that drives the RF stage's supply through a low-pass filter. Since the
late 1980s the dominant design has been \emph{digital amplitude modulation}: dozens of solid-state RF
modules, each switched fully on or off, whose outputs are summed in a combiner so that the number of
active modules follows the sampled audio---a power DAC operating at RF. Switching amplifiers are
85--90\% efficient or better. The same idea, \emph{envelope tracking}, is used in every 4G and 5G
handset to power the PA efficiently while it amplifies a high peak-to-average OFDM signal
(Chapter~\ref{ch:sdr}). Broadcasters also save energy with \emph{modulation-dependent carrier level}
(MDCL) control, which reduces the carrier during loud passages when the sidebands are strong and the
listener would not notice; it can save a substantial fraction of the electricity bill.
\end{inpractice}

\subsection{The envelope detector}
\label{sec:ch04:envdet}

At the other end of the link sits the cheapest demodulator ever devised: a diode followed by a parallel
$RC$ network (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_detector_circuits}b). On each positive half-cycle the diode conducts
and charges the capacitor to the peak; between peaks the diode is reverse biased and the capacitor
discharges slowly through $R$. The capacitor voltage therefore rides along the tops of the carrier
cycles, tracing out the envelope. Two constraints fix the time constant:
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{f_c}\ll RC\ll\frac{1}{W}.
\label{eq:ch04:rc}
\end{equation}
The capacitor must hold its charge across one carrier cycle, but let go fast enough to follow the
voice. If $RC$ is too short the output ripples at the carrier frequency. If it is too long the capacitor
cannot follow the falling envelope, and the output shows \term{diagonal clipping}: it decays
exponentially along a straight-looking diagonal instead of following the envelope down
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_envelope_clip}). The worst case is deep modulation at the highest audio frequency,
when the envelope falls fastest. For a tone the limit works out to
\begin{equation}
RC\le\frac{\sqrt{1-\mu^2}}{2\pi f_m\,\mu}.
\label{eq:ch04:diagclip}
\end{equation}
The highest modulating frequency and the deepest modulation set the limit. As $\mu\to1$ the bound goes
to zero: at 100\% modulation some diagonal clipping on the troughs is inevitable, one of several reasons
broadcasters leave a little headroom on negative peaks.

\begin{deeper}[Deriving the diagonal-clipping limit]
The envelope of a tone-modulated carrier is $a(t)=A_c(1+\mu\cos\omega_m t)$, and the capacitor voltage,
when the diode is off, falls with slope $-v/RC$. The detector can follow only if the envelope never falls
faster than this:
\begin{equation}
-\frac{da}{dt}\le\frac{a(t)}{RC}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
RC\le\frac{1+\mu\cos\omega_m t}{\mu\,\omega_m\sin\omega_m t}\quad\text{for all }t.
\end{equation}
Minimising the right-hand side over $t$ (the minimum occurs at $\cos\omega_m t=-\mu$, where
$\sin\omega_m t=\sqrt{1-\mu^2}$ and the numerator is $1-\mu^2$) gives~\eqref{eq:ch04:diagclip}.
\end{deeper}

\mdcfig{ch04_envelope_clip}{Simulated ideal-diode envelope detector (red) on a 50\,kHz carrier modulated
80\% by a 1\,kHz tone; the dashed green curve is the true envelope. Too short a time constant leaves
carrier ripple; too long a time constant produces diagonal clipping on the falling half of each cycle.
The limit~\eqref{eq:ch04:diagclip} is 119\,$\mu$s here.}

Drag the modulation-index slider past 1.0 and watch the detected audio fold over; then set $\mu=0.8$
and slide the detector's $RC$ from 5 to 500\,$\mu$s. You will see carrier ripple at one end, diagonal

\end{tryit}

A second, subtler distortion comes from the load. The detector's DC load is $R$, but the audio signal is
coupled through a capacitor to the next stage, whose input resistance $R_L$ appears in parallel at audio
frequencies. The \emph{AC load} $R_{ac}=R\parallel R_L$ is smaller than the DC load, and the detector can
then follow modulation troughs only if
\begin{equation}
\mu\le\frac{R_{ac}}{R_{dc}},
\label{eq:ch04:negclip}
\end{equation}
or the troughs are flattened (\emph{negative-peak clipping}). Radio designers kept $R_L$ large, or tapped
the diode load, to keep this ratio near one. Finally, a real diode has a forward drop and a curved
characteristic at low levels; a germanium or Schottky diode with a small drop, and an IF level of at
least a volt or so at the detector, keep the detector operating in its \emph{linear} (large-signal)
region. At low levels any diode becomes a \emph{square-law} detector, whose output contains
$\big(1+\mu m(t)\big)^2$, and therefore second-harmonic distortion of relative amplitude about $\mu/4$ for
a tone---25\% at full modulation. Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_negclip} shows trough clipping at work.

\mdcfig{ch04_negclip}{Negative-peak clipping (simulation, 90\% modulation). With an AC load almost equal to
the DC load the detector follows the troughs (left); with $R_{ac}/R_{dc}=0.82$ it cannot discharge below the
dotted level, and the bottoms of the waveform are sliced off (right).}

\begin{worked}[Designing an envelope detector]
A 455\,kHz IF carries AM with audio up to 5\,kHz at up to 90\% modulation. The diagonal clipping limit at
5\,kHz and $\mu=0.9$ is $RC\le\sqrt{1-0.81}/(2\pi\cdot 5000\cdot 0.9)=15.4\,\mu$s. The ripple condition
asks for $RC\gg 1/455\,\text{kHz}=2.2\,\mu$s. Choosing $R=10$\,k$\Omega$ and $C=1$\,nF gives
$RC=10\,\mu$s, which satisfies both. The remaining 455\,kHz ripple is removed by a simple audio low-pass
filter.

Now the AC load. If the audio amplifier that follows has an input resistance of 47\,k$\Omega$, then
$R_{ac}=10\parallel47=8.25$\,k$\Omega$ and~\eqref{eq:ch04:negclip} limits clean detection to
$\mu\le0.82$: troughs of the 90\%-modulated signal would be clipped. Raising the amplifier input
resistance to 220\,k$\Omega$ gives $R_{ac}/R_{dc}=0.96$ and solves the problem
(compare the two halves of Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_negclip}). In the vacuum-tube radios
of Section~\ref{sec:ch04:aa5} the volume control itself (typically 500\,k$\Omega$ to 2\,M$\Omega$) was the
detector load, with a grid resistor of several megohms after it.
\end{worked}

\subsection{The crystal set}

The simplest radio receiver ever built has no power source at all, and in the early 1920s it was most
people's first radio (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_crystal_family}). A long-wire antenna feeds a tuned circuit (a
coil and a variable capacitor), whose resonance selects a station; a \emph{crystal detector}---a fine
wire, the \emph{cat's whisker}, touching a crystal of galena (lead sulphide) or silicon---rectifies the
RF; and a pair of high-impedance headphones, with a small capacitor across them, acts as the $RC$ filter
and transducer in one (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_detector_circuits}a).

\mdcphoto[0.72]{ch04_crystal_family}{Listening to a crystal set in the early 1920s, from a 1922
advertisement. Every listener needed headphones: with no amplifier, the only power available was the
few microwatts captured by the antenna.}

All the audio power comes from the received signal itself, which is why crystal sets work only near
strong stations and need headphones of a few thousand ohms. Finding a ``hot spot'' on the crystal with
the cat's whisker was a delicate, fiddly ritual (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_catswhisker}): the rectifying
junction is a tiny point contact, and only some spots on a natural crystal work. The point-contact
rectifier was patented by Greenleaf Pickard (silicon, 1906) and others. Soldiers in the Second World
War built ``foxhole radios'' from a razor blade and a pencil lead (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_foxhole}); a
receiver with no power supply also radiates nothing that an enemy could find.

The crystal set is also a lesson in selectivity. Its single tuned circuit, loaded by the antenna and the
detector, has a loaded $Q$ of perhaps 20--50, so at 1\,MHz its bandwidth is 20--50\,kHz, several
broadcast channels. Selectivity good enough for a crowded band needs more tuned circuits, more gain, and
eventually the superheterodyne of Section~\ref{sec:ch04:superhet}. The point-contact crystal itself had a
second career: the silicon point-contact diodes of wartime radar mixers led, through the Bell Labs
semiconductor programme, to the transistor of 1947.

\subsection{Synchronous detection of AM}

The envelope detector has two weaknesses. It fails when the noise is comparable to the carrier (the AM
threshold, Section~\ref{sec:ch04:noise}), and it distorts badly under \emph{selective fading}, which
short-wave listeners know well: at night the ionosphere delivers several copies of the signal with
different delays, and their interference can suppress the carrier while the sidebands survive. The
envelope then dips through zero and the detector produces a harsh, gargling distortion. A
\term{synchronous detector} regenerates a clean local carrier, locked to the received one by a
phase-locked loop, and multiplies, exactly as for DSB-SC below. It is immune to carrier fades, and it
can select either sideband to escape interference on the other. Many high-end short-wave receivers offer a ``SYNC'' mode for this reason, and every SDR can implement it in a
few lines. Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_selective_fading} shows the difference.

\mdcfig{ch04_selective_fading}{A selective fade has knocked the carrier down by 20\,dB while the sidebands
survive (simulation). The envelope now dips through zero, and the envelope detector (left) delivers a
rectified, doubled-frequency mess; a synchronous detector (right), with its own clean carrier, recovers the
tone perfectly.}

\subsection{The signal and coherent detection}

We saw that a broadcast AM station spends most of its power on a carrier that says nothing. The obvious
question is: why send it at all? If the receiver can regenerate the carrier itself, it does not need to be
transmitted. Removing it gives DSB-SC,
\begin{equation}
s_{\text{DSB}}(t)=A_c\,m(t)\cos(2\pi f_c t),
\label{eq:ch04:dsb}
\end{equation}
whose spectrum is~\eqref{eq:ch04:amspec} without the delta functions (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_am_family}).
All transmitted power is now in the sidebands. But there is a catch: the envelope is now $|m(t)|$, and
the phase flips by $180^\circ$ every time $m(t)$ changes sign, so an envelope detector produces a
rectified, badly distorted message. The receiver must instead multiply by a \emph{coherent} local
carrier $\cos(2\pi f_c t+\theta)$ and low-pass filter:
\begin{equation}
\text{LPF}\{s_{\text{DSB}}(t)\cos(2\pi f_c t+\theta)\}=\tfrac{A_c}{2}m(t)\cos\theta.
\label{eq:ch04:coh}
\end{equation}
A phase error $\theta$ scales the output by $\cos\theta$; at $90^\circ$ the output vanishes completely. A
frequency error $\Delta f$ is worse: the output beats as $m(t)\cos(2\pi\Delta f t)$, a periodic fading at
$2\Delta f$ that is intolerable for even a fraction of a hertz (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_ssb_mistune}, left).
Coherent detection therefore needs a carrier recovery loop.

\mdcfig{ch04_am_family}{Spectra of the four amplitude modulations with a voice-like message on a 12\,kHz
carrier: conventional AM carries a strong carrier line; DSB-SC removes it; the two SSB signals keep only
one sideband, upper or lower.}

\begin{pitfall}[A coherent detector is only as good as its carrier]
It is tempting to think of the local oscillator in~\eqref{eq:ch04:coh} as a detail. It is not. With
DSB-SC, a 1\,Hz frequency error makes the voice swell and vanish twice a second, and a $90^\circ$ phase
error makes it disappear. Every coherent receiver in this book---DSB, QAM, PSK, OFDM---lives or dies by
its carrier recovery, which is why Chapter~\ref{ch:sync} is devoted to it.
\end{pitfall}

\subsection{Balanced and ring modulators}
\label{sec:ch04:ring}

Generating~\eqref{eq:ch04:dsb} requires a multiplier. Accurate analog multipliers are hard to build, but a
\emph{switch} is easy, and it turns out that switching is enough. Multiply $m(t)$ by a square wave $p(t)$
that alternates between $+1$ and $-1$ at $f_c$. Its Fourier series is
\begin{equation}
p(t)=\frac{4}{\pi}\Big[\cos\omega_ct-\frac13\cos3\omega_ct+\frac15\cos5\omega_ct-\cdots\Big],
\end{equation}
so $m(t)p(t)$ contains the wanted DSB-SC term $(4/\pi)m(t)\cos\omega_ct$ plus copies around $3f_c$,
$5f_c$, \dots, which a band-pass filter removes (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_switching_mod}). Neither the
carrier nor the message appears alone at the output: the modulator is \term{balanced}.

\mdcfig{ch04_switching_mod}{Multiplication by switching. Reversing the message's polarity at the carrier
rate (left) produces a signal whose spectrum (right) contains DSB-SC copies at $f_c$, $3f_c$ and $5f_c$,
and neither the carrier nor the original message. A band-pass filter keeps the copy at $f_c$.}

The \term{ring modulator} (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_ring}) implements the polarity-reversing switch with four
diodes and two centre-tapped transformers. A strong carrier applied between the centre taps turns on one
pair of diodes during its positive half-cycles, connecting the transformers ``straight'', and the other
pair during negative half-cycles, connecting them ``crossed''. The message passing through is therefore
multiplied by $\pm1$ at the carrier rate.

The ring modulator was invented in the 1930s for the carrier telephone systems of
Section~\ref{sec:ch04:fdm}, where it generated the SSB channels of every channel bank. With hot-carrier
(Schottky) diodes and ferrite transformers it remains the \emph{double-balanced mixer} found in RF test
equipment and high-dynamic-range receivers, valued for its isolation between ports and its excellent
intermodulation performance. Its integrated-circuit successor is the \term{Gilbert cell}, Barrie
Gilbert's 1968 transistor four-quadrant multiplier, which became the standard active mixer of bipolar
and BiCMOS radio chips. (Many CMOS transceivers, of the kind inside the B200 of Chapter~\ref{ch:sdr},
instead use passive switching mixers---the ring modulator's principle again, built from MOS switches.)

\subsection{Recovering the carrier}
\label{sec:ch04:carrierrec}

How does a DSB-SC receiver obtain a coherent carrier when none is transmitted? It turns out the carrier
is hiding in the signal; you just have to know how to look. Squaring the received signal gives
\begin{equation}
s_{\text{DSB}}^2(t)=\tfrac{A_c^2}{2}m^2(t)+\tfrac{A_c^2}{2}m^2(t)\cos(4\pi f_ct),
\end{equation}
and since $m^2(t)$ has a nonzero mean, the second term contains a discrete spectral line at $2f_c$. A
narrow band-pass filter or phase-locked loop extracts it, and a divide-by-two gives the carrier---with a
$180^\circ$ ambiguity, since squaring cannot tell $m$ from $-m$. This is the \term{squaring loop}.

John Costas's 1956 alternative uses two product detectors driven in quadrature by a voltage-controlled
oscillator: the in-phase arm gives $\frac12m(t)\cos\theta$, the quadrature arm $\frac12m(t)\sin\theta$,
and their product $\frac18m^2(t)\sin2\theta$ is a phase-error signal that steers the oscillator,
independent of the sign of $m$. The in-phase arm is then the demodulated output. The \term{Costas loop},
born for DSB-SC voice, recovers the carrier of every BPSK and QPSK signal in Chapter~\ref{ch:sync}
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_costas}).

\caption{The Costas loop. The in-phase arm (top) delivers the demodulated message; the product of the two
arms is proportional to $\sin2\theta$ whatever the sign of $m(t)$, and steers the VCO until the phase
error $\theta$ is zero (or $180^\circ$).}
\label{fig:ch04_costas}
\end{figure}

A further trick doubles the capacity of DSB-SC. Two independent messages can share one carrier frequency
if one modulates $\cos\omega_ct$ and the other $\sin\omega_ct$:
\begin{equation}
s(t)=m_1(t)\cos\omega_ct-m_2(t)\sin\omega_ct,
\end{equation}
because coherent detection with $\cos$ and $\sin$ separates them exactly. This \term{quadrature amplitude
modulation} (QAM) carried the colour information of NTSC and PAL television (Section~\ref{sec:ch04:tv})
and AM stereo (Section~\ref{sec:ch04:digitalradio}), and with discrete-valued $m_1,m_2$ it becomes the
QAM of Chapter~\ref{ch:modulation}. Its weakness is the same as DSB-SC's: a phase error $\theta$ leaks
$m_2$ into $m_1$ in proportion to $\sin\theta$, which is why NTSC receivers needed a ``tint'' control.

\section{Single sideband}
\label{sec:ch04:ssb}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Only half the spectrum is needed}

Look again at the AM spectrum and you will notice something wasteful: for a real message, the lower
sideband is just the mirror image of the upper. Both echoes carry the same information. Transmitting
both wastes half the bandwidth. \term{Single sideband} (SSB) sends one. Its complex envelope is the
analytic signal of the message,
\begin{equation}
\tilde s_{\text{USB}}(t)=m(t)+j\hat m(t),\qquad
s_{\text{USB}}(t)=m(t)\cos 2\pi f_c t-\hat m(t)\sin 2\pi f_c t,
\label{eq:ch04:ssb}
\end{equation}
where $\hat m$ is the Hilbert transform (Chapter~\ref{ch:signals}). Changing the sign of the second term
gives the lower sideband. To see why~\eqref{eq:ch04:ssb} has only an upper sideband, recall that the
analytic signal $m+j\hat m$ has spectrum $2M(f)$ for $f>0$ and zero for $f<0$; multiplying by
$e^{j2\pi f_ct}$ shifts this one-sided spectrum up to $f_c$, and taking the real part adds the mirror
image at $-f_c$. Nothing lands below $f_c$ (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_analytic}).

\mdcfig{ch04_analytic}{How the Hilbert transform builds single sideband. A real message has mirror-image
positive and negative halves (left). Adding $j\hat m$ cancels the negative half and doubles the positive
one (centre). Shift that one-sided spectrum to $f_c$ and take the real part: only the upper sideband
survives (right).}

SSB occupies bandwidth $W$, puts all its power into information, and fades more gracefully than AM
because there is no carrier to suffer selective fading. Its envelope is $\sqrt{m^2+\hat m^2}$, which is
\emph{not} the message; an SSB signal with no modulation is silent, which also saves power between words.
SSB has been the standard for long-haul HF voice since the 1950s: military, maritime, aeronautical and
amateur. Its analog-telephony cousin, SSB frequency-division multiplexing, carried the long-distance
telephone network (Section~\ref{sec:ch04:fdm}).

\begin{history}[Carson, single sideband and the transatlantic telephone]
Single sideband was born in the Bell System. John Renshaw Carson of AT\&T worked out in 1915 that one
sideband of a modulated carrier carried all the information, and patented the idea (US patent
1,449,382, granted 1923). AT\&T used it first on wire: carrier telephone systems of the late 1910s
stacked several SSB voice channels on one open-wire pair. Its most dramatic early use was radio. On
7 January 1927 the first commercial transatlantic radiotelephone service opened between New York and
London, using single sideband on a long-wave carrier of about 60\,kHz transmitted from Rocky Point,
Long Island; a three-minute call cost 75 dollars---well over a thousand dollars in today's money.

On HF, SSB displaced AM in the 1950s, helped decisively by the military. Arthur Collins's company in
Cedar Rapids, which had built SSB equipment for the US Air Force, demonstrated it to General Curtis
LeMay's Strategic Air Command, whose bombers needed reliable voice contact across continents; SAC's
adoption of SSB in the mid-1950s set the pattern for military HF worldwide, and amateurs followed within
a decade, many of them with Collins equipment (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_kwm2a}).
\end{history}

\mdcphoto[0.62]{ch04_kwm2a}{A Collins KWM-2A HF single-sideband transceiver, a classic of the 1960s.
Note the EMISSION switch (LSB, USB, TUNE/LOCK) and the large tuning dial: an SSB operator tunes by ear
until the voice sounds natural.}

\subsection{Three ways to generate SSB}
\label{sec:ch04:ssbgen}

There are three classic recipes for throwing away a sideband: filter it out, cancel it, or never create
it in the first place.

\paragraph{The filter method.} Generate DSB-SC at a fixed frequency and remove one sideband with a sharp
band-pass filter. The difficulty is the gap between the sidebands: with voice from 300\,Hz to 3\,kHz the
filter must pass $f_c+300$\,Hz and reject $f_c-300$\,Hz, a transition band of 600\,Hz, with perhaps
50--60\,dB of rejection. At 9\,MHz that is a fractional transition of $7\times10^{-5}$, which needs a
crystal \emph{lattice} filter of six to ten quartz resonators; at 455\,kHz, Collins's \emph{mechanical
filters} of the early 1950s used resonant nickel-alloy discs coupled by wires and driven by
magnetostrictive transducers. The required gap is the reason voice SSB traditionally cuts off below
300\,Hz. After the filter, mixers translate the SSB signal to the operating frequency. Almost every SSB
transceiver built from the 1950s to the 2000s used this method, at an IF of 455\,kHz, 3--9\,MHz or
(later) 8--10.7\,MHz, and it is the reason ``SSB filter'' remains a catalogue item (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_mechfilter}).

\mdcphoto[0.5]{ch04_mechfilter}{Two Collins mechanical filters from a 75A-4 receiver of the 1950s. Inside
each can, a chain of resonant metal discs passes a narrow band around 455\,kHz; the one on the left is a
500\,Hz filter for Morse code.}

\paragraph{The phasing method.} Implement~\eqref{eq:ch04:ssb} directly (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_ssb_blocks}a):
two balanced modulators driven by carriers in quadrature, one fed with $m(t)$ and the other with a
$90^\circ$-shifted copy $\hat m(t)$. The unwanted sideband is produced twice, with opposite signs, and
cancels; the wanted one adds. Ralph Hartley patented the method in 1928 and it is often called the
\emph{Hartley modulator}. No sharp filter is needed, but the unwanted sideband cancels only as well as
the two paths match, and an analog $90^\circ$ network that is accurate over a decade of audio is hard to
build (the classic solution is a pair of all-pass networks whose phase \emph{difference} stays near
$90^\circ$ over 300--3000\,Hz). If the paths have relative gain $g$ and a phase error $\phi$, the ratio of
wanted to unwanted sideband power is
\begin{equation}
\text{SSR}=\frac{|1+g e^{j\phi}|^2}{|1-ge^{j\phi}|^2}=\frac{1+2g\cos\phi+g^2}{1-2g\cos\phi+g^2}
\approx\frac{4}{\phi^2+(1-g)^2}
\label{eq:ch04:ssr}
\end{equation}
for small errors. Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_ssb_phase} shows how quickly suppression degrades: a $2^\circ$
phase error alone limits it to about 35\,dB, and 1\,dB of gain error to about 25\,dB.

\mdcfig[0.62]{ch04_ssb_phase}{Unwanted-sideband suppression of the phasing method as a function of the
phase error of the 90$^\circ$ network, for three gain mismatches. The same curves give the image rejection
of any I/Q modulator or zero-IF receiver.}

\begin{keyidea}[The phasing equation is everywhere]
Equation~\eqref{eq:ch04:ssr} is precisely the \emph{image rejection} problem of every I/Q transmitter
and direct-conversion receiver, including the B200 of Chapter~\ref{ch:sdr}: it is their image-rejection
ratio. When an engineer calibrates the I/Q balance of a modern radio chip, she is tuning a 1928 Hartley
modulator.
\end{keyidea}

\paragraph{The Weaver method.} Donald Weaver's ``third method'' (1956) avoids both the sharp filter and
the wideband $90^\circ$ network. Shift the audio band, 300--3000\,Hz, so that its centre $f_0=1650$\,Hz
moves to 0\,Hz by multiplying by $e^{-j2\pi f_0t}$ (two real mixers with quadrature audio-frequency
oscillators). The positive-frequency half of the message now occupies $\pm1350$\,Hz around DC, and the
negative-frequency half has moved down to $-1950$ to $-4650$\,Hz. Two identical low-pass filters with
cutoff 1350\,Hz remove the latter; then a second quadrature mixer at $f_c+f_0$ translates the remaining
complex signal to the output frequency, where taking the real part gives the upper sideband.
Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_weaver} follows the spectrum through each stage.

The unwanted sideband is rejected by the low-pass filters, which are easy to build and to match, and the
quadrature oscillators need to be accurate only at single frequencies. The price is that any residual
carrier leakage in the second mixer lands in the \emph{middle} of the audio band, at $f_0$, where it is
audible as a tone. In DSP, where matching is perfect, Weaver's method and its relatives are the standard
way of generating and demodulating SSB.

\mdcfig{ch04_weaver}{The Weaver SSB modulator, simulated with a voice-band message. (a) The real message
has both positive and negative frequencies. (b) Mixing with $e^{-j2\pi f_0t}$ centres the positive half on

\subsection{Receiving SSB}

An SSB receiver is a coherent \emph{product detector} that multiplies the IF signal by a local
\term{beat-frequency oscillator} (BFO), placed where the suppressed carrier would have been, and low-pass
filters. With a local carrier in error by $\Delta f$ and $\theta$, the output is
\begin{equation}
\tfrac12\big[m(t)\cos(2\pi\Delta f\,t+\theta)\mp\hat m(t)\sin(2\pi\Delta f\,t+\theta)\big],
\end{equation}
which is the message with every frequency component shifted by $\Delta f$ (and its phases rotated by
$\theta$). This is where SSB is kinder than DSB-SC. A pure phase error is harmless to the ear, which is
insensitive to phase. A frequency error is a pitch shift, not the deep periodic fading of DSB-SC
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_ssb_mistune}).

\mdcfig{ch04_ssb_mistune}{Mistuning, two ways. Left: a DSB-SC receiver whose carrier is 1\,Hz off makes
the voice swell and vanish twice a second. Right: an SSB receiver 70\,Hz off shifts every harmonic of a
120\,Hz voice by the same 70\,Hz, so they are no longer multiples of the pitch: the famous ``Donald Duck''
sound.}

Speech stays intelligible up to about 50--100\,Hz of error, which is why SSB could be used with
free-running oscillators, and why a mistuned SSB voice sounds like \emph{Donald Duck}: the harmonics of
the voice, which should be at integer multiples $kf_0$ of the pitch, land at $kf_0+\Delta f$ and lose
their harmonic relationship. Music, whose harmonic structure carries its meaning, cannot tolerate even a
few hertz, which is one reason SSB was never adopted for broadcasting. Tuning an SSB receiver is a skill:
an operator adjusts the BFO (or the main tuning) until the voice sounds natural, and modern transceivers
with synthesised oscillators accurate to a few hertz make the problem largely disappear.

Variants transmit a little help. \emph{Reduced-carrier} or \emph{pilot-carrier} SSB sends the carrier
10--20\,dB down, so that the receiver can lock to it; it was used on point-to-point HF radiotelephone
links and is the basis of the ``AM-equivalent'' (H3E) mode permitted on some maritime distress
frequencies, which an ordinary AM receiver can demodulate with some distortion. \emph{Independent
sideband} (ISB) puts different programmes on the upper and lower sidebands of one carrier.

\begin{inpractice}[title=Who uses SSB today]
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Amateur radio.} By long convention, voice SSB uses the lower sideband below 10\,MHz and the
upper sideband above it (an accident of 1950s filter designs and mixing schemes that became a standard).
Digital modes such as FT8 are generated as audio and sent through an SSB transmitter in a 3\,kHz
channel---the SSB exciter as a general-purpose up-converter.
\item \emph{Maritime and aeronautical HF.} Ship-to-shore and oceanic air-traffic voice use upper
sideband, on channel plans in the ITU Radio Regulations; airliners crossing oceans beyond VHF range use
HF USB, now supplemented by satellite links and HF data link.
\item \emph{Military HF.} Upper-sideband 3\,kHz channels carry voice and data modems (MIL-STD-188-110
serial-tone and wideband waveforms) with automatic link establishment (MIL-STD-188-141).
\item \emph{SSB in disguise.} Every I/Q transmitter that sends a single tone offset from its centre
frequency is an SSB modulator; every low-IF receiver relies on the same image cancellation.
\end{itemize}
\end{inpractice}

\mdcfig{ch04_ssb_vs_am}{The SSB advantage in one picture. Both transmitters are limited to the same peak
envelope (dotted). AM must reserve half its peak swing for the carrier; SSB uses the whole swing for the
message, and its receiver admits half the noise bandwidth: 9\,dB better, as the worked example shows.}

\begin{worked}[The SSB power advantage over AM]
Compare AM at 100\% tone modulation with SSB at the same \emph{peak envelope power}
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_ssb_vs_am}), the quantity a
transmitter's tubes or transistors limit. Let the AM carrier amplitude be $A$, so AM's PEP is
$(2A)^2/2=2A^2$. An SSB tone of amplitude $A_s$ has PEP $A_s^2/2$; equal PEP gives $A_s=2A$.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{AM:} the envelope detector output is $A\cos\omega_mt$, signal power $A^2/2$. The in-phase
noise in the $2W$ IF bandwidth has power $2N_0W$. Output SNR $=A^2/(4N_0W)$.
\item \emph{SSB:} the product detector (with a gain-2 reference) outputs $A_s\cos\omega_mt$, signal
power $A_s^2/2=2A^2$, and noise $N_0W$ in the $W$-wide IF. Output SNR $=2A^2/(N_0W)$.
\end{itemize}
The ratio is 8, or 9\,dB: 3\,dB from halving the noise bandwidth and 6\,dB from putting the whole
peak-power budget into one sideband rather than into a carrier. On speech, with its high peak-to-average
ratio, the practical advantage is often quoted as larger still, because SSB transmits nothing in the
pauses. This 9\,dB---an eightfold transmitter---is why SSB won the HF bands.
\end{worked}

\section{Vestigial sideband}
\label{sec:ch04:vsb}
%======================================================================

Television engineers faced a puzzle that SSB could not solve. Video extends right down to DC: a flat grey
picture is a constant voltage. A clean SSB filter with its transition at DC is impossible, and DSB wastes
spectrum a TV channel cannot afford. The compromise is \term{vestigial sideband} (VSB): keep one
sideband and a gradually rolled-off \emph{vestige} of the other, arranged so that the two overlapping
pieces add back up to exactly the right thing after detection.

\mdcfig[0.85]{ch04_vsb}{Vestigial sideband in pictures. Top: a receiver IF response with a linear
``Nyquist slope'' through the picture carrier, where it passes half amplitude. Bottom: after detection the
vestige folds back onto the upper sideband, and the two slopes add to a perfectly flat response.}

Let the VSB signal be DSB-SC passed through a band-pass filter $H(f)$. Coherent detection shifts the
spectrum back to baseband, where the two halves overlap, and the output spectrum is
\begin{equation}
Y(f)=\tfrac{A_c}{4}M(f)\big[H(f+f_c)+H(f-f_c)\big],\qquad |f|<W.
\label{eq:ch04:vsb}
\end{equation}
The message is recovered without distortion if
\begin{equation}
H(f+f_c)+H(f-f_c)=\text{constant},\qquad |f|\le W:
\label{eq:ch04:vsbcond}
\end{equation}
the filter's response must be antisymmetric about $f_c$, passing exactly half amplitude at the carrier and
trading the vestige of one sideband against the missing part of the other
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_vsb}). Readers who have met the Nyquist criterion for zero intersymbol interference
(Chapter~\ref{ch:baseband}) will recognise the same symmetry condition, discovered twice; and indeed
television engineers called the receiver's sloping IF response around the picture carrier the
\emph{Nyquist slope}.

Analog TV used VSB in a particular, pragmatic way. The transmitter sent the full upper sideband and only
0.75\,MHz of the lower, with a flat response; the \emph{receiver}'s IF filter then applied the Nyquist
slope, $-6$\,dB at the picture carrier, to satisfy~\eqref{eq:ch04:vsbcond}, and an envelope detector
demodulated the result. With a large carrier, envelope detection of VSB is nearly distortion-free; the
small \emph{quadrature distortion} it introduces was acceptable for pictures. The digital ATSC standard
that replaced NTSC kept VSB, as 8-level VSB carrying 19.39\,Mb/s in 6\,MHz with a small pilot at the old
carrier position, a direct descendant of this analog design.

\caption{Instantaneous frequency. Top: an FM signal. Bottom: its instantaneous frequency
$f_i(t)$, which is simply a copy of the message riding on $f_c$ and swinging by $\pm\Delta f$.}\label{fig:ch04_inst_freq}\end{figure}

\section{Angle modulation}
\label{sec:ch04:angle}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Phase and frequency modulation}

Now we turn the other knob. Angle modulation keeps the amplitude constant and varies the phase:
\begin{equation}
s(t)=A_c\cos\big(2\pi f_c t+\phi(t)\big).
\end{equation}
What does ``frequency'' mean for a signal whose frequency is changing? The answer is the
\term{instantaneous frequency}: the rate of change of the total phase, divided by $2\pi$,
\begin{equation}
f_i(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{d}{dt}\big[2\pi f_ct+\phi(t)\big]=f_c+\frac{1}{2\pi}\dot\phi(t).
\label{eq:ch04:finst}
\end{equation}
This definition generalises the frequency of a sinusoid in exactly the way a car's speedometer
generalises ``distance divided by time''. In \term{phase modulation} (PM), $\phi(t)=k_p m(t)$. In
\term{frequency modulation} (FM), the instantaneous frequency follows the message,
\begin{equation}
f_i(t)=f_c+k_f\,m(t),\qquad \phi(t)=2\pi k_f\int_{-\infty}^t m(\tau)\,d\tau.
\label{eq:ch04:fm}
\end{equation}
FM of $m$ is PM of the integral of $m$, and PM of $m$ is FM of its derivative; the two differ only by a
filter in front of the modulator. Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_inst_freq} shows the idea: the speedometer needle,
$f_i(t)$, swings up and down with the message.

The \term{peak frequency deviation} is $\Delta f=k_f\max|m(t)|$: how far the pitch swings. For a tone
$m(t)=\cos2\pi f_mt$ the phase is $\phi(t)=\beta\sin2\pi f_mt$, with \term{modulation index}
\begin{equation}
\beta=\frac{\Delta f}{f_m},
\end{equation}
the peak phase excursion in radians. For a general message the analogous quantity is the
\emph{deviation ratio} $D=\Delta f/W$. Note the inverse dependence on $f_m$: with a fixed deviation, low
audio frequencies produce large phase swings and high audio frequencies small ones. In PM the phase swing
is the same for all audio frequencies, and the frequency deviation grows with $f_m$. Broadcast FM with
pre-emphasis (Section~\ref{sec:ch04:preemph}) is, in fact, PM at high audio frequencies and FM at low ones.
Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_fm_vs_pm} contrasts the two.

\mdcfig{ch04_fm_vs_pm}{FM and PM respond differently to the pitch of the message. For a full-scale tone, FM
keeps the frequency swing fixed (right), so the phase swing $\beta=\Delta f/f_m$ falls as the tone rises
(left); PM keeps the phase swing fixed, so its frequency swing grows with the tone.}

Because the amplitude of an angle-modulated signal is constant, it can be amplified by saturated, highly
efficient class-C power amplifiers without distortion, and a receiver can strip off amplitude noise with
a \emph{limiter} before demodulating. Both properties were central to FM's adoption, and both reappear in
the constant-envelope digital modulations (GMSK in GSM, FSK in Bluetooth and LoRa's chirps) of later
chapters.

\subsection{Narrowband FM and the phasor picture}
\label{sec:ch04:nbfm}

If the peak phase deviation is small, $|\phi(t)|\ll1$ rad, then
$\cos(\omega_ct+\phi)\approx\cos\omega_ct-\phi(t)\sin\omega_ct$, and
\begin{equation}
s_{\text{NBFM}}(t)\approx A_c\cos\omega_ct-A_c\phi(t)\sin\omega_ct.
\label{eq:ch04:nbfm}
\end{equation}
This is \term{narrowband FM} (or PM): a carrier plus a DSB-SC signal in \emph{quadrature} with it. Compare
AM, $A_c\cos\omega_ct+A_c\mu m(t)\cos\omega_ct$, where the DSB-SC term is \emph{in phase} with the carrier.
For a tone the difference is a single sign: narrowband FM has the same three spectral lines as AM, at
$f_c$ and $f_c\pm f_m$, but the lower sideband is inverted. In the rotating phasor picture
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_phasors}, right) the two sideband phasors now sum to a vector \emph{perpendicular}
to the carrier, so the resultant swings back and forth in angle while its length stays (nearly) constant.
Narrowband FM occupies $2W$, like AM, and~\eqref{eq:ch04:nbfm} is also how Armstrong generated FM
(Section~\ref{sec:ch04:fmgen}).

\subsection{The spectrum of FM: Bessel functions}

For larger deviations the approximation fails and FM becomes genuinely nonlinear: the spectrum of an FM
signal is not a shifted copy of the message spectrum. Something surprising happens instead. A single
audio tone produces not two sidebands but an infinite family of them:
\begin{equation}
s(t)=A_c\cos\big(2\pi f_c t+\beta\sin 2\pi f_m t\big)=A_c\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}J_n(\beta)\cos\big(2\pi(f_c+nf_m)t\big),
\label{eq:ch04:bessel}
\end{equation}
where $J_n$ is the Bessel function of the first kind of order $n$. The sidebands are spaced $f_m$ apart,
with amplitudes $J_n(\beta)$ (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_fm_bessel}).

\begin{deeper}[Where the Bessel functions come from]
The complex envelope $e^{j\beta\sin\omega_mt}$ is periodic with period $1/f_m$, so it has a Fourier series,
$e^{j\beta\sin\omega_mt}=\sum_n c_n e^{jn\omega_mt}$. Its coefficients
$c_n=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{j(\beta\sin x-nx)}dx$ are, by definition, $J_n(\beta)$. Multiply by
$e^{j\omega_ct}$ and take the real part to obtain~\eqref{eq:ch04:bessel}.
\end{deeper}

\mdcfig{ch04_fm_bessel}{Top: Bessel functions $J_0$ to $J_4$ versus modulation index. Bottom: line spectra
of tone-modulated FM for four indices, including the first carrier null at $\beta=2.405$.}

Several facts follow, each with practical consequences.
\begin{itemize}
\item The total power is constant, $\sum J_n^2(\beta)=1$: modulation does not add power, it redistributes
power from the carrier to the sidebands. (Contrast AM, where sidebands add to the carrier.)
\item The carrier amplitude $J_0(\beta)$ vanishes at $\beta=2.405, 5.520, 8.654,\dots$---the carrier can
disappear entirely while the signal is at full strength.
\item $J_{-n}(\beta)=(-1)^nJ_n(\beta)$: the odd-order lower sidebands are inverted, generalising the
narrowband case.
\item For small $\beta$, $J_0(\beta)\approx1$, $J_1(\beta)\approx\beta/2$, and higher orders are
negligible: \term{narrowband FM} is recovered, with bandwidth $2f_m$.
\item For $|n|>\beta+1$ the amplitudes fall off very quickly (faster than exponentially), which is the
basis of Carson's rule below.
\end{itemize}

\begin{inpractice}[Setting FM deviation with a vanishing carrier]
Engineers use the \emph{Bessel nulls} to calibrate deviation with nothing more than a signal generator and
a spectrum analyser: modulate with a tone of known frequency, raise the audio level until the carrier
disappears on the analyser, and the index is exactly 2.405. With a 31.19\,kHz tone the first null
corresponds to 75\,kHz deviation, the broadcast standard; with 1\,kHz, to 2.405\,kHz. It is one of the
few calibrations in radio that needs no calibrated instrument at all.
\end{inpractice}

For messages that are not tones the spectrum has no closed form, but there is a beautiful large-deviation
result, \term{Woodward's theorem} (1952): as the deviation ratio grows, the power spectrum of an FM signal
tends to the \emph{probability density} of the instantaneous frequency $f_c+k_fm(t)$. The intuition is
that with a slowly varying message the signal spends a fraction of its time near each frequency equal to
the probability that $k_fm(t)$ takes that value, and so deposits that fraction of its power there---like a
slide whistle whose average sound is spread over the notes it lingers on.

Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_fm_spectra} verifies both results by simulation: the FFT of a tone-modulated signal
lands exactly on the Bessel predictions, and a Gaussian message with 10\,kHz rms deviation produces a
Gaussian spectrum of 10\,kHz standard deviation, while with only 1\,kHz deviation (deviation ratio near
one) the spectrum is a narrow peak bearing no resemblance to the pdf. The theorem is why wideband FM's
occupied bandwidth is governed by the \emph{peak deviation} rather than the message bandwidth.

\mdcfig{ch04_fm_spectra}{Left: FFT of a simulated tone-modulated FM signal with $\beta=5$ (lines) against
$|J_n(5)|$ (circles); the shaded region is the Carson bandwidth. Right: Woodward's theorem. With a Gaussian
message of 1\,kHz bandwidth and 10\,kHz rms deviation, the FM spectrum (blue) matches the Gaussian density
of the instantaneous frequency (dashed); with 1\,kHz rms deviation (orange) it does not.}

\subsection{Carson's rule}

In theory FM bandwidth is infinite; in practice sideband amplitudes fall rapidly once $|n|>\beta+1$. The
rule of thumb that bears John Carson's name estimates the band containing about 98\% of the power:
\begin{equation}
B_T\approx 2(\Delta f+W)=2(\beta+1)W.
\label{eq:ch04:carson}
\end{equation}
In words: the signal swings $\pm\Delta f$, and the message adds a margin of $W$ on each side. The rule
interpolates sensibly between the two extremes: for $\beta\ll1$ it gives $2W$ (narrowband FM), and for
$\beta\gg1$ it gives $2\Delta f$ (Woodward: the signal sweeps over $\pm\Delta f$).
Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_carson} compares it with the exact 98\% bandwidth for a tone. For specifications where
adjacent-channel interference matters, a more conservative estimate $2(\Delta f+2W)$ is sometimes used, and
regulators define occupied bandwidth by measurement.

\mdcfig[0.62]{ch04_carson}{Carson's rule (dashed) against the exact bandwidth containing 98\% of the power
of tone-modulated FM (steps). The rule is accurate across the range of practical modulation indices.}

\begin{worked}[Carson bandwidths of real FM services]
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Mono broadcast FM}: $\Delta f=75$\,kHz, $W=15$\,kHz, so $D=5$ and $B_T=2(75+15)=180$\,kHz,
which fits the 200\,kHz channel raster of the Americas.
\item \emph{Stereo broadcast FM}: the baseband now extends to 53\,kHz (Section~\ref{sec:ch04:stereo}), so
Carson gives $2(75+53)=256$\,kHz, more than a channel. In practice the stereo signal's energy at 53\,kHz is
small and the peak deviation is shared among the components, so the measured occupied bandwidth is close
to 200--240\,kHz; regulators protect stations by not assigning adjacent channels in the same area.
Carson's rule is an estimate, not a law.
\item \emph{Land-mobile FM}: $\Delta f=5$\,kHz, $W=3$\,kHz gives 16\,kHz in a 25\,kHz channel;
\emph{narrowband} land-mobile with $\Delta f=2.5$\,kHz gives 11\,kHz in a 12.5\,kHz channel.
\item \emph{AMPS cellular} (Section~\ref{sec:ch04:amps}): $\Delta f=12$\,kHz, $W=3$\,kHz gives 30\,kHz,
exactly the AMPS channel spacing.
\item \emph{Analog satellite TV} (C-band, 1980s): video of $W=4.2$\,MHz with a peak deviation of roughly
10\,MHz gives $2(10+4.2)\approx28$\,MHz, which is why one TV channel filled one 36\,MHz transponder.
\end{itemize}
\end{worked}

\subsection{Generating FM}
\label{sec:ch04:fmgen}

\paragraph{Direct FM.} The obvious FM modulator is an oscillator whose frequency follows the message: a
\term{voltage-controlled oscillator} (VCO). In tube equipment, a \emph{reactance-tube modulator} was
connected across the oscillator's tank circuit; feedback through a small RC phase shifter made the tube's
plate current lead its plate voltage by $90^\circ$, so the tube looked like a capacitance whose value
depended on its grid bias, and the audio on the grid varied the oscillator frequency. In solid-state
equipment a \emph{varactor} diode, whose junction capacitance varies with reverse bias, does the same job
directly. For an LC oscillator, $f=1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$, so a small capacitance change $\delta C$ gives
$\delta f/f\approx-\frac12\delta C/C$: to deviate a 100\,MHz oscillator by 75\,kHz needs a capacitance
change of only 0.15\%.

The problem is stability: the same sensitivity makes the centre frequency drift with temperature and
supply voltage, far beyond the $\pm2$\,kHz that FM broadcast rules allow. The cure is to enclose the VCO in
a phase-locked loop referenced to a crystal, with a loop bandwidth below the lowest audio frequency, so the
loop holds the average frequency while the audio modulation passes through unhindered
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_pllfm}).

\paragraph{Indirect FM (the Armstrong method).} Armstrong, who could not get a sufficiently stable direct
modulator in the early 1930s, started instead from a crystal oscillator, whose frequency is extremely
stable but cannot be pulled far. He integrated the audio and generated \emph{narrowband} PM with a
balanced modulator, using~\eqref{eq:ch04:nbfm}: a DSB-SC signal in quadrature, added to the carrier. The
resulting deviation is tiny---to keep the distortion of the approximation small, $\beta$ must stay below
about 0.5 at the lowest audio frequency---so he then \emph{multiplied} the frequency. A frequency
multiplier (a class-C amplifier rich in harmonics, followed by a filter tuned to the $n$th harmonic) turns
$\cos\theta(t)$ into $\cos n\theta(t)$, multiplying both carrier and deviation by $n$. A mixer translates
the carrier frequency without changing the deviation, which gives the designer a second degree of
freedom. Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_armstrong} shows a representative design.

\begin{worked}[Designing an Armstrong transmitter]
We need a 90.0\,MHz carrier with about 75\,kHz deviation, and audio down to 50\,Hz. At 50\,Hz the
narrowband modulator's index must stay below about 0.5, so its deviation is at most $0.5\times50=25$\,Hz.
Starting from a 200\,kHz crystal with $\Delta f_1=25$\,Hz, the total multiplication needed is
$75\,000/25=3000$; we use $64\times48=3072$, giving 76.8\,kHz. Multiplying the carrier directly by 3072
would give $200\,\text{kHz}\times3072=614.4$\,MHz, far too high. So after the first multiplier
($\times64$: 12.8\,MHz, 1.6\,kHz) we mix with a 10.925\,MHz crystal oscillator, taking the difference
frequency, 1.875\,MHz, which still carries 1.6\,kHz of deviation. The second multiplier ($\times48$) gives
$1.875\times48=90.0$\,MHz and $1.6\times48=76.8$\,kHz. The price of the method is visible in the
arithmetic: twelve or so multiplier stages, each a tuned amplifier, and any phase noise on the crystal
oscillators is multiplied by 3072 too (by $20\log_{10}3072\approx70$\,dB), a lesson every
frequency-synthesiser designer relearns.
\end{worked}

Today most FM is generated digitally: an NCO in an FPGA or DSP accumulates $2\pi k_f m[n]/f_s$ each
sample, and an I/Q modulator or a direct digital synthesiser translates the result to RF. A
software-defined radio produces broadcast-quality stereo FM in a few lines of code.

\subsection{Demodulating FM}
\label{sec:ch04:fmdemod}

An FM demodulator must output a voltage proportional to instantaneous frequency---a frequency-to-voltage
converter. It should also ignore amplitude variations, which carry only noise and interference; classic FM
receivers therefore precede the detector with one or more \term{limiter} stages that clip the IF signal
hard, leaving a constant-amplitude square wave whose zero crossings carry the information. The historical
sequence of detectors is a tour of analog circuit ingenuity, and Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_discriminators}
compares their transfer characteristics, the famous \emph{S-curves}.

\paragraph{Slope detector.} Tune a resonant circuit so that the carrier sits on the skirt of its response.
Frequency variations become amplitude variations, which an envelope detector recovers. This is FM-to-AM
conversion by a frequency-dependent filter, and any filter with a sloping response will do it---including,
by accident, the IF filter of an AM receiver, which is how early listeners received FM on AM sets. The
slope of a single tuned circuit is not straight, so the slope detector is badly nonlinear, and it responds
fully to amplitude noise (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_slope_detector}).

\mdcfig[0.72]{ch04_slope_detector}{The slope detector. Park the carrier on the skirt of a tuned circuit and
the frequency swing of FM becomes an amplitude swing that an envelope detector can follow---but only as
straight as the skirt is straight.}

\paragraph{Foster--Seeley discriminator and ratio detector.} Two tuned circuits, one above and one below
the carrier, feed two envelope detectors whose outputs are subtracted. The result is zero at the centre
frequency and, for a well-chosen spacing, close to linear over the deviation range: the S-curve of
Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_discriminators}. Dudley Foster and Stuart Seeley of RCA (1937) achieved the same
response with a single tuned transformer: the voltages across the two halves of a centre-tapped secondary
are the vector sum and difference of the primary voltage and a secondary voltage that is $90^\circ$ out of
phase at resonance, and the phase shift varies with frequency on either side. The \term{ratio detector}
(Seeley and Avins, 1947) rearranged the diodes and added a large capacitor that holds the \emph{sum} of the
two rectified voltages constant, so that the output depends only on their \emph{ratio}: built-in limiting,
which saved a tube. Between them, these two circuits demodulated almost every FM broadcast and TV sound
signal from the 1940s to the 1970s.

\paragraph{Quadrature detector.} Multiply the limited IF signal by a copy passed through a network whose
phase shift is $90^\circ$ at the centre frequency and varies linearly with frequency around it. With a tuned
quadrature network of loaded quality factor $Q_L$ the phase is $\theta(f)\approx\arctan[2Q_L(f-f_0)/f_0]$,
and the product's average is proportional to $\sin\theta$, which is linear in $f-f_0$ near the centre. A
pure delay $\tau$ chosen so that $2\pi f_0\tau=\pi/2$ works too: the output is then
$\propto\sin(2\pi(f-f_0)\tau)$. Because the multiplier is an easily integrated Gilbert cell, the quadrature
detector became the FM detector of integrated FM IF chips from the 1970s onward, in broadcast receivers, TV
sound and two-way radios alike.

\mdcfig[0.75]{ch04_discriminators}{Discriminator characteristics (S-curves) for a 10.7\,MHz IF, each
normalised to unit slope at the centre. A single tuned circuit used as a slope detector is badly nonlinear;
a balanced pair of stagger-tuned circuits (the response of the Foster--Seeley and ratio detectors) and a
tuned quadrature detector are close to linear over the $\pm75$\,kHz broadcast deviation (shaded) and turn
over beyond it.}

\paragraph{PLL demodulator.} Lock a VCO to the incoming signal with a phase-locked loop. When the loop
tracks, the VCO's instantaneous frequency equals the input's, so the VCO's control voltage is proportional
to the instantaneous frequency: it \emph{is} the demodulated audio. For a first-order view, a loop with VCO
gain $K_v$ (Hz per volt) tracking an input of frequency $f_c+k_fm(t)$ settles at a control voltage
$v(t)=k_fm(t)/K_v$, within the loop bandwidth. The loop bandwidth must accommodate the message bandwidth and
the deviation; because the loop rejects noise outside its bandwidth, PLL demodulators extend the threshold
by a few decibels (Section~\ref{sec:ch04:threshold}). PLL theory is developed in Chapter~\ref{ch:sync}.

\paragraph{Digital.} With complex baseband samples $z[n]=I[n]+jQ[n]$, the phase difference between
successive samples is the instantaneous frequency:
\begin{equation}
\hat f[n]=\frac{f_s}{2\pi}\arg\big(z[n]\,z^*[n-1]\big).
\label{eq:ch04:digfm}
\end{equation}
Since $z[n]z^*[n-1]=|z[n]||z[n-1]|e^{j(\theta[n]-\theta[n-1])}$, the amplitude drops out completely---the
limiter is built in---and the angle is unambiguous as long as the phase changes by less than $\pi$ per
sample, that is $|f|<f_s/2$. This single line of code is the FM demodulator in GNU Radio's
\texttt{Quadrature Demod} block and in almost every SDR. A variant that avoids the arctangent computes
$(I\dot Q-Q\dot I)/(I^2+Q^2)$ with discrete derivatives; it is the digital twin of the quadrature detector.

\begin{history}[Armstrong, FM and the war with RCA]
Edwin Howard Armstrong (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_armstrong_photo}) is the central figure of this chapter. As a
Columbia undergraduate in 1912 he discovered \emph{regeneration}---positive feedback around a
triode---which turned the feeble audion into a sensitive amplifier and, pushed further, into an
oscillator. As a Signal Corps officer in Paris in 1918 he invented the superheterodyne. In 1922 he added the
super-regenerative receiver. Then he spent a decade on the problem every listener complained of, static.

The prevailing view, stated in Carson's influential 1922 paper, was that FM offered no bandwidth advantage
over AM and was inherently inferior; Carson was right about \emph{narrowband} FM. Armstrong's insight was to
go the other way: use a deviation much \emph{larger} than the audio bandwidth, and trade that bandwidth for
noise immunity. He received four FM patents on 26 December 1933, demonstrated the system to RCA engineers on
the Empire State Building, and presented it to the Institute of Radio Engineers in November 1935, with
dramatically quiet demonstrations; his paper appeared in 1936.

RCA, run by Armstrong's former friend David Sarnoff, was then investing in television and declined to back
FM. Armstrong built his own high-power station, W2XMN, at Alpine, New Jersey (1939;
Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_w2xmn}), and by 1941 there were dozens of FM stations and several hundred thousand
receivers on the original 42--50\,MHz band. In 1945 the FCC moved FM to 88--106\,MHz (later 108\,MHz),
making every existing receiver and transmitter obsolete; the decision also allocated the old spectrum to
television. When RCA and others built FM sets and FM television sound without licensing his patents,
Armstrong sued (1948). The litigation outlasted his patents and his savings, and in January 1954 he took his
own life. His widow, Marion, pursued the cases and won or settled all of them by 1967. Every FM radio, and
the principle that bandwidth can buy signal-to-noise ratio, is his monument.
\end{history}

\section{Noise in analog systems}
\label{sec:ch04:noise}
%======================================================================

Anyone who listened to AM radio during a summer thunderstorm and then switched to FM heard the difference
at once: the crashes and crackles simply vanished. Armstrong's 1935 demonstrations reportedly astonished
his audience for the same reason. This section explains why, with numbers. It is the most important calculation of the
chapter, because it is the first time in this book that we will see bandwidth being traded for
signal-to-noise ratio.

\subsection{The figure of merit}

To compare modulations fairly we need a common yardstick. We use the framework of
Chapter~\ref{ch:noise}: white Gaussian noise of one-sided density $N_0$ added at the receiver input, a
received power $P_R$, and a message bandwidth $W$. The reference is \emph{baseband} transmission of the
message itself through an ideal low-pass filter of bandwidth $W$, whose output SNR is
\begin{equation}
\gamma=\frac{P_R}{N_0W}.
\label{eq:ch04:gamma}
\end{equation}
For each modulation we compute the output SNR after an ideal pre-detection filter of the modulation's
bandwidth and the appropriate demodulator, and express it as a multiple of $\gamma$. A modulation that
scores above $\gamma$ is beating baseband; one that scores below is wasting power. Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_snr_setup}
shows the set-up: think of it as a race in which every contestant gets the same received power and the same
noise.

\begin{deeper}[Output SNR of DSB-SC, SSB and AM]
Bandpass noise of bandwidth $B$ centred on $f_c$ is written $n(t)=n_c(t)\cos\omega_ct-n_s(t)\sin\omega_ct$,
where the in-phase and quadrature components are independent baseband Gaussian processes, each of power
$N_0B$ (Chapter~\ref{ch:noise}).

\emph{DSB-SC.} The received signal $A_cm(t)\cos\omega_ct$ has power $P_R=A_c^2P_m/2$. Coherent detection
(multiplying by $2\cos\omega_ct$ and low-pass filtering) gives $A_cm(t)+n_c(t)$, with signal power
$A_c^2P_m$ and noise power $N_0\cdot2W$. The ratio is $A_c^2P_m/(2N_0W)=P_R/(N_0W)=\gamma$.

\emph{SSB.} Signal power after detection is $P_R$-equivalent and the noise bandwidth is $W$; the output SNR
is again $\gamma$.

\emph{AM with envelope detection.} When the carrier is much larger than the noise, the envelope of
$A_c[1+\mu m(t)]\cos\omega_ct+n(t)$ is approximately $A_c[1+\mu m(t)]+n_c(t)$; the quadrature noise only
rotates the phasor slightly. The useful output is $A_c\mu m(t)$ and the noise is $n_c$, of power $2N_0W$.
With $P_R=\frac{A_c^2}{2}(1+\mu^2P_m)$ this gives~\eqref{eq:ch04:amsnr}.
\end{deeper}

The results are simple to state. \textbf{DSB-SC} is exactly as good as baseband: it doubles both the noise
bandwidth and the coherent signal amplitude, and these cancel. \textbf{SSB} also matches baseband, with half
the bandwidth of DSB. \textbf{AM with an envelope detector} pays for its carrier:
\begin{equation}
\text{SNR}_{\text{AM}}=\frac{A_c^2\mu^2P_m}{2N_0W}=\frac{\mu^2P_m}{1+\mu^2P_m}\,\gamma.
\label{eq:ch04:amsnr}
\end{equation}
AM pays the carrier's power out of the SNR, exactly the efficiency~\eqref{eq:ch04:ameff}: at best (tone,
$\mu=1$) it is 4.8\,dB worse than DSB-SC, and with real speech perhaps 10--15\,dB worse. When the noise is
comparable to the carrier the approximation fails: the envelope is then dominated by the noise, the message
appears only multiplied by noise, and the output SNR falls much faster than the input. This is the \emph{AM
threshold}, at roughly 10\,dB carrier-to-noise ratio; a coherent (synchronous) detector has no threshold.

\subsection{FM noise: the parabolic spectrum}
\label{sec:ch04:fmnoise}

FM is different in kind, and the reason is geometric. Picture the received signal as a long arrow (the FM
carrier) with a short, randomly jittering arrow (the noise) added to its tip. An FM demodulator only cares
about the \emph{angle} of the sum. Noise along the carrier changes the length---which the limiter throws
away. Only noise perpendicular to the carrier tilts the arrow, and it tilts it by an angle inversely
proportional to the carrier's length. A strong carrier is hard to tilt (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_noise_phasor}).

\mdcfig{ch04_noise_phasor}{FM noise geometry. The red cloud is the noise added to the tip of the carrier
phasor. Only its sideways component tilts the phasor, and the tilt shrinks as the carrier grows: with a
strong carrier (left) the phase barely moves; with a weak one (right) the same noise swings it wildly.}

There is a second twist. The demodulator outputs \emph{frequency}, the rate of change of that angle, and
differentiation emphasises fast wiggles. So the output noise is not flat: it rises with frequency, quiet in
the bass and loud in the treble. The result is the famous \term{parabolic noise spectrum} of FM,
\begin{equation}
S_{f_n}(f)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^2}(2\pi f)^2\frac{N_0}{A_c^2}=\frac{N_0f^2}{A_c^2},\qquad |f|<B_T/2.
\label{eq:ch04:parabolic}
\end{equation}
After the audio low-pass filter of bandwidth $W$ the output SNR is
\begin{equation}
\text{SNR}_{\text{FM}}=\frac{3A_c^2k_f^2P_m}{2N_0W^3}=3\Big(\frac{k_f}{W}\Big)^2P_m\,\gamma
=3D^2P_m\,\gamma,
\label{eq:ch04:fmsnr}
\end{equation}
where for a normalised message ($|m|\le1$) $k_f=\Delta f$ and $D=\Delta f/W$ is the deviation ratio. For a
tone, $P_m=\frac12$ and $D=\beta$, giving $\frac32\beta^2\gamma$.

Read~\eqref{eq:ch04:fmsnr} back in words: \emph{doubling the deviation quadruples the output SNR---6\,dB---at
the cost of roughly doubling the bandwidth.} Note also what did \emph{not} appear: the carrier amplitude
cancelled between signal and noise in such a way that only the received power and the deviation matter.

\begin{deeper}[Deriving the parabolic spectrum and the FM SNR]
Write the received signal as $A_c\cos(\omega_ct+\phi(t))+n(t)$ and assume the carrier-to-noise ratio is
high. Because the demodulator responds only to phase, resolve the noise into components parallel and
perpendicular to the signal phasor; only the perpendicular component $n_\perp(t)$ moves the phase, by
approximately $n_\perp(t)/A_c$ radians. For the statistics it does not matter that the signal phase is
moving (to a good approximation $n_\perp$ is statistically the same as $n_s$), so the phase noise is
\begin{equation}
\theta_n(t)\approx\frac{n_s(t)}{A_c},\qquad S_{\theta}(f)=\frac{N_0}{A_c^2},\quad|f|<B_T/2.
\end{equation}
The discriminator outputs instantaneous frequency, the derivative of phase divided by $2\pi$.
Differentiation multiplies a spectrum by $(2\pi f)^2$, which gives~\eqref{eq:ch04:parabolic}. After the
audio low-pass filter of bandwidth $W$, the output noise power is
\begin{equation}
N_o=\int_{-W}^{W}\frac{N_0f^2}{A_c^2}\,df=\frac{2N_0W^3}{3A_c^2}.
\end{equation}
The signal at the discriminator output is $k_fm(t)$, of power $k_f^2P_m$. With $P_R=A_c^2/2$ the ratio
is~\eqref{eq:ch04:fmsnr}.
\end{deeper}

Table~\ref{tab:ch04:snr} collects the results. DSB-SC and SSB tie with baseband: coherent linear modulation
neither gains nor loses. AM loses at least 4.8\,dB to the carrier. FM is different in kind: its output SNR
grows as $D^2$, so every doubling of deviation (and roughly of bandwidth) buys 6\,dB. This is the first
appearance in this book of a principle that information theory (Chapter~\ref{ch:infotheory}) makes exact:
\emph{bandwidth can be exchanged for signal-to-noise ratio}. FM's exchange is, however, a poor one by
Shannon's standard: the output SNR grows only quadratically with bandwidth, whereas an ideal system's SNR
grows \emph{exponentially} with the bandwidth expansion factor. Digital coding gets much closer to the ideal.
Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_snr_ladder} puts the numbers of this section side by side.

\mdcfig[0.82]{ch04_snr_ladder}{The noise scoreboard: output SNR relative to baseband for the same received

modulation breaks even; broadcast FM wins by almost 30\,dB---by spending twelve times the bandwidth.}

\begin{worked}[FM's advantage at 75\,kHz deviation]
Broadcast FM: $\Delta f=75$\,kHz, $W=15$\,kHz, $D=5$.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Tone test} ($P_m=\frac12$): $\text{SNR}=3\cdot25\cdot\frac12\gamma=37.5\gamma$, a gain of 15.7\,dB
over baseband and, since AM gives at best $\gamma/3$, of $37.5\times3=112.5$, or 20.5\,dB, over
100\%-modulated AM in the same noise.
\item \emph{De-emphasis} (next subsection) adds about 13.2\,dB for the North American 75\,$\mu$s standard,
for a total of about 29\,dB over baseband and 34\,dB over AM.
\item \emph{What received power does high fidelity need?} For an output SNR of 60\,dB, $\gamma$ must be
$60-15.7-13.2\approx31$\,dB. In $W=15$\,kHz with a receiver noise figure of 7\,dB,
$N_0W=-174+7+41.8=-125.2$\,dBm, so $P_R\approx-94$\,dBm---about 5\,$\mu$V across 75\,$\Omega$. This is close
to the ``50\,dB quieting'' sensitivity that receiver data sheets quote. Stereo needs roughly 20\,dB more
(Section~\ref{sec:ch04:stereo}).
\item \emph{Check the threshold.} The carrier-to-noise ratio in the 180\,kHz Carson band is
$\gamma W/B_T=31-10.8\approx20$\,dB, comfortably above the 10\,dB threshold.
\end{itemize}
\end{worked}

\subsection{Pre-emphasis and de-emphasis}
\label{sec:ch04:preemph}

The parabolic noise spectrum invites an obvious fix. Speech and music have most of their energy at low
frequencies and comparatively little above a few kilohertz---exactly where FM noise is worst. So boost the
high audio frequencies before modulation with a \term{pre-emphasis} filter $H_{\text{pe}}(f)=1+jf/f_1$, and
cut them by the same amount after demodulation with a \term{de-emphasis} filter
$H_{\text{de}}(f)=1/(1+jf/f_1)$. The message passes unchanged; the noise above $f_1$ is attenuated.

\mdcfig{ch04_emphasis_concept}{Pre-emphasis and de-emphasis in four frames. The transmitter boosts the
weak treble of the programme (2); the channel adds FM's rising noise (3); the receiver cuts the treble back
(4), restoring the programme and pulling the hiss down with it (dotted: the noise without de-emphasis).}

\begin{analogy}[Shout the treble so the hiss is quieter afterwards]
Imagine dictating a message down a hissy telephone line to a friend who writes it down. You know the hiss
mostly masks high-pitched sounds, so you deliberately \emph{shout} the s's and t's. Your friend, who knows
the trick, mentally turns the treble back down---and the hiss goes down with it, while your words come out
normal (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_emphasis_concept}). The limit: you can only shout so loud. If the programme is
already full of treble (cymbals, sibilants), shouting it louder would over-deviate the transmitter, and the
station must turn its audio down instead.
\end{analogy}

The standards specify the time constant $\tau=1/(2\pi f_1)$: 75\,$\mu$s ($f_1=2122$\,Hz) in the Americas and
South Korea, 50\,$\mu$s ($f_1=3183$\,Hz) in Europe and most of the rest of the world
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_preemphasis}). The circuit is a single RC network---a 75\,k$\Omega$ resistor and a 1\,nF
capacitor make a 75\,$\mu$s de-emphasis.

\mdcfig{ch04_preemphasis}{Left: the standard broadcast emphasis curves. Right: the parabolic FM output noise
spectrum and its shape after de-emphasis. Integrated over $W=15$\,kHz, de-emphasis reduces the noise by
13.2\,dB (75\,$\mu$s) or 10.2\,dB (50\,$\mu$s).}

The improvement is the ratio of output noise without and with de-emphasis:
\begin{equation}
I_{\text{de}}=\frac{\int_0^W f^2\,df}{\int_0^W\dfrac{f^2}{1+(f/f_1)^2}\,df}
=\frac{(W/f_1)^3}{3\big[W/f_1-\arctan(W/f_1)\big]}.
\label{eq:ch04:deemph}
\end{equation}
For $W=15$\,kHz and $\tau=75\,\mu$s, $W/f_1=7.07$, and $I_{\text{de}}=353/[3(7.07-1.43)]=20.9$, or 13.2\,dB;
for 50\,$\mu$s, 10.2\,dB. The improvement is not free: if the programme has strong high-frequency content
(cymbals, sibilants, modern heavily processed pop music), pre-emphasis increases the peak deviation and the
station must turn its audio down, or limit the treble. FM broadcast audio processors contain dedicated
high-frequency limiters for exactly this reason. Analog TV sound, FM satellite links and two-way radio use
the same idea (two-way radio typically with a 750\,$\mu$s characteristic over 300--3000\,Hz, a
$6$\,dB/octave rise that makes it effectively PM). Dolby noise reduction in cassette recorders was a cousin:
boost quiet high frequencies before the noisy medium, and cut them afterwards.

\subsection{The threshold effect}
\label{sec:ch04:threshold}

Drive away from an FM station and the sound stays clean, clean, clean---and then, over a few kilometres,
falls apart into crackles and pops. That cliff is the \term{threshold effect}. The FM advantage holds only
above a carrier-to-noise ratio of about 10\,dB in the IF bandwidth. Below it the high-CNR assumption
fails, and something dramatic happens.

\mdcfig{ch04_phasor_click}{Why FM clicks. The sum of a carrier (navy) and a rotating disturbance (red)
follows the circle drawn by the disturbance's tip. If the circle misses the origin the phase of the sum
only wobbles (green, right). If the disturbance is larger than the carrier the circle encloses the origin
and the phase slips by a whole cycle each time around (red, right): each slip is a click.}

Go back to the picture of a long carrier arrow with a noise arrow on its tip
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_phasor_click}). As long as the noise arrow is shorter than the carrier, the tip of the
sum can wander, but it can never swing around the origin, and the phase only wobbles. Occasionally,
though, the noise arrow becomes \emph{longer} than the carrier and sweeps around the origin, so the total
phase gains or loses a full $2\pi$ in a short time. The discriminator, which differentiates phase, turns
each of these encirclements into an impulse of area $\pm1$ cycle. In the loudspeaker each one is a sharp
\term{click}, and as the CNR falls further the clicks merge into a crackling roar that swamps the
programme.

Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_fm_clicks} shows the effect in a full simulation: at 16\,dB CNR the output is clean, at
4\,dB it is peppered with clicks, and the phase error of the received signal has wandered by whole cycles.

\mdcfig{ch04_fm_clicks}{FM clicks (simulation, $\beta=5$ tone, Carson bandwidth IF). Top: demodulated audio
at 16\,dB CNR follows the message (dashed). Middle: at 4\,dB CNR, impulses appear. Bottom: the phase error of
the received signal at 4\,dB, showing whole-cycle slips; each slip is one click.}

\mdcfig[0.5]{ch04_click_rate}{Rice's formula~\eqref{eq:ch04:rice} for a 180\,kHz IF. Below about 10\,dB the
click rate climbs by orders of magnitude in a few decibels: a wall, not a slope.}

S.~O.~Rice's analysis (1963) gives the average click rate for an unmodulated carrier with an ideal
rectangular IF filter of bandwidth $B$ and carrier-to-noise ratio $\rho$ as
\begin{equation}
\nu\approx\frac{B}{\sqrt{12}}\,\operatorname{erfc}\sqrt{\rho}\quad\text{clicks per second},
\label{eq:ch04:rice}
\end{equation}
and modulation increases it. For $B=180$\,kHz the rate is about 0.4 clicks per second at $\rho=10$\,dB, about
80 at 7\,dB, about 600 at 5\,dB and about 2400 at 3\,dB: a steep wall (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_click_rate}). Each
click carries energy spread across the audio band, so the output SNR collapses.
Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_fm_threshold} shows a Monte Carlo simulation of the whole curve: above threshold the
output SNR follows~\eqref{eq:ch04:fmsnr} and higher deviation wins; below it the curves bend sharply down,
and the wider-band signal crosses threshold first because it collects more noise. The threshold effect is
the analog ancestor of the ``cliff effect'' of digital systems: a good FM link sounds perfect until it
suddenly does not.

\mdcfig[0.8]{ch04_fm_threshold}{Output SNR versus carrier-to-noise ratio for FM with $\beta=2$ and $\beta=5$
(Monte Carlo with a tone), compared with AM and baseband. Above threshold FM beats AM by up to 20\,dB;
below about 10\,dB CNR the ``clicks'' take over.}

The threshold sets a practical limit on how much deviation is useful. In terms of $\gamma$, the CNR in the
Carson band is $\rho=\gamma W/B_T=\gamma/[2(D+1)]$, so a threshold at $\rho\approx10$ needs
$\gamma\ge20(D+1)$. Raising $D$ improves the above-threshold SNR as $D^2$ but raises the threshold as $D$.
For a given received power there is an optimum deviation: the largest that keeps the link above threshold
with margin. Designers of FM satellite and microwave links (in the 1960s--1980s, when both carried FM) chose
deviations precisely this way.

\emph{Threshold extension} lowers the knee by reducing the noise bandwidth the demodulator sees. The
\emph{FM feedback} (FMFB) demodulator, proposed by J.~G.~Chaffee in 1939, uses the demodulated output to
steer the receiver's local oscillator so that the IF signal's deviation is compressed and a narrower IF
filter can be used; the PLL demodulator achieves a similar effect. Gains of 3--7\,dB were obtained, and they
mattered enormously for early satellite TV and for the space programme, where telemetry and television from
distant spacecraft lived close to threshold.

\begin{tryit}[Lab 14: find the cliff]
In the \emph{FM threshold} experiment of \lab{lab14\_analog\_am\_fm.py}, start at a CNR of 20\,dB and lower it
1\,dB at a time while watching the demodulated waveform and the output-SNR readout. Around 10\,dB the first
isolated spikes appear; by 5\,dB the output is mostly clicks. Then repeat with $\beta=2$ instead of 5: the
clean-region SNR is lower, but the cliff arrives later. Press \emph{Listen} to hear the clicks for yourself.
\end{tryit}

\subsection{The capture effect}
\label{sec:ch04:capture}

When two FM signals arrive on the same channel, the demodulator output is dominated by the stronger one,
and the weaker is suppressed almost completely once the ratio exceeds a few decibels.

\begin{analogy}[The louder voice in a crowded room]
At a noisy party, if two people talk to you at once and one is noticeably louder, you hear the louder one
and simply stop hearing the other---your attention ``locks'' on. An FM receiver behaves the same way, only
far more decisively: give one signal a couple of decibels of advantage and the other all but disappears.
The limit: unlike your brain, the receiver cannot choose \emph{which} voice to follow. It always follows the stronger one, even if the weaker one is the station you wanted.
\end{analogy}

\mdcfig[0.72]{ch04_capture}{The capture effect (simulation). Two co-channel signals carry different tones
with a 500\,Hz carrier offset; the horizontal axis is the power ratio of the wanted to the unwanted carrier
and the vertical axis the output signal-to-interference ratio. FM switches sharply from one programme to the
other at 0\,dB; AM changes only gradually, and its envelope detector adds the 500\,Hz heterodyne whistle.}

The reason is the same phasor geometry as the threshold. The phase of the sum of two phasors follows the
larger one, with only a bounded wobble from the smaller: this is the left-hand picture of
Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_phasor_click}, with the interferer playing the part of the noise. If the weaker signal
has relative amplitude $r<1$, the resultant phase deviates from the stronger signal's phase by at most
$\arcsin r$, and the net phase never slips; the weaker signal's \emph{frequency modulation} appears in the
output only through this wobble, and is suppressed further by the audio filter.
Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_capture} shows a simulation. As the input ratio passes through 0\,dB, the FM output
switches abruptly from one programme to the other, by more than 70\,dB; the AM output changes only
gradually, in proportion to the input.

A receiver's \term{capture ratio}---the input ratio needed to suppress the weaker signal by a specified
amount (30\,dB in the classic IHF tuner measurement standard)---is about 1--2\,dB for a good broadcast tuner
and depends on the linearity of the limiter and the flatness of the IF filter's phase response. Capture is
why FM made VHF mobile radio practical: co-channel stations could be reused much closer together, and the
cellular systems of Chapter~\ref{ch:multipleaccess} began with FM for this reason
(Section~\ref{sec:ch04:amps}).

\begin{inpractice}[Why pilots still talk in AM]
Capture explains a decision that puzzles many newcomers to radio: aircraft still use AM on VHF. If two
pilots transmit at once, AM produces an audible heterodyne and garbled speech that tells the controller
something is wrong, whereas FM would silently deliver the stronger and hide the weaker transmission. In a
system where a missed call can be fatal, ``everyone can hear the collision'' is a feature, not a bug.
\end{inpractice}

\subsection{FM stereo multiplex}
\label{sec:ch04:stereo}

FM stereo, standardised in the USA in 1961 (the Zenith--General Electric pilot-tone system, which most of
the world then adopted), faced a brutal constraint: it had to remain compatible with millions of mono
receivers already in people's homes. A mono listener must hear both channels, undisturbed; a stereo
listener must hear them separated. The solution (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_stereo_mpx}) builds a
\term{multiplex} (MPX) baseband that frequency-modulates the carrier:
\begin{equation}
m_{\text{MPX}}(t)=a\,\big[L(t)+R(t)\big]+a\,\big[L(t)-R(t)\big]\cos(2\omega_pt)+b\cos(\omega_pt),
\label{eq:ch04:mpx}
\end{equation}
with $\omega_p=2\pi\times19$\,kHz, $a\approx0.45$ and $b\approx0.09$. It contains
\begin{itemize}
\item the sum signal $L+R$ from 30\,Hz to 15\,kHz, which a mono receiver plays as usual;
\item a 19\,kHz \term{pilot} tone ($\pm2$\,Hz) at 8 to 10\% of the peak deviation;
\item the difference $L-R$ as DSB-SC on a suppressed 38\,kHz subcarrier, occupying 23 to 53\,kHz,
phase-locked to the pilot so that both cross zero together.
\end{itemize}

\mdcfig[0.88]{ch04_stereo_mpx}{The FM stereo multiplex baseband: mono-compatible $L+R$, the 19\,kHz pilot,
the $L-R$ DSB-SC signal around a suppressed 38\,kHz subcarrier, and RDS at 57\,kHz.}

A clever detail limits the deviation. When $L$ and $R$ are equal, $L-R=0$; when one channel is silent, the
sum and difference have equal amplitudes, and their peaks interleave in time: where the 38\,kHz subcarrier
is $+1$, the MPX equals $2aL$; where it is $-1$, $2aR$ (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_mpx_time}). The total peak is
therefore no larger than for mono, and the $L+R$ and $L-R$ components can each be given 45\% of the
deviation without overmodulating.

\mdcfig[0.88]{ch04_mpx_time}{The stereo multiplex in time, with sound in the left channel only. The tops of
the 38\,kHz bursts trace $2aL$ and the bottoms trace $2aR$, so the MPX peak never exceeds the mono peak: the
designers got stereo without giving up any loudness.}

The stereo decoder (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_stereo_codec}b) doubles the pilot to regenerate the 38\,kHz carrier
coherently---an early, and very successful, example of \emph{pilot-aided carrier recovery}, the technique
that synchronises OFDM in Chapter~\ref{ch:ofdm}---recovers $L-R$, and forms $L=\frac12[(L+R)+(L-R)]$ and
$R=\frac12[(L+R)-(L-R)]$. Many integrated decoders use an equivalent \emph{switching} decoder that samples
the MPX alternately at the peaks and troughs of the 38\,kHz carrier, which by the interleaving argument
above deliver $L$ and $R$ directly. The receivers that did this became the centrepiece of the 1970s living
room (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_sx450}).

\mdcphoto[0.85]{ch04_sx450}{The illuminated dial of a Pioneer SX-450 AM/FM stereo receiver of the late
1970s. One pointer serves two superheterodynes: FM in megahertz on the upper scale, AM in tens of kilohertz
below, with a tuning meter on the left. Inside, a pilot-locked decoder of the kind in
Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_stereo_codec} separates left from right.}

Channel \emph{separation} depends on how accurately the decoder reconstructs $L-R$. If the regenerated
38\,kHz carrier has phase error $\theta$, the recovered difference signal is scaled by $\cos\theta$, and the
leakage of one channel into the other gives a separation of
\begin{equation}
\text{separation}=20\log_{10}\frac{1+\cos\theta}{1-\cos\theta}\ \text{dB},
\end{equation}
which is 42\,dB for $\theta=10^\circ$ and 63\,dB for $3^\circ$. A gain error $g$ in the $L-R$ path has the
same effect, with $\cos\theta$ replaced by $g$: a 1\% gain error limits separation to 46\,dB. Broadcast
specifications ask for 30--40\,dB, and the ear needs only 20--25\,dB to perceive a full stereo image.

The price of stereo is noise. Because the $L-R$ channel sits high in the MPX spectrum, where FM noise is
parabolic, the decoded stereo signal is about 20\,dB noisier than mono at the same carrier-to-noise ratio
(Problem~\ref{prob:ch04:stereonoise}). Every car radio therefore blends
progressively to mono as the signal weakens; a station that sounds clean in mono can hiss in stereo. Home
receivers simply gave the listener a button for it (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_hk630}).

FM STEREO, FM (mono) or AM; the MONO button trades stereo for roughly 20\,dB less hiss on a weak station; FM
MUTING silences the roar of noise between stations.}

\subsection{RDS and subsidiary carriers}

Above the stereo band, the \term{Radio Data System} (RDS; RBDS in North America, standardised as IEC 62106
and NRSC-4) carries station names, programme type, alternative frequencies, clock time and traffic
information---the text that scrolls across your car radio's display. Its subcarrier is at 57\,kHz, the third
harmonic of the pilot, so the decoder can lock to it coherently, and its bit rate is $57\,000/48=1187.5$\,b/s.
The data are differentially encoded and biphase (Manchester) coded before BPSK modulation, which removes
energy at the subcarrier frequency so the signal does not disturb receivers' pilot loops, and confines it to
about $\pm2.4$\,kHz (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_rds_spectrum}). Data travel in groups of four 26-bit blocks, each with 16 information bits and a 10-bit
check word from a shortened cyclic code that supports both error detection and burst correction and also
provides block synchronisation (Chapter~\ref{ch:classiccodes}). It is a complete digital modem riding inside
an analog broadcast---and a useful lab project, since a software-defined radio can decode it in real time.

\mdcfig[0.72]{ch04_rds_spectrum}{The spectrum of the RDS subcarrier (simulated). Biphase coding puts a deep
null exactly at 57\,kHz and keeps the signal within about $\pm2.4$\,kHz, tucked between the stereo
sidebands and the subsidiary carriers.}

Before RDS, US stations leased \emph{Subsidiary Communications Authorization} (SCA) subcarriers at 67 and
92\,kHz, typically narrowband FM, for background music services, radio reading services for the blind and
paging. In Europe, the German ARI system (1974) put a 57\,kHz unmodulated carrier on the air to flag traffic
announcements, the ancestor of RDS's TA/TP flags.

\subsection{AM stereo, HD Radio and DAB}
\label{sec:ch04:digitalradio}

\emph{AM stereo} is a cautionary tale. In 1982 the FCC declined to choose among competing systems and left the
choice to the ``marketplace''; receiver makers, faced with incompatible standards, largely ignored all of
them. Motorola's \term{C-QUAM} (compatible QAM) eventually prevailed and was adopted as the US standard in
1993, by which time AM listeners had moved to FM for music. C-QUAM is elegant: it starts from analog QAM,
$(1+L+R)\cos\omega_ct+(L-R)\sin\omega_ct$, whose envelope would distort a mono envelope detector, and then
\emph{replaces} the envelope by $1+L+R$, leaving only the phase to carry $L-R$. A mono receiver hears $L+R$
undistorted; a stereo receiver reconstructs the QAM signal and demodulates coherently, identifying stereo by
a 25\,Hz pilot tone. It is an early example of designing a modulation around a legacy receiver.

Digital radio arrived in two philosophies. Europe's \term{DAB} (Eureka-147, ETSI EN 300 401; on air from
1995) and its successor DAB+ (HE-AAC audio coding, Chapter~\ref{ch:sourcecoding}) use OFDM in a 1.5\,MHz
ensemble, usually in VHF Band III (174--240\,MHz), multiplexing a dozen or more services and operating in
\emph{single-frequency networks} where every transmitter sends the same signal on the same
frequency---possible only because OFDM's guard interval absorbs the echoes (Chapter~\ref{ch:ofdm}). The US
\term{HD Radio} system (NRSC-5, approved by the FCC in 2002) instead places low-power OFDM sidebands on either
side of existing AM and FM analog carriers (``in-band on-channel'', IBOC), allowing a gradual transition
without new spectrum; a hybrid FM station carries roughly 100--150\,kb/s of digital audio and data alongside
its analog signal. \emph{Digital Radio Mondiale} (DRM, ETSI ES 201 980) brings OFDM to the long, medium and
short-wave bands in the same 9 or 10\,kHz channels as AM. Some countries have begun switching national FM
networks off in favour of DAB+: Norway in 2017, and Switzerland's public broadcaster at the end of 2024.

\subsection{Detectors and the tuned radio-frequency receiver}

The first receivers detected the bursts of spark transmitters with the \emph{coherer}, a glass tube of metal
filings whose resistance dropped when RF arrived and which had to be tapped to reset---a one-bit threshold
detector, and a temperamental one. Magnetic and electrolytic detectors, the crystal of 1906, John Ambrose
Fleming's thermionic diode of 1904 and Lee de Forest's three-electrode \emph{audion} of 1906 followed. The
audion, little understood by its inventor, became a true amplifier once better vacuums were achieved around
1912--1913, and the race was on to build sensitive, selective receivers.

The natural architecture was the \term{tuned radio-frequency} (TRF) receiver (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_trf}): several stages of RF
amplification, each with its own tuned circuit, followed by a detector. A 1920s TRF set might have three
tuned stages, each tuned by a separate dial that the listener set to the same logged numbers, until
manufacturers learned to gang the capacitors on one shaft. TRF receivers suffered from two problems. Every
stage had to be \emph{tracked} across the whole band, and the triodes' grid-to-plate capacitance fed energy
back and made the stages oscillate unless \emph{neutralised} (Hazeltine's \emph{Neutrodyne}, 1923). More
fundamentally, a tuned circuit's bandwidth is $f/Q$: with $Q=100$ a stage is 5.5\,kHz wide at 550\,kHz but
15\,kHz wide at 1500\,kHz, so selectivity varied across the dial and was poorest exactly where the stations
were most crowded. Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_selectivity} compares the selectivity of a crystal set, a TRF and the
superheterodyne that replaced them.

the two fixed 455\,kHz IF transformers of a superheterodyne give a flat top over the wanted channel and steep
skirts beyond it.}

\subsection{Regeneration and super-regeneration}

Armstrong's 1912 \term{regenerative receiver} fed a fraction of the amplified signal back in phase to the
input tuned circuit. Positive feedback with loop gain $G\beta<1$ multiplies the gain by $1/(1-G\beta)$ and,
equivalently, cancels part of the circuit's loss so its effective $Q$ rises in the same proportion. With the
feedback (``tickler'' coil or ``regeneration'' control) advanced to just below oscillation, a single triode
gave the gain and selectivity of several stages. Advanced past the edge, the circuit oscillated, and the beat
between the oscillation and an incoming CW telegraph signal produced an audible tone: the regenerative
receiver was also the first practical heterodyne receiver for Morse. Its drawbacks were twofold: it was
touchy, demanding a skilled hand on the regeneration control, and an oscillating regenerative receiver
radiated through its antenna, filling the band with whistles that neighbours' sets heard.

Armstrong's 1922 \term{super-regenerative} receiver turned this instability into a feature. A \emph{quench}
oscillator, typically at tens to hundreds of kilohertz, repeatedly pushes the stage into oscillation and then
kills it. Each time the oscillation starts it grows exponentially from whatever signal is present in the
tuned circuit, so the time it takes to reach a given amplitude (and hence the average output over a quench
cycle) depends on the input signal level. A single active device then provides gains of a million or more.
Super-regenerative receivers are broad, noisy and radiate, but they are astonishingly cheap: until recently
many garage-door openers, wireless doorbells, toy radio controls and remote key fobs at 27, 49, 315 and
433\,MHz used one transistor in this circuit.

\begin{inpractice}[title=Regeneration in court]
Lee de Forest claimed to have discovered regeneration in 1912 too, and the dispute between him and Armstrong
became one of the longest patent fights in American history, running from 1914 through the 1920s and reaching
the Supreme Court, which ruled for de Forest in 1934 on largely procedural grounds. Most engineers thought the
court wrong. When Armstrong attempted to return the Medal of Honor the Institute of Radio Engineers had
awarded him in 1917, largely for regeneration, the IRE's board refused to take it back and reaffirmed the
award. Patent battles over radio fundamentals---regeneration, the superheterodyne, FM---shaped the industry,
encouraged the formation of RCA as a patent pool in 1919, and foreshadowed the standard-essential-patent
disputes of the cellular industry (Chapter~\ref{ch:cellular}).
\end{inpractice}

\section{The superheterodyne receiver}
\label{sec:ch04:superhet}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Armstrong's architecture}

In 1918, as a Signal Corps officer in Paris, Armstrong was wrestling with a military problem: how to
receive weak, high-frequency signals that the tubes of the day could barely amplify. His answer was so
good that, a century later, almost every radio still uses it. The \term{superheterodyne} mixes every
incoming station down to a single fixed \term{intermediate frequency} (IF), where all the selectivity and
most of the gain can be designed once and for all.

\begin{analogy}[Translate everything into one language before listening]
Imagine a conference interpreter who must follow speakers in fifty languages. Learning to understand all
fifty perfectly is hopeless. Instead, a translator in front of her renders each speaker into English, and
she becomes a world expert at listening to English. The superheterodyne's mixer is the translator: whatever
station you tune, it is shifted to the same IF, 455\,kHz in an AM radio (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_superhet_concept}),
and the IF filter and amplifiers become experts at that one frequency. The limit of the analogy: this
translator is not perfect. As we will see, it happily translates a second, unwanted station into the same
words---the image.
\end{analogy}

\mdcfig{ch04_superhet_concept}{The superheterodyne principle. To listen to station A, B or C, the receiver
tunes its local oscillator 455\,kHz above that station, so the station always lands in the same fixed IF
filter, which never needs to be retuned.}

The IF filter does not need to tune, so it can be made with many coupled resonators, or later with ceramic,
crystal or SAW elements, and its bandwidth is the same for every station---curing the TRF's worst defect.
Only the input preselector and the local oscillator need to tune, and they need not be sharp. Nearly every
radio receiver built from 1930 to 2000 was a superheterodyne, and the architecture lives on in spectrum
analysers, satellite receivers and the IF sections of base stations.

A mixer multiplies the input with a local oscillator (LO). An input at $f_{RF}$ and an LO at $f_{LO}$ produce
$|f_{RF}\pm f_{LO}|$; the IF filter selects $f_{IF}=|f_{RF}-f_{LO}|$. With \term{high-side injection},
$f_{LO}=f_{RF}+f_{IF}$; with \term{low-side injection}, $f_{LO}=f_{RF}-f_{IF}$. Either way, tuning the receiver
means tuning the LO.

\begin{pitfall}[High-side injection flips the spectrum]
With high-side injection the upper sideband of the RF signal becomes the \emph{lower} sideband at the IF: the
spectrum is mirrored. For AM nobody notices. For SSB it turns USB into LSB, and for digital signals it
conjugates the complex baseband, rotating constellations the wrong way. Software-radio programmers rediscover
this every year; count the high-side conversions in your chain and conjugate if the count is odd.
\end{pitfall}

\subsection{The image problem}
\label{sec:ch04:image}

The mixer has a blind spot: it cannot tell whether the input is above or below the LO. An input at
$f_{\text{image}}=f_{LO}+f_{IF}$ for high-side injection, two IFs away from the wanted signal, produces
exactly the same IF.

\begin{analogy}[A mirror-image station sneaking in]
Think of the LO as a mirror standing on the frequency axis. The wanted station stands 455\,kHz in front of
it; anything standing 455\,kHz \emph{behind} the mirror looks, to the mixer, exactly the same distance away
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_image_mirror}). Once both have passed the mixer they are superimposed on the same IF and
nothing can separate them. The only defence is to stop the image \emph{before} the mixer. The analogy is
exact rather than loose: the image frequency is literally the reflection of the wanted frequency about the
LO.
\end{analogy}

\mdcfig{ch04_image_mirror}{The image, before and after the mixer. A receiver tuned to 1000\,kHz with its LO at
1455\,kHz also hears 1910\,kHz, the mirror image of the wanted station about the LO. After mixing both sit at
455\,kHz, on top of each other.}

Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_superhet} shows the same problem in an FM receiver tuned to 98.1\,MHz with a 10.7\,MHz
IF: the image is at 119.5\,MHz, which falls in the aeronautical band. A tuned \term{preselector} ahead of the
mixer must attenuate the image before mixing. This sets the first rule of IF selection: \emph{the IF must be
high enough that the preselector can reject the image}. The second rule is its opposite: \emph{the IF must be
low enough that the IF filter can provide adjacent-channel selectivity}. At 455\,kHz, a few tuned transformers
or a ceramic filter easily make a 10\,kHz AM channel; at 10.7\,MHz, a ceramic filter makes a 200\,kHz FM
channel.

\mdcfig{ch04_superhet}{Frequency plan of an FM superheterodyne with a 10.7\,MHz IF and high-side LO. The
preselector must suppress the image two IFs above the wanted station before the mixer.}

A single tuned circuit of loaded quality factor $Q$, resonant at the wanted frequency $f_s$, attenuates a
signal at $f_i$ by
\begin{equation}
\text{IRR}=\sqrt{1+Q^2\rho^2},\qquad \rho=\frac{f_i}{f_s}-\frac{f_s}{f_i},
\label{eq:ch04:irr}
\end{equation}
and $n$ identical, isolated tuned circuits give the $n$th power. Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_image_rejection}
plots~\eqref{eq:ch04:irr} across the two broadcast bands. Image rejection is easy on medium waves, where the
image is far away in relative terms ($f_i/f_s$ is 1.5--2.7), and hard at VHF, where it is only about 20\% away.

\mdcfig{ch04_image_rejection}{Image rejection of tuned preselectors computed from~\eqref{eq:ch04:irr}. Left: a
455\,kHz IF puts the medium-wave image 910\,kHz away, and a single tuned circuit gives 30--40\,dB; the older
262\,kHz IF does worse. Right: at VHF the 10.7\,MHz IF image is only 21.4\,MHz away, and two tuned circuits are
needed for 40\,dB.}

\begin{worked}[The image rejection a superhet needs]
\emph{Medium wave.} A receiver tuned to 1000\,kHz with a 455\,kHz IF and high-side LO (1455\,kHz) has its image
at 1910\,kHz. Then $\rho=1.910-0.524=1.386$. A loop-antenna preselector with loaded $Q=40$ gives
$\text{IRR}=\sqrt{1+55.4^2}=55$, or 35\,dB. Image signals on medium waves are other broadcast stations
(1910\,kHz is above the band, so in fact there are none for this setting; but tuned to 600\,kHz the image is at
1510\,kHz, squarely in the band). If a strong local station at the image frequency is 30\,dB stronger than a
weak wanted station that needs 20\,dB protection, the requirement is 50\,dB, which needs a second tuned
circuit---an RF amplifier stage, which better radios had and the cheapest did not.

\emph{VHF FM.} Tuned to 98.1\,MHz, the image is at 119.5\,MHz: $\rho=1.218-0.821=0.397$. With $Q=30$, one tuned
circuit gives $\sqrt{1+11.9^2}=11.9$, or 21.5\,dB. If an aeronautical transmitter at 119.5\,MHz arrives 40\,dB
stronger than the wanted station, and we want it at least 20\,dB below the wanted station at the IF so that
capture holds firmly, we need 60\,dB: three tuned circuits, or a higher IF. This is why good FM tuners had
three- and four-gang tuning capacitors, and why many modern designs use a much higher first IF.
\end{worked}

\subsection{Choosing the IF, and tracking the oscillator}

Standard IFs reflect these compromises and the history of filter technology: 175 and 262\,kHz in early sets;
455\,kHz for AM broadcast in North America and Japan (450--470\,kHz in Europe); 10.7\,MHz for FM broadcast,
because it is above half the 20\,MHz width of the FM band (so no FM station can be another's image) and below
the band itself; about 45\,MHz for analog television (picture IF 45.75\,MHz in the US); 70 and 140\,MHz for
satellite and microwave links; and 950--2150\,MHz as the first IF of satellite TV, generated by the LNB on the
dish (Chapter~\ref{ch:satellite}). A further practical rule is that the IF must not lie inside a band the
receiver tunes, or a strong station at the IF leaks straight through; this is why the AM band's lower edge,
530\,kHz, sits a comfortable distance above 455\,kHz.

The choice between high-side and low-side injection is made by the oscillator's tuning range. For the
medium-wave band 530--1700\,kHz (a ratio of 3.21, so the preselector capacitor must vary by $3.21^2=10.3$:1),
a high-side LO tunes 985--2155\,kHz, a ratio of 2.19 needing only a 4.8:1 capacitance change; a low-side LO
would need 75--1245\,kHz, a ratio of 16.6 and a capacitance change of 276:1, which is impossible with a ganged
capacitor. So broadcast receivers use high-side injection.

The two sections of the ganged capacitor must then keep the LO exactly 455\,kHz above the RF across the
band---\term{tracking}. Designers achieved it with a smaller-capacity oscillator section (``cut plates'') or
with a series \emph{padder} capacitor and a parallel \emph{trimmer}, which give exact tracking at three points
in the band (\emph{three-point tracking}) and small errors in between (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_tracking}). Every
radio service manual began its alignment procedure with these adjustments.

\mdcfig[0.8]{ch04_tracking}{Tracking a ganged-capacitor superhet, computed for a 10--365\,pF gang and a
229\,$\mu$H preselector coil. With only a trimmer the LO can be right at two points and is tens of kilohertz
off elsewhere; adding a series padder (about 380\,pF here) makes it exact at three points (dots) and within a
few kilohertz across the band---small compared with the preselector's bandwidth.}

\subsection{Double conversion and the path to zero IF}

When one IF cannot satisfy both rules, use two. \term{Double-conversion} communications receivers convert first
to a high IF and then to a low IF where the channel filtering is done. A modern HF receiver covering 0--30\,MHz
typically \emph{up-converts} to a first IF of 45 or 70\,MHz, above the entire tuning range; the image is then
90--140\,MHz away, and a fixed 30\,MHz low-pass filter rejects it completely without any tracking. A crystal
\emph{roofing filter} at the first IF limits the bandwidth seen by later stages, and a second conversion to
455\,kHz or 10.7\,MHz, or to an ADC, does the fine selection (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_dblconv}). Cellular
handsets of the 1990s followed the same pattern.

\subsection{Automatic gain control}
\label{sec:ch04:agc}

Received signal strengths span more than 100\,dB, from a station across town to one at the edge of coverage,
and fading adds rapid swings of tens of decibels. \term{Automatic gain control} (AGC; in broadcast receivers,
\emph{automatic volume control}, AVC) measures the signal level after the IF and adjusts the gain of the RF
and IF stages to keep the detector input constant. Harold Wheeler of Hazeltine introduced it in the
mid-1920s, and it transformed radio listening: before AVC a fading station had to be chased with the volume
control.

\begin{analogy}[AGC is the receiver's iris]
Walk from a sunny street into a dim room and, within a second or so, you can see again: the iris of your eye
opens to let in more light. Walk back out and it snaps shut. An AGC loop does the same for a radio, holding
the level at the detector nearly constant while the antenna signal varies over a range of millions to one
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_agc_static}). Like the iris, it is deliberately not instantaneous, and like the iris it
closes faster than it opens. The limit: your iris does not care what you are looking at, but an AGC that
reacts too fast will try to ``correct'' the loud and soft passages of the programme itself.
\end{analogy}

\mdcfig[0.6]{ch04_agc_static}{The static characteristic of a delayed AGC. Below the knee the receiver runs at
full gain for the best noise figure; above it the loop holds the detector level within a few decibels while
the antenna signal rises by 85\,dB. Without AGC the detector would overload.}

In an AM receiver the detector already provides the measurement: the DC component of the envelope-detector
output is proportional to the carrier amplitude. Filtered by a time constant of a tenth of a second or so, it
becomes a negative bias applied to the grids of the RF and IF tubes, whose \emph{remote-cutoff} (variable-mu)
design makes their gain depend smoothly on bias. The loop then holds the carrier at the detector roughly
constant. Two refinements are universal. \emph{Delayed} AGC applies no gain reduction to the RF amplifier
until the signal is well above the noise floor, so weak signals keep the full RF gain and the best noise
figure. And the time constants encode a compromise: fast enough to follow fading, slow enough not to flatten
the modulation itself.

Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_agc} simulates a feedback AGC with level steps of $+30$, $-40$ and $+15$\,dB. With a
detector that averages over many audio cycles and an asymmetric loop---\emph{fast attack} so a sudden strong
signal does not overload the detector, \emph{slow decay} so the gain does not pump up in speech pauses---the
output level is restored within tenths of a second while the modulation survives. A loop that is too fast
removes the modulation along with the fading.

\mdcfig{ch04_agc}{AGC on an AM envelope (simulation). Top: input envelope with steps of $+30$, $-40$ and
$+15$\,dB and slow fading (log scale; a 40\,Hz tone stands in for the audio so it is visible). Middle: a
well-designed loop restores the output level after each step while preserving the modulation; note the brief
overshoot after the upward step (attack) and the slower recovery after the downward step (decay). Bottom: a
loop with millisecond time constants follows the envelope too closely and flattens the modulation.}

SSB receivers need a different AGC, because there is no carrier to measure: the loop must be derived from the
speech peaks themselves, with fast attack and a slow ``hang'' decay that holds the gain constant through
syllables. Digital receivers implement AGC in firmware, and it is the first loop to converge in every receive
chain: in an SDR its job is to keep the signal inside the ADC's range without clipping (Chapter~\ref{ch:sdr}),
and in an LTE or Wi-Fi receiver it must settle within the first few microseconds of a packet's preamble.

hear the background noise ``breathe'' up between words; make both very fast and watch the loop eat the
modulation.
\end{tryit}

\subsection{Sensitivity, selectivity and dynamic range}
\label{sec:ch04:dynrange}

A receiver is judged by three questions: how weak a signal can it hear, how well does it reject signals on
other frequencies, and how strong can those other signals be before they cause trouble? Its
\emph{sensitivity} is set by its noise figure $F$ (Chapter~\ref{ch:noise}): the noise floor referred to the
input, the \term{minimum discernible signal}, is
\begin{equation}
\text{MDS}=-174\,\text{dBm/Hz}+10\log_{10}B+\text{NF}\quad\text{(dBm)}.
\label{eq:ch04:mds}
\end{equation}
For an SSB receiver with $B=2.4$\,kHz and $\text{NF}=10$\,dB, $\text{MDS}=-130$\,dBm, about 0.07\,$\mu$V in
50\,$\Omega$. Below about 20\,MHz this is more than good enough, because atmospheric and man-made noise picked
up by the antenna exceeds the receiver's own noise by tens of decibels; the first stage of an HF receiver is
chosen for its linearity rather than its noise figure. At VHF and above the receiver's noise figure dominates,
and the Friis formula of Chapter~\ref{ch:noise} says it is set by the first stages.

The upper end is set by nonlinearity. Any amplifier or mixer has a transfer characteristic
$y=a_1x+a_2x^2+a_3x^3+\cdots$. Two strong signals at $f_1$ and $f_2$, neither of them on the wanted channel,
produce through the cubic term \term{third-order intermodulation} products at $2f_1-f_2$ and $2f_2-f_1$. These
lie close to the original pair and can fall right on the wanted channel: ghost signals manufactured inside
the receiver, which no filter after the culprit stage can remove. Their amplitude grows as the \emph{cube} of
the input, three decibels for every decibel of input, while the wanted output grows one-for-one, so on a
log--log plot the two lines meet at the \term{third-order intercept point} (IP3),
Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_ip3}. For $y=a_1x+a_3x^3$ the input intercept amplitude is
$A_{\text{IIP3}}=\sqrt{\tfrac43|a_1/a_3|}$. Referred to the input, the IM3 power at input level
$P_{\text{in}}$ (per tone) is
\begin{equation}
P_{\text{IM3}}=3P_{\text{in}}-2\,\text{IIP3}\quad\text{(dB quantities)}.
\label{eq:ch04:im3}
\end{equation}
The \term{spurious-free dynamic range} is the range of input levels between the noise floor and the level at
which IM3 products just reach it:
\begin{equation}
\text{SFDR}=\tfrac23\big(\text{IIP3}-\text{MDS}\big).
\label{eq:ch04:sfdr}
\end{equation}

\mdcfig[0.75]{ch04_ip3}{Two-tone test of a compressive cubic nonlinearity (simulation; markers) and the
straight-line asymptotes that define the third-order intercept. The IM3 products rise three decibels per
decibel of input; the spurious-free dynamic range is the input range over which they stay below the noise
floor.}

\begin{worked}[Dynamic range of an HF receiver]
A contest-grade HF receiver has $\text{NF}=12$\,dB and a 500\,Hz CW filter, so $\text{MDS}=-174+27+12=-135$\,dBm.
Its front end has $\text{IIP3}=+30$\,dBm. Then $\text{SFDR}=\frac23(30+135)=110$\,dB. Two signals at $-25$\,dBm
each (strong but common from nearby stations on a crowded band) create IM3 at $3(-25)-60=-135$\,dBm, just at
the noise floor. A cheap receiver with $\text{IIP3}=0$\,dBm would have $\text{SFDR}=90$\,dB, and the same two
signals would produce IM3 at $-75$\,dBm, an S9 signal that is not really there. Published receiver tests (for
example in the ARRL's product reviews) report exactly these numbers, along with \emph{blocking}
(desensitisation by a single strong signal) and \emph{reciprocal mixing}, in which a strong nearby signal
mixes with the LO's phase noise and spreads noise onto the wanted channel. Chapter~\ref{ch:sdr} extends these
ideas to direct-sampling receivers, where the ADC's own nonlinearity and clipping set the limits.
\end{worked}

Every detail rewards study. The \emph{loop antenna}, a flat spiral of wire on the back cover, is also the
preselector's inductor: one tuned circuit, giving the 30--35\,dB of image rejection computed above, which is
adequate on medium waves. The \emph{pentagrid converter} uses its first grids as the triode of a Hartley
oscillator and its signal grid to modulate the electron stream, so a single tube multiplies RF by LO; its
output, a mixture of sum and difference frequencies, goes to the first IF transformer. The two \emph{IF
transformers} are double-tuned, each a pair of coupled resonant circuits adjusted by slugs or trimmer
capacitors to give a flat-topped passband about 6--8\,kHz wide; together with one stage of gain they set the
radio's selectivity and much of its sensitivity. The \emph{12AV6} contains two diodes and a triode: one diode
is the envelope detector whose load is the volume control, the AVC voltage is taken from the same point
through a large RC filter, and the triode amplifies the audio. The 50C5 delivers a watt or two into a small
speaker. Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_philco_chassis} shows what this looks like in metal.

The heater string is the design's signature and its hazard. Wiring the five heaters in series makes their
voltages add to the line voltage, eliminating the expensive, heavy power transformer; the 35W4 rectifier
supplies about 120\,V of B+ directly from the line.

\begin{pitfall}[The hot chassis]
With no transformer, the AA5's chassis is connected to one side of the mains: depending on which way the
plug went in, the metal chassis could be at 120\,V. The knobs and cabinet were insulating for that reason, and
generations of repair technicians learned to use an isolation transformer. If you restore one of these
radios, do the same---and never connect a grounded oscilloscope probe to its chassis without one.
\end{pitfall}

The AA5 is the superheterodyne reduced to its essence---one conversion, one IF stage, one detector, one audio
stage---and every receiver since, including the RFIC in a phone, solves the same problems with the same
blocks.

\begin{history}[The All American Five and the transistor radio]
The transformerless five-tube superhet appeared in the early 1930s, spread rapidly with the octal tubes
introduced in 1935 (the original lineup was 12SA7, 12SK7, 12SQ7, 50L6, 35Z5---the board of
Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_aa5_demo}) and was redesigned around seven-pin miniature tubes after the Second World War.
Its replacement came from the transistor. The Regency TR-1, released in time for Christmas 1954, was the
first commercial transistor radio: four germanium transistors made by Texas Instruments in a superheterodyne
that, apart from the transistors and a 22.5\,V battery, followed the AA5 block diagram, with a 262\,kHz IF.
Sony's TR-55 followed in 1955. Within a decade the transistor portable had made radio personal and mobile, and
the AA5 retreated to the kitchen and then to collectors' shelves. Its block diagram did not retreat at all.
\end{history}

\section{Analog television}
\label{sec:ch04:tv}
%======================================================================

Analog television was the most complex consumer signal ever designed without a computer, and the most
ingenious. In a 6\,MHz channel it carried a moving picture, its synchronisation, colour, and FM sound, using
AM, VSB, QAM, FM and frequency interleaving at once, and it did so compatibly with receivers designed before
colour existed. Every engineer should understand it at least once.

\subsection{Scanning and synchronisation}

\mdcside{ch04_raster}{Interlaced raster scanning. The beam draws the odd lines (field 1), returns to the top,
and fills in the even lines (field 2); the dotted flyback strokes are blanked.}

How do you send a two-dimensional picture down a one-dimensional wire? You read it, line by line, like a
page of text. A camera tube or CRT turns an image into a signal by \term{raster scanning}: an electron beam
sweeps across the picture in horizontal \emph{lines}, left to right, and steps down the picture between
lines; the signal voltage at each instant is the brightness at the beam's position. The US monochrome
standard adopted in 1941 by the first National Television System Committee (NTSC), later called System M,
uses 525 lines per frame and 30 frames per second, a 4:3 aspect ratio, and \term{interlace}: each frame is
sent as two \emph{fields} of 262.5 lines, the first carrying the odd lines and the second the even lines
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_raster}), so the screen is refreshed 60 times a second (fast enough to avoid flicker at
the brightness levels of the day) while the bandwidth is that of 30 full frames. The 60\,Hz field rate was
chosen to match the mains frequency, so that hum bars and ripple in the receiver's power supply would stand
still on the screen rather than crawl through it; Europe's 50\,Hz mains gave 50 fields and 625 lines.

The line rate is $525\times30=15\,750$\,Hz, a line period of 63.5\,$\mu$s, of which about 52.6\,$\mu$s carry
picture and the rest is \emph{horizontal blanking}, during which the beam flies back. At the start of each
blanking interval a \emph{horizontal sync pulse}, more negative than black (``blacker than black''), tells the
receiver's line oscillator when to start a new line; a longer, serrated \emph{vertical sync} interval of
several lines, with equalising pulses to keep the interlace accurate, starts each field. If the receiver's oscillators drift too far from the transmitted rates, the picture
rolls or tears, which is why early sets had \emph{hold} controls to trim them (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_bush_tv22}).
About 483 of the 525 lines carry picture; the rest form the \emph{vertical blanking interval}, later used to carry closed captions
(line 21) and teletext. Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_ntsc_line} shows one line of a colour-bar test signal. The
amplitude scale is in \emph{IRE units}: blanking is 0, peak white 100, sync tip $-40$, and in NTSC black is
set up at 7.5.

\mdcphoto[0.5]{ch04_bush_tv22}{A Bush TV22 of 1950, a British receiver for the 405-line service, now in the
Design Museum, London. The knobs marked HOLD trim the free-running line and field oscillators until the
sync pulses can lock them; the round-cornered screen is the face of the cathode-ray tube itself.}

\mdcfig{ch04_ntsc_line}{One line of NTSC composite video carrying 75\% colour bars (synthesised from the
standard's equations). Left: the whole 63.56\,$\mu$s line: front porch, sync tip at $-40$\,IRE, colour burst
on the back porch, then eight bars whose average level is the luminance and whose superimposed 3.58\,MHz
oscillation is the chrominance. Right: the sync pulse and the nine-cycle colour burst.}

How much bandwidth does a picture need? If horizontal resolution is to match vertical resolution, a line
should carry as many picture elements across $4/3$ of the picture height as there are lines. With about 483
active lines and a \emph{Kell factor} of about 0.7 (the effective vertical resolution of a scanned raster is
less than the line count), that is about $0.7\times483\times\frac43\approx450$ elements per line, or 225
cycles of a black-white pattern, in 52.6\,$\mu$s: about 4.3\,MHz. The 1941 standard chose 4.2\,MHz of video
bandwidth. The arithmetic shows why television was a VHF service: a single channel needed 600 times the
bandwidth of an AM radio station. Stations checked all of this---resolution, linearity, interlace---with test
cards such as RCA's famous Indian-head pattern (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_indianhead}).

\mdcphoto[0.6]{ch04_indianhead}{A redrawing of RCA's Indian-head test pattern, broadcast by US stations for
decades before and between programmes. The converging wedges, numbered in tens of lines (30 means 300), measured
resolution; the circles revealed scanning nonlinearity; the grey steps checked the transfer curve.}

\subsection{The 6\,MHz channel}

The video signal amplitude-modulates the picture carrier with \emph{negative} modulation in System M: sync
tips produce maximum carrier power and white produces about 12.5\%. Negative modulation makes impulse noise
appear as dark rather than bright specks, gives the sync pulses the strongest signal, and gives the
receiver's AGC a stable reference (the sync tip level). VSB (Section~\ref{sec:ch04:vsb}) fits the 4.2\,MHz
video into the channel: the picture carrier sits 1.25\,MHz above the lower channel edge, the full upper
sideband extends 4.2\,MHz above it, and a vestige of the lower sideband extends 0.75\,MHz below it. The sound
is a separate FM carrier 4.5\,MHz above the picture carrier, with $\pm25$\,kHz deviation and 75\,$\mu$s
pre-emphasis (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_ntsc_spectrum}, top).

Receivers exploited the fixed 4.5\,MHz spacing in the \emph{intercarrier} sound system: the video detector,
being a nonlinear device, produces a 4.5\,MHz beat between the picture and sound carriers, which carries the
sound's FM (the picture carrier's AM is removed by limiting). The sound IF is then 4.5\,MHz regardless of the
tuner's drift, since both carriers drift together. That design decision, made for cheap receivers in the
1940s, became a constraint that shaped the colour standard.

\mdcfig{ch04_ntsc_spectrum}{Top: the 6\,MHz System M channel: vestigial-sideband picture with its carrier
1.25\,MHz above the lower edge, the colour subcarrier 3.58\,MHz above the picture carrier and the FM sound
carrier at $+4.5$\,MHz. Bottom (schematic): near the colour subcarrier, luminance energy is concentrated at
harmonics of the line rate, and the chrominance, modulated on a subcarrier at an odd multiple of half the line
rate, falls between them.}

\subsection{Compatible colour}

Colour television had to transmit three signals---red, green and blue---in the same 6\,MHz, and the second
NTSC (1953) insisted that existing monochrome receivers display a good picture from a colour broadcast, and
colour receivers from a monochrome one. It sounds impossible: three times the information, no extra
bandwidth, and the old sets must not notice. The solution has three ideas.

\paragraph{Luminance and chrominance.} Transmit, in place of the monochrome signal, the \term{luminance} $Y$, a
weighted sum of the gamma-corrected primaries matched to the eye's brightness sensitivity:
\begin{equation}
Y=0.299R+0.587G+0.114B.
\label{eq:ch04:luma}
\end{equation}
A monochrome receiver displays $Y$ and sees a normal picture. Colour is carried by two colour-difference
signals, which vanish for grey: NTSC used rotated versions of $R-Y$ and $B-Y$ called $I$ (in-phase, roughly
the orange--cyan axis) and $Q$ (quadrature, roughly green--purple), because the eye resolves fine detail better
along the $I$ axis. $I$ was given about 1.3\,MHz of bandwidth and $Q$ about 0.5\,MHz; the eye cannot see fine
colour detail, so the colour information needs far less bandwidth than the luminance.

\paragraph{QAM on a subcarrier.} $I$ and $Q$ are sent as quadrature DSB-SC on a \term{colour subcarrier} of
about 3.58\,MHz, inside the luminance band:
\begin{equation}
v(t)=Y(t)+I(t)\cos(\omega_{sc}t+33^\circ)+Q(t)\sin(\omega_{sc}t+33^\circ).
\end{equation}
The amplitude of the chroma signal is the colour's saturation and its phase is the hue: every colour is a
point on a circle, exactly like a QAM constellation point. Studios checked the whole chain with colour bars
displayed on a \emph{vectorscope} (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_smpte_bars}). Because the subcarrier is suppressed, the receiver
needs a phase reference: a \term{colour burst} of 8 to 10 cycles of the subcarrier is sent on the back porch of
every line (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_ntsc_line}), and a crystal oscillator in the receiver is phase-locked to it,
the most widespread PLL application before the FM stereo decoder. A phase error rotates every hue, which is
why NTSC sets had a ``tint'' control and why engineers joked that NTSC stood for ``Never Twice the Same
Colour''.

\paragraph{Frequency interleaving.} A picture is highly correlated from line to line, so the luminance
spectrum is not smooth: its energy is concentrated in clusters at harmonics of the line rate $f_H$ (each split
further by the frame rate). If the subcarrier is placed at an \emph{odd multiple of half the line rate}, the
chroma spectrum's clusters fall halfway between the luminance clusters (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_ntsc_spectrum},
bottom), like the teeth of two combs pushed together. The two signals share the band with little mutual
interference, and on a monochrome receiver the chroma appears as a fine dot pattern whose phase reverses
from line to line and frame to frame, so it largely cancels visually. Later receivers used \emph{comb
filters}, line-delay filters with nulls at the luminance harmonics, to separate the two almost perfectly.

\caption{SMPTE colour bars, the reference test signal of analog colour studios. On a

\begin{worked}[Deriving 3.579545\,MHz and 29.97\,Hz]
The colour subcarrier should be (i) high in the video band, where luminance energy is weak, but low enough to
leave room for chroma sidebands below 4.2\,MHz; (ii) an odd multiple of $f_H/2$ for interleaving; and (iii)
such that its beat with the 4.5\,MHz sound carrier, which the nonlinear video detector inevitably produces at
about 920\,kHz, is \emph{also} an odd multiple of $f_H/2$, so that this visible interference pattern
interleaves too.

With the monochrome line rate $f_H=15\,750$\,Hz, the sound carrier $4.5\,\text{MHz}=285.714f_H$ is not a
multiple of $f_H/2$ at all, so condition (iii) fails. The committee chose to make the sound carrier an
\emph{even} harmonic of $f_H/2$, $4.5\,\text{MHz}=286f_H$, and the subcarrier the odd harmonic
$455\times f_H/2$. Then the sound--chroma beat is $(572-455)f_H/2=117f_H/2$, odd, as required. Keeping the
sound at exactly 4.5\,MHz preserved every intercarrier-sound receiver; so the \emph{line rate} changed instead:
\begin{equation}
f_H=\frac{4.5\,\text{MHz}}{286}=15\,734.27\,\text{Hz},\qquad
f_{sc}=\frac{455}{2}f_H=\frac{315}{88}\,\text{MHz}=3.579545\,\text{MHz}.
\end{equation}
The frame rate became $f_H/525=29.97$\,Hz and the field rate 59.94\,Hz: both reduced by the factor
$15\,734.27/15\,750=1000/1001$. The 0.1\% change was small enough for every monochrome receiver's line and
field oscillators to follow. Its consequences outlived analog television: digital video standards kept 29.97
and 59.94 frames per second in the Americas, and editors still wrestle with \emph{drop-frame time code}, which
skips two frame numbers every minute except every tenth to keep time code aligned with the clock.
\end{worked}

\begin{history}[The colour war: CBS versus RCA]
Two incompatible visions of colour television fought for the US market from 1940 to 1953. CBS, under engineer
Peter Goldmark, developed a \emph{field-sequential} system: a spinning colour wheel in front of camera and
receiver showed successive fields in red, green and blue. It gave excellent colour but was incompatible with
the existing 525-line monochrome sets, which could not display it without an adapter, and its mechanical wheel
limited screen size. RCA, under David Sarnoff, insisted on an \emph{all-electronic, compatible} system, but its
early demonstrations were poor. In 1950 the FCC adopted the CBS system. RCA sued and lost in the Supreme Court
in 1951, but few CBS sets were made: the Korean War restricted materials for consumer electronics, and the
industry, owning millions of monochrome sets' worth of goodwill, waited. Meanwhile the second NTSC, drawing on
work at RCA, Hazeltine, Philco, General Electric and others, refined the compatible approach described here.
On 17 December 1953 the FCC reversed itself and adopted the NTSC compatible colour standard. The lesson that a
new system must coexist with the installed base---which FM stereo, C-QUAM, HD Radio and the GSM-to-5G
migrations all relearned---has rarely been taught more expensively.
\end{history}

\subsection{PAL and SECAM}

Europe adopted colour a decade later and improved on NTSC's phase sensitivity. Walter Bruch's \term{PAL}
(Phase Alternating Line, Telefunken, 1963) reverses the phase of the $V$ ($R-Y$) component on alternate lines.
A phase error that rotates hue one way on one line rotates it the other way on the next; a one-line delay in
the receiver averages the two and converts the hue error into a slight, much less visible loss of saturation.
No tint control is needed. Henri de France's \term{SECAM} (Séquentiel couleur à mémoire, France, 1967) avoided
QAM altogether: it sends the two colour-difference signals on alternate lines as \emph{FM} subcarriers, and a
delay-line memory supplies the missing one. FM made SECAM robust to phase errors and amplitude distortion, at
the cost of visible subcarrier patterns and difficult studio processing. Table~\ref{tab:ch04:tvstd} compares the
systems.

\begin{inpractice}[title=Ghosts, snow and the 2009 transition]
Analog TV made radio propagation visible. A reflection from a building or hill arriving $\Delta\tau$ after the
direct signal drew a displaced copy of the picture---a \emph{ghost}---shifted right by
$\Delta\tau/52.6\,\mu\text{s}$ of the picture width: a 1\,$\mu$s echo, with 300\,m of extra path, moved the
ghost about 2\% of the screen. This was multipath (Chapter~\ref{ch:channels}) displayed directly, and
ghost-cancelling reference signals and equalisers appeared in the 1990s just as analog's end approached.
Thermal noise appeared as \emph{snow}, a direct picture of white Gaussian noise; a viewer judged signal
strength by its density. In the United States full-power analog broadcasting ended on 12 June 2009, replaced
by ATSC digital television (8-VSB, 19.39\,Mb/s in the same 6\,MHz channels, carrying one HD or several SD
programmes), and the spectrum freed above 698\,MHz became the 700\,MHz LTE band (Chapter~\ref{ch:cellular}).
Europe's switch-overs to DVB-T ran from the mid-2000s to 2012, and the ATSC~3.0 standard now brings OFDM
(Chapter~\ref{ch:ofdm}) to US broadcasting.
\end{inpractice}

\section{Analog cellular: AMPS}
\label{sec:ch04:amps}
%======================================================================

The first-generation cellular systems were analog FM---essentially a two-way FM radio with a computer
attached. The Advanced Mobile Phone System (AMPS), developed at Bell Labs in the 1970s and launched
commercially in Chicago in October 1983, gave each call a pair of 30\,kHz channels, one in 824--849\,MHz
(mobile to base) and one 45\,MHz higher in 869--894\,MHz (base to mobile)---832 duplex channels after the
band expansion of the late 1980s, split between two competing carriers in each market. The voice was
narrowband FM with $\pm12$\,kHz peak deviation, so Carson's rule gives exactly the 30\,kHz channel. To improve
the dynamic range of speech over a noisy, fading channel, AMPS used a 2:1 \emph{syllabic compandor}
(compressing the speech level range by half in decibels before transmission and expanding it after) together
with pre-emphasis. The handsets were famously large (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_dynatac}).

Two features show how analog systems handled control. Each base station added a \emph{supervisory audio
tone} (SAT) at 5970, 6000 or 6030\,Hz above the voice band, which the phone transponded back; a wrong SAT told
the network it was hearing a co-channel call from another cell. Signalling during a call (handover commands,
for example) used \emph{blank-and-burst}: the voice was muted for about a tenth of a second while 10\,kb/s
Manchester-coded FSK data were sent on the voice channel. Dedicated control channels carried call set-up with
the same 10\,kb/s FSK.

The system relied on frequency reuse: the same channels were reused in cells far enough apart, in a pattern of
seven cells (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_reuse7}), that co-channel interference stayed about 18\,dB below the wanted
signal, which FM's capture effect (Section~\ref{sec:ch04:capture}) turned into acceptable voice quality.
Chapters~\ref{ch:multipleaccess} and~\ref{ch:cellular} develop the cellular concept and its 18\,dB design
target.

Analog cellular had well-known weaknesses: anyone with a scanner could listen to calls, which led US law to ban
receivers covering the cellular bands; phones could be \emph{cloned} by copying their serial numbers over the
air; and capacity was limited to one call per 30\,kHz. Digital AMPS (IS-54/IS-136) put three TDMA calls into the
same 30\,kHz channel, and GSM and CDMA displaced it entirely; the FCC allowed US carriers to switch AMPS off from
February 2008. Similar analog systems---NMT in the Nordic countries (1981), TACS in the UK (1985)---followed the
same course.

\section{Analog multiplexing: the FDM telephone network}
\label{sec:ch04:fdm}
%======================================================================

In 1951, when AT\&T opened its first transcontinental microwave route, a single long-distance circuit across
the United States was not a dedicated wire: it was one 4\,kHz slice of a radio channel shared by hundreds of
other conversations, each shifted to its own frequency slot. Before digital transmission, long-distance
telephony was a vast application of single sideband.

\begin{analogy}[Lanes on a highway]
Frequency-division multiplexing is a multi-lane highway: each conversation gets its own lane (a 4\,kHz slot),
and cars in different lanes never collide. Building the hierarchy is like merging local roads into a highway,
then highways into a superhighway: twelve lanes become a group, five groups a supergroup, and so on. The limit
of the analogy: on a real highway the lanes are side by side in space; here they are side by side in
frequency, on the same wire, and the ``lane markings'' are crystal filters that must be astonishingly sharp.
\end{analogy}

Each level of the hierarchy carried \emph{pilot} tones that let automatic gain regulators in the repeaters
compensate for the temperature-dependent loss of cables. The Bell System's coaxial \emph{L-carrier} systems
grew from a few hundred circuits per coaxial pair in the L1 system of 1941 to 10\,800 in L5 (1974), with
repeaters about every mile; a cable of 22 coaxial tubes, ten working pairs plus a spare, carried over 100\,000
simultaneous conversations. On microwave radio relay, such as AT\&T's TD-2 system that opened the first
transcontinental route in 1951, the FDM baseband frequency-modulated a microwave carrier: \emph{FDM-FM}, the
same arrangement used on the first generation of communications satellites (Chapter~\ref{ch:satellite}). FDM
systems were marvels of analog engineering: intermodulation in thousands of repeaters, pilot regulation,
crystal filters by the million.

\begin{keyidea}[Why digital transmission won]
An analog repeater amplifies whatever arrives, signal and noise together, so noise and distortion
\emph{accumulate} along a route: a link of $N$ identical repeater sections has $N$ times the noise of one
section (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_repeaters}), and long FDM circuits were engineered to tight per-kilometre noise
budgets (the CCITT's objectives for a 2500\,km hypothetical reference circuit). A digital repeater
\emph{regenerates}: it decides each bit and sends a fresh pulse, so as long as each section's error rate is
tiny, the end-to-end quality is independent of distance. This, together with the falling cost of digital logic
and the ease of switching time slots, is why the Bell System began installing T1 PCM carrier in 1962
(Chapter~\ref{ch:pcm}), why the long-haul network was digital by the late 1980s, and why the FDM hierarchy is
now history. During the transition, \emph{transmultiplexers} converted between the two worlds digitally---two
FDM groups (24 channels) to one 24-channel T1 stream---using the polyphase filter banks of
Chapter~\ref{ch:dsp}, an early triumph of multirate signal processing and the direct ancestor of OFDM.
\end{keyidea}

section adds its own noise, so the end-to-end SNR falls by 3\,dB every time the route doubles in length; a
regenerated digital circuit keeps the quality set by its codec, whatever the distance.}

\section{Analog systems that survive}
\label{sec:ch04:survive}
%======================================================================

Analog modulation is not only history. In 2026 it remains in service where simplicity, backward compatibility
or graceful degradation matter:
\begin{itemize}
\item AM broadcasting on long, medium and short waves (Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_lisnagarvey}), and aviation VHF
voice (AM), for the capture-effect
reason above and for decades of installed equipment worldwide. Aviation is unlikely to change soon: every
aircraft and control tower would have to be re-equipped at once.
\item FM broadcasting worldwide, which remains the most-listened-to radio platform in most countries, although
some (Norway in 2017, Switzerland's public broadcaster in 2024) have switched national FM networks off in
favour of DAB+.
\item Narrowband FM in marine VHF, amateur radio, family radio services and much of the world's low-cost
two-way radio, although professional land-mobile radio has largely moved to digital systems such as P25, TETRA
and DMR, whose 12.5\,kHz channel plans were inherited from analog FM.
\item SSB on HF for maritime, military and amateur long-distance links, increasingly alongside digital HF
modems that use the SSB transceiver as a 3\,kHz pipe.
\item Analog FM in wireless microphones and in-ear monitors, baby monitors and the cheapest wireless audio
links, and analog video in some low-latency drone (FPV) links, where FM's graceful degradation and zero
latency are prized.
\end{itemize}

\mdcphoto[0.42]{ch04_lisnagarvey}{The Lisnagarvey transmitter mast in Northern Ireland, photographed when it
carried BBC Radio Ulster on medium wave (1341\,kHz, 100\,kW). AM broadcasting is shrinking, but masts like this
still carry it in many countries.}

\begin{keyidea}[What analog radio teaches the digital engineer]
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Everything is a complex envelope.} AM, SSB, PM and FM differ only in how $\tilde{s}(t)$ is
built. So do QPSK, QAM and OFDM.
\item \textbf{Coherent detection needs a carrier reference.} The Costas loop was invented for DSB-SC voice;
the FM stereo pilot and the NTSC colour burst were the first pilot-aided synchronisation schemes.
\item \textbf{Bandwidth buys noise immunity}, but only above a threshold. Shannon made both statements exact.
\item \textbf{Image rejection and I/Q balance are the same problem}, from the phasing SSB exciter of 1950 to
the zero-IF RFIC of today.
\item \textbf{Compatibility shapes standards.} FM stereo, NTSC colour and HD Radio were designed around
receivers already in people's homes; 29.97 frames per second is the scar.
\end{itemize}
\end{keyidea}

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:ch04:summary}
%======================================================================

\begin{itemize}
\item Modulation translates a message to a carrier chosen for antenna size, propagation and multiplexing.
Every analog modulation is a rule for building the complex envelope.
\item AM (DSB-LC) spends most of its power on the carrier so that a diode envelope detector, whose $RC$ time
constant must lie between the carrier and message periods and below the diagonal-clipping limit
$\sqrt{1-\mu^2}/(2\pi f_m\mu)$, can demodulate it. High-power transmitters modulate an efficient class-C or
switching amplifier's supply.
\item DSB-SC and SSB put all power in the sidebands but need coherent detection. Ring modulators and Gilbert
cells multiply; squaring and Costas loops recover the carrier. SSB is generated by filtering, by phasing
(Hartley) or by the Weaver method; a mistuned SSB voice is pitch-shifted, not destroyed. VSB, with its
Nyquist-symmetric vestige, carried television video.
\item FM spectra are given by Bessel functions; Carson's rule $2(\Delta f+W)$ estimates the bandwidth. FM is
generated by VCOs in PLLs or by Armstrong's narrowband-modulation-and-multiplication, and demodulated by
discriminators, quadrature detectors, PLLs or the one-line digital $\arg(z[n]z^*[n-1])$.
\item Above threshold FM's output SNR is $3D^2P_m\gamma$ thanks to the parabolic noise spectrum, which
de-emphasis reduces by a further 10--13\,dB. Below about 10\,dB CNR, clicks cause a threshold. The capture effect
suppresses weaker co-channel FM signals.
\item FM stereo multiplexes $L+R$, a 19\,kHz pilot and $L-R$ on a coherent 38\,kHz DSB-SC subcarrier; RDS adds
data at 57\,kHz.
\item The superheterodyne converts every station to a fixed IF. The IF is a compromise between image rejection
and selectivity; double conversion and zero-IF are the escapes. AGC holds the level; noise figure sets
sensitivity and IP3 sets dynamic range. The All American Five is the design at its leanest.
\item NTSC television combined VSB-AM video, FM sound and QAM colour with frequency interleaving, and derived
its 3.579545\,MHz subcarrier and 29.97\,Hz frame rate from a compatibility constraint.
\item AMPS cellular used FM in 30\,kHz channels; the FDM telephone network stacked SSB channels into groups and
supergroups until digital regeneration replaced it.
\end{itemize}

\problems
\begin{enumerate}[label=\thechapter.\arabic*]
\item An AM transmitter radiates 10\,kW of carrier. With tone modulation at $\mu=0.8$, find the total power, the
power in each sideband, the efficiency and the PEP. An oscilloscope shows an envelope with $A_{\max}=1.6$\,V and
$A_{\min}=0.4$\,V: what is $\mu$, and by what percentage has the antenna current risen above its unmodulated
value?
\item Derive the diagonal-clipping condition~\eqref{eq:ch04:diagclip}, showing that the worst case occurs at
$\cos\omega_mt=-\mu$. Then show that with an AC load $R_{ac}<R_{dc}$ the detector clips the troughs unless
$\mu\le R_{ac}/R_{dc}$.
\item A square-law detector produces $y=x^2$ followed by a low-pass filter. For tone-modulated AM, find the
ratio of the second-harmonic to the fundamental at the output and show that it is $\mu/4$. What modulation index
keeps this distortion below 5\%?
\item A DSB-SC signal is demodulated with a local carrier having a phase error that drifts linearly at 1\,Hz.
Describe the output. Repeat for SSB, and explain the difference in terms of the analytic signal.
\item Derive the Bessel expansion~\eqref{eq:ch04:bessel} from the generating function
$e^{j\beta\sin\theta}=\sum_n J_n(\beta)e^{jn\theta}$. Use it to show that $\sum_nJ_n^2(\beta)=1$.
\item You must set the deviation of a land-mobile transmitter to exactly 5\,kHz using the first Bessel null.
What tone frequency should you use? Which tone frequency would set a broadcast exciter to 75\,kHz at the
\emph{second} carrier null ($\beta=5.520$)?
\item Using Carson's rule, how many 12.5\,kHz land-mobile FM channels ($\Delta f=2.5$\,kHz, $W=3$\,kHz) fit in the
bandwidth of one broadcast FM channel? Compare the output SNR advantage over AM of each system, above
threshold.
\item Design an Armstrong transmitter for a 100.1\,MHz carrier with at least 75\,kHz deviation, starting from a
100\,kHz crystal oscillator whose narrowband modulator gives 20\,Hz deviation. Use only doublers and triplers,
and one mixer. By how many decibels is the crystal's phase noise multiplied?
\item Show that for PM the output noise PSD is flat rather than parabolic, and derive the output SNR
$k_p^2P_m\gamma$. For a message with a flat spectrum, compare FM and PM with the same peak frequency deviation.
Why does broadcast FM with pre-emphasis behave like PM at high audio frequencies?
\item Evaluate the de-emphasis improvement~\eqref{eq:ch04:deemph} for 50\,$\mu$s and $W=15$\,kHz, and for a
two-way radio with $W=3$\,kHz and a 750\,$\mu$s characteristic. Why does the improvement depend so strongly on
$W/f_1$?
\item An FM link has $P_R/N_0=70$\,dB-Hz and carries audio with $W=15$\,kHz. Assuming a threshold at 10\,dB CNR in
the Carson bandwidth and a 3\,dB margin, find the largest usable deviation and the resulting output SNR for a
tone. Use Rice's formula~\eqref{eq:ch04:rice} to estimate the click rate at threshold.
\item An FM receiver has IF 10.7\,MHz and high-side LO. It is tuned to 88.1\,MHz. Find the image frequency. What
preselector attenuation is needed if an image signal is 40\,dB stronger than the wanted one and the demodulator
needs 20\,dB of protection? How many tuned circuits of $Q=30$ provide it? Repeat for a receiver with a 150\,MHz
first IF.
\item Show that the digital FM demodulator~\eqref{eq:ch04:digfm} is unbiased for any carrier amplitude and
explain what happens when $|\hat f|>f_s/2$. What minimum sample rate does a broadcast FM stereo signal need?
\item\label{prob:ch04:stereonoise} Explain why FM stereo is about 20\,dB noisier than mono at the same
carrier-to-noise ratio. (\emph{Hint:} integrate the parabolic noise PSD over 23--53\,kHz and over 0--15\,kHz, and
account for the 45\% injection levels and the dematrixing.)
\item The PAL colour subcarrier is $f_{sc}=(1135/4+1/625)f_H$ with $f_H=15\,625$\,Hz. Compute it, and explain why
PAL uses a quarter-line rather than half-line offset. For NTSC, verify that the chroma--sound beat is an odd
multiple of $f_H/2$, and compute the drop-frame time-code error that would accumulate in one hour without
dropping frame numbers.
\item A receiver has a noise figure of 8\,dB, a 3\,kHz IF bandwidth and an input IP3 of $+10$\,dBm. Find its MDS
and SFDR. Two interfering carriers at $-40$\,dBm each, 20 and 40\,kHz above the wanted channel, are present.
Where do the IM3 products fall, and how strong are they?
\item \emph{(Simulation)} Generate an FM stereo MPX signal with different tones in $L$ and $R$, frequency-modulate
it with 75\,kHz deviation at 400\,kS/s, add noise, and write a decoder. Plot left--right separation versus pilot
phase error and compare with the formula in Section~\ref{sec:ch04:stereo}. Then reproduce the 20\,dB stereo noise
penalty by measuring the output SNR in mono and stereo modes.
\item \emph{(Simulation)} Implement the Weaver SSB modulator of Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_ssb_blocks}b and a matching
Weaver demodulator. Measure the unwanted-sideband suppression as a function of the low-pass filter order, and
as a function of a gain imbalance between the two branches. Compare the latter with the phasing-method curve of
Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_ssb_phase}.
\end{enumerate}

\furtherreading
\begin{itemize}
\item J.~R.~Carson, ``Notes on the theory of modulation,'' \emph{Proceedings of the IRE}, vol.~10, no.~1,
pp.~57--64, 1922. The first analysis of FM bandwidth; it concluded, correctly for narrowband FM, that FM offered
no bandwidth saving over AM. Read it alongside Armstrong.
\item E.~H.~Armstrong, ``A method of reducing disturbances in radio signaling by a system of frequency
modulation,'' \emph{Proceedings of the IRE}, vol.~24, no.~5, pp.~689--740, 1936. The paper that launched wideband
FM, with field-test data from the Empire State Building experiments.
\item S.~Haykin and M.~Moher, \emph{Communication Systems}, 5th ed., Wiley, 2009. A standard textbook treatment
of amplitude and angle modulation, VSB, the superheterodyne and noise in analog systems, including the FM
threshold.
\item B.~P.~Lathi and Z.~Ding, \emph{Modern Digital and Analog Communication Systems}, 5th ed., Oxford University
Press, 2019. Clear derivations of AM and FM noise performance and of Armstrong's indirect method.
\item \emph{The ARRL Handbook for Radio Communications}, American Radio Relay League (annual). Practical SSB, FM
and receiver circuits, filter design and receiver measurements (MDS, IP3, blocking, reciprocal mixing), updated
every year.
\item T.~Lewis, \emph{Empire of the Air: The Men Who Made Radio}, HarperCollins, 1991. A gripping history of de
Forest, Armstrong and Sarnoff, the regeneration and FM patent wars, and the birth of broadcasting.
\item F.~E.~Terman, \emph{Radio Engineers' Handbook}, McGraw-Hill, 1943. The definitive compendium of
vacuum-tube-era radio engineering: tuned circuits, modulators, detectors, superheterodynes and transmitters, by
the Stanford professor who taught a generation of radio engineers.
\item U.~L.~Rohde, J.~C.~Whitaker and H.~Zahnd, \emph{Communications Receivers: Principles and Design}, 4th ed.,
McGraw-Hill, 2017. The practitioner's reference on superheterodynes, AGC, dynamic range and modern receiver
architectures.
\item D.~K.~Weaver, ``A third method of generation and detection of single-sideband signals,''
\emph{Proceedings of the IRE}, vol.~44, no.~12, 1956; and J.~P.~Costas, ``Synchronous communications,'' in the
same issue. Two short classics from the IRE's single-sideband special issue.
\item S.~O.~Rice, ``Noise in FM receivers,'' in M.~Rosenblatt (ed.), \emph{Time Series Analysis}, Wiley, 1963. The
click analysis behind the FM threshold.
\item Members of the Technical Staff, Bell Telephone Laboratories, \emph{Transmission Systems for
Communications}, 5th ed., 1982. The Bell System's own account of FDM channel banks, the L-carrier and microwave
radio, alongside the early digital systems that replaced them.
\item K.~B.~Benson (ed.), \emph{Television Engineering Handbook}, McGraw-Hill, 1986. NTSC, PAL and SECAM in full
engineering detail, from scanning standards to colour encoding and RF transmission.
\end{itemize}
