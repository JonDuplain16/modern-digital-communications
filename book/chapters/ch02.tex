\chapter{Signals, Spectra and Systems}
\label{ch:signals}

\begin{chapterintro}
A communication engineer thinks in two domains at once: the time domain, where a signal
is a waveform on an oscilloscope, and the frequency domain, where it is a distribution of
energy across a spectrum analyzer screen. This chapter builds fluency in both. We start
from phasors and Fourier series, develop the Fourier transform and its properties, study
linear time-invariant systems and the distortion they introduce, define bandwidth in the
several ways engineers actually use, move to discrete time with the DFT and FFT, and
finish with the complex-envelope representation of bandpass signals that the rest of the
book is written in.
\end{chapterintro}

\section{Signals as the language of communication}

A \term{signal} is any quantity that varies with time (or space) and carries information:
the voltage from a microphone, the current in a telegraph line, the electric field at an
antenna, the light intensity in a fibre. Communication engineering is the art of shaping
signals so that the information they carry survives a journey through a physical medium.
The medium always does three things to a signal: it \emph{attenuates} it, it
\emph{distorts} it (different frequencies are delayed and attenuated differently), and it
\emph{adds noise}. The tools of this chapter describe the first two exactly; Chapter~\ref{ch:noise}
describes the third.

We use a handful of standard signal classes. \emph{Deterministic} signals are known
functions of time, like a carrier $\cos(2\pi f_c t)$ or a pulse shape $p(t)$.
\emph{Random} signals, like noise or a stream of data symbols, are described statistically.
\emph{Energy signals} have finite energy $E=\int |x(t)|^2dt$ (a pulse); \emph{power
signals} have finite average power $P=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}|x(t)|^2dt$
(a sinusoid, or a data stream that goes on indefinitely). Real signals are measured by
real instruments, but we will quickly find that complex-valued signals are the natural
mathematical description of real radio waves.

\section{Sinusoids, phasors and negative frequency}

The sinusoid $x(t)=A\cos(2\pi f_0 t+\phi)$ is the atom of signal analysis, for a simple
reason: linear time-invariant systems (Section~\ref{sec:lti}) map sinusoids to
sinusoids of the same frequency, changing only amplitude and phase. Euler's formula,
\begin{equation}
e^{j\theta}=\cos\theta+j\sin\theta ,
\end{equation}
lets us write the sinusoid as the real part of a rotating complex number, the
\term{phasor}:
\begin{equation}
A\cos(2\pi f_0 t+\phi)=\re\{A e^{j\phi}e^{j2\pi f_0 t}\}
=\frac{A}{2}e^{j\phi}e^{j2\pi f_0 t}+\frac{A}{2}e^{-j\phi}e^{-j2\pi f_0 t}.
\label{eq:ch02:euler}
\end{equation}
The second form, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ch02_phasor}, is the source of the
\emph{negative frequencies} that appear in every spectrum in this book. A real cosine is
the sum of two counter-rotating phasors, one at $+f_0$ and one at $-f_0$; their imaginary
parts cancel and their real parts add. Negative frequency is not a mathematical fiction:
a complex signal $e^{-j2\pi f_0 t}$ is a phasor rotating clockwise, and a radio receiver with
I and Q outputs can tell it apart from one rotating counter-clockwise. For a \emph{real}
signal, however, the spectrum at $-f$ is always the complex conjugate of the spectrum at
$+f$, so the negative-frequency half carries no new information.

\mdcfig{ch02_phasor}{Left: a real cosine is the sum of two phasors of half amplitude
rotating in opposite directions. Right: a phase offset $\phi$ rotates the phasor and shifts
the waveform in time. Carrier phase recovery (Chapter~\ref{ch:sync}) is the problem of
estimating $\phi$.}

\begin{keyidea}[Think in phasors]
Multiplying a signal by $e^{j2\pi\Delta f t}$ shifts its whole spectrum by $\Delta f$; multiplying by
$e^{j\phi}$ rotates it by $\phi$. Almost every operation in a radio---mixing, tuning, carrier
recovery, Doppler correction, OFDM---is one of these two multiplications.
\end{keyidea}

\section{Fourier series}

In 1807 Joseph Fourier claimed, to the scepticism of Lagrange, that any periodic function
could be written as a sum of sinusoids. For a signal with period $T_0$ and fundamental
frequency $f_0=1/T_0$,
\begin{equation}
x(t)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}c_k\,e^{j2\pi k f_0 t},
\qquad c_k=\frac{1}{T_0}\int_{T_0}x(t)\,e^{-j2\pi k f_0 t}\,dt .
\label{eq:ch02:fs}
\end{equation}
The coefficients $c_k$ are the amplitudes of the harmonics. For a square wave of unit
amplitude, only odd harmonics appear and their amplitudes fall as $4/(\pi k)$, as
Figure~\ref{fig:ch02_fourier_series} shows. Partial sums converge to the square wave
everywhere except at the jumps, where they overshoot by about 9\% no matter how many
terms are used: the \term{Gibbs phenomenon}. It reappears whenever a spectrum is truncated
abruptly---in the ringing of a sharp filter's step response and in the sidelobes of a
rectangular window (Section~\ref{sec:windows}).

\mdcfig{ch02_fourier_series}{Fourier synthesis of a square wave. The overshoot at the
discontinuity does not shrink as harmonics are added; it only narrows. The spectrum falls at
only 6\,dB per octave because the waveform has jumps.}

\begin{inpractice}[Harmonics are everywhere]
A transmitter's power amplifier driven into saturation turns a sinusoid into something
closer to a square wave, generating harmonics at $2f_c$, $3f_c$ and above. Regulators set
strict limits on these \emph{spurious emissions}---the FCC's general limit for many
licensed services is 43\,dB plus $10\log_{10}$ of the transmit power in watts below the
carrier---so every transmitter ends with a harmonic filter. The rate at which harmonics
decay is set by the smoothness of the waveform: a jump gives $1/k$ decay, a jump in slope
gives $1/k^2$, and so on. This is the same smoothness--bandwidth trade that governs
pulse shaping in Chapter~\ref{ch:baseband}.
\end{inpractice}

\section{The Fourier transform}

Letting the period grow without bound turns the Fourier series into the Fourier transform,
which describes non-periodic signals:
\begin{equation}
X(f)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)\,e^{-j2\pi f t}\,dt,
\qquad x(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}X(f)\,e^{j2\pi f t}\,df.
\label{eq:ch02:ft}
\end{equation}
We use ordinary frequency $f$ in hertz rather than angular frequency $\omega$ because it
removes all the stray factors of $2\pi$ and because engineers read spectra in hertz. $X(f)$
is complex: its magnitude says how much of each frequency is present, and its phase says
how the components are aligned in time.

\subsection{Properties worth knowing by heart}

Table~\ref{tab:ch02:props} lists the properties used most often. Each has a physical
meaning. \emph{Time shift} multiplies the spectrum by a linear phase: delay is phase slope.
\emph{Frequency shift} (modulation) moves the spectrum: this is how every transmitter
places a signal at its carrier. \emph{Scaling} says that compressing a signal in time
stretches its spectrum: short pulses need wide bandwidth. \emph{Convolution} in time is
multiplication in frequency: this is why filters are described by frequency responses.
\emph{Parseval's theorem} says energy can be computed in either domain.

\begin{table}[htbp]\centering\small
\caption{Fourier transform properties ($x(t)\leftrightarrow X(f)$).}\label{tab:ch02:props}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Property & Time domain & Frequency domain\\
\midrule
Linearity & $a\,x(t)+b\,y(t)$ & $a\,X(f)+b\,Y(f)$\\
Time shift & $x(t-\tau)$ & $e^{-j2\pi f\tau}X(f)$\\
Frequency shift & $x(t)\,e^{j2\pi f_0 t}$ & $X(f-f_0)$\\
Modulation & $x(t)\cos(2\pi f_0 t)$ & $\tfrac12[X(f-f_0)+X(f+f_0)]$\\
Scaling & $x(at)$ & $\frac{1}{|a|}X(f/a)$\\
Conjugation & $x^*(t)$ & $X^*(-f)$\\
Convolution & $x(t)*y(t)$ & $X(f)\,Y(f)$\\
Multiplication & $x(t)\,y(t)$ & $X(f)*Y(f)$\\
Differentiation & $\frac{d}{dt}x(t)$ & $j2\pi f\,X(f)$\\
Duality & $X(t)$ & $x(-f)$\\
Parseval & $\int|x(t)|^2dt$ & $\int|X(f)|^2df$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsection{Transform pairs}

A few pairs appear over and over (Figure~\ref{fig:ch02_pairs}). The rectangular pulse of
width $T$ transforms to a sinc function,
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{rect}(t/T)\ \leftrightarrow\ T\,\sinc(fT),\qquad \sinc(x)=\frac{\sin\pi x}{\pi x},
\end{equation}
whose first nulls are at $\pm1/T$ and whose sidelobes decay slowly, as $1/f$. By duality,
an ideal ``brick-wall'' low-pass spectrum corresponds to a sinc pulse in time---the pulse
Nyquist proposed for signalling without intersymbol interference. The triangle,
the convolution of two rectangles, transforms to $\sinc^2$ with faster-decaying sidelobes.
The Gaussian is its own transform, and among all pulses it minimises the product of
time duration and bandwidth (measured as standard deviations): this is the
time--bandwidth \emph{uncertainty principle}, $\sigma_t\sigma_f\ge 1/(4\pi)$. GSM's GMSK
modulation uses Gaussian-filtered pulses for exactly this reason (Chapter~\ref{ch:modulation}).
Two idealised pairs complete the toolkit: the impulse $\delta(t)\leftrightarrow1$, and the
impulse train $\sum_n\delta(t-nT)\leftrightarrow\frac1T\sum_k\delta(f-k/T)$, which is the
mathematics of sampling.

\mdcfig{ch02_pairs}{Four Fourier transform pairs. Smoother time functions have spectra that
decay faster; narrower time functions have wider spectra.}

\subsection{Energy and power spectral density}

For an energy signal, $|X(f)|^2$ is the \term{energy spectral density}: it tells how
the energy is distributed in frequency. For power signals---which include every random
data stream---we need the \term{power spectral density} (PSD) $S_x(f)$, defined through
the autocorrelation function by the Wiener--Khinchin theorem (Chapter~\ref{ch:noise}):
\begin{equation}
S_x(f)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}R_x(\tau)\,e^{-j2\pi f\tau}\,d\tau,\qquad
R_x(\tau)=\E[x(t+\tau)x^*(t)].
\end{equation}
The PSD is what a spectrum analyzer displays, what a regulator's emission mask
constrains, and what we compute for every modulation format in this book.

\section{Linear time-invariant systems}
\label{sec:lti}

A system is \emph{linear} if the response to a sum of inputs is the sum of the responses,
and \emph{time-invariant} if delaying the input merely delays the output. Wires, cables,
filters, amplifiers operated in their linear range, and radio channels over short
enough intervals are all well modelled as LTI systems. An LTI system is completely
described by its \term{impulse response} $h(t)$, and its output is the convolution
\begin{equation}
y(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}h(\tau)\,x(t-\tau)\,d\tau
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad Y(f)=H(f)\,X(f).
\label{eq:ch02:conv}
\end{equation}
The \term{frequency response} $H(f)$ is the transform of $h(t)$. A sinusoid at frequency
$f$ passes through with its amplitude multiplied by $|H(f)|$ and its phase advanced by
$\angle H(f)$. The two numbers that summarise an LTI channel are therefore the gain and the
phase at every frequency---and a channel is ``good'' when both are well behaved across the
signal's band.

\subsection{Distortionless transmission}

A channel that only scales and delays, $y(t)=a\,x(t-\tau)$, has
$H(f)=a\,e^{-j2\pi f\tau}$: constant magnitude and \emph{linear} phase. Any departure from
either causes distortion. Amplitude distortion changes the relative sizes of frequency
components; phase distortion misaligns them in time. It is convenient to describe phase
behaviour by the \term{group delay},
\begin{equation}
\tau_g(f)=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{d\angle H(f)}{df},
\end{equation}
the delay experienced by a narrow packet of energy near frequency $f$. A distortionless
channel has constant group delay; Heaviside's distortionless line of Chapter~\ref{ch:history}
is a physical example.

\subsection{Filters: the classic trade-offs}

Figure~\ref{fig:ch02_filters} compares four classic fifth-order analog low-pass filter
families, all designed for a 1\,kHz cutoff. The \emph{Butterworth} filter is maximally flat
in the passband. The \emph{Chebyshev} filter allows passband ripple in exchange for a
steeper transition. The \emph{elliptic} (Cauer) filter allows ripple in both bands and gives
the steepest transition of all for a given order. The \emph{Bessel} filter gives up
selectivity for nearly constant group delay. The time-domain panel reveals the price of
selectivity: the sharp filters have large group-delay peaks near the band edge, and a
rectangular pulse passing through them rings. For data signals, where the pulse shape is
the information, this ringing is intersymbol interference; that is why data receivers
often prefer linear-phase digital FIR filters (Chapter~\ref{ch:dsp}).

\mdcfig{ch02_filters}{Four fifth-order low-pass filters with a 1\,kHz cutoff. Left: magnitude.
Centre: group delay. Right: response to a 3\,ms rectangular pulse. Steeper magnitude
responses bring larger group-delay variation and more ringing.}

\begin{worked}[Filter selection for an audio channel]
A voice channel must pass 300--3400\,Hz and reject a 4\,kHz pilot tone by 40\,dB. The
transition band is only $4000/3400=1.18$ octaves wide. Standard design charts give the
required orders as roughly 15 (Butterworth), 7 (Chebyshev, 0.5\,dB ripple) or 5 (elliptic).
The elliptic design is the cheapest in components but has the worst group delay; for voice,
where the ear is insensitive to moderate phase distortion, it is the usual choice. For a
data modem using the same channel, the group-delay variation would have to be equalized
(Chapter~\ref{ch:equalization}).
\end{worked}

\section{Bandwidth}
\label{sec:bandwidth}

``Bandwidth'' sounds like a single number, but there are several useful definitions, and
confusing them causes real errors in system design. Figure~\ref{fig:ch02_bandwidth}
illustrates them for the spectrum of a rectangular pulse of duration $T$.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt]
\item[3\,dB bandwidth:] the width of the band where the PSD is within 3\,dB of its peak;
for the rectangular pulse, about $0.89/T$.
\item[Null-to-null bandwidth:] the width of the main lobe, $2/T$ here.
\item[Occupied bandwidth:] the band containing a stated fraction, usually 99\%, of the
power. This is the regulator's definition, and for the rectangular pulse it is over
$20/T$ because the sidelobes decay so slowly.
\item[Noise-equivalent bandwidth:] the width $B_N$ of an ideal rectangular filter that
passes the same white-noise power as the actual filter, $B_N=\int|H(f)|^2df/|H(0)|^2$. It is
the right number to use in noise calculations (Chapter~\ref{ch:noise}).
\item[Nyquist bandwidth:] the minimum bandwidth, $R_s/2$ at baseband, in which symbols at
rate $R_s$ can be sent without intersymbol interference (Chapter~\ref{ch:baseband}).
\end{description}

\mdcfig[0.85]{ch02_bandwidth}{Three bandwidth definitions applied to the spectrum of a
rectangular pulse. Because the sidelobes decay slowly, the 99\% occupied bandwidth is
more than ten times the null-to-null bandwidth.}

\begin{pitfall}[Comparing bandwidths across definitions]
A datasheet may quote a filter's 3\,dB bandwidth, a standard may specify an occupied
bandwidth, and a noise calculation needs a noise-equivalent bandwidth. For a
root-raised-cosine spectrum with roll-off 0.35 at 1\,Msymbol/s, the three are roughly
1.0, 1.3 and 1.0\,MHz; for a Gaussian filter they differ more. Always state which
definition you are using.
\end{pitfall}

\section{Discrete time: from DTFT to FFT}

Modern radios process sampled signals. If $x[n]=x(nT_s)$ is sampled at rate
$f_s=1/T_s$, its spectrum is the \emph{discrete-time Fourier transform}
\begin{equation}
X(e^{j2\pi f/f_s})=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}x[n]\,e^{-j2\pi fn/f_s},
\end{equation}
which is periodic in $f$ with period $f_s$. The periodicity is the frequency-domain face of
sampling: a sampled signal cannot distinguish $f$ from $f+kf_s$. Chapter~\ref{ch:pcm} develops
the sampling theorem and aliasing in full; for now it is enough to remember that all
the information of a correctly sampled signal lies in one period, $-f_s/2\le f<f_s/2$.

\subsection{The discrete Fourier transform}

A computer can only handle a finite block of $N$ samples and a finite set of
frequencies. The \term{discrete Fourier transform} (DFT) evaluates the spectrum at $N$
equally spaced frequencies $f_k=kf_s/N$:
\begin{equation}
X[k]=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]\,e^{-j2\pi kn/N},\qquad
x[n]=\frac1N\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}X[k]\,e^{j2\pi kn/N}.
\label{eq:ch02:dft}
\end{equation}
The DFT treats the block as one period of a periodic signal. That implicit periodicity has
two consequences that dominate practical spectrum analysis: \emph{leakage} and
\emph{resolution}.

\subsection{Windows and leakage}
\label{sec:windows}

If a sinusoid does not complete an integer number of cycles in the block, the periodic
extension has a jump at the block boundary, and energy ``leaks'' from the true frequency into
all other bins, following the sidelobes of the sinc-shaped transform of the rectangular
block. Multiplying the block by a smooth \term{window} that tapers to zero at both ends
removes the jump. Figure~\ref{fig:ch02_windows} shows the trade: the rectangular window
has the narrowest main lobe but sidelobes only 13\,dB down, so a weak tone 60\,dB below a
strong one 30\,Hz away is completely hidden. The Hann window lowers sidelobes to $-31$\,dB
with a faster roll-off, the Blackman--Harris window to $-92$\,dB, and the flat-top window
sacrifices resolution for amplitude accuracy. Choosing a window is choosing between
resolution, dynamic range and amplitude accuracy.

\mdcfig{ch02_windows}{Left: DFT of a strong tone and a tone 60\,dB weaker, 30\,Hz away, with
four windows. Only low-sidelobe windows reveal the weak tone. Right: the spectra of the
windows themselves, in units of DFT bins.}

\subsection{Resolution and zero padding}

Two tones closer together than about $f_s/N$ Hz---the reciprocal of the observation time
$NT_s$---cannot be resolved, no matter how the data are processed. Padding the block with
zeros computes the spectrum at more closely spaced frequencies, which makes plots smoother
and helps locate an isolated peak precisely, but it does not add resolution
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch02_dft_resolution}). Resolution requires observation time.

\mdcfig{ch02_dft_resolution}{Left: two tones 1.2\,Hz apart are merged with 64 samples at
100\,Hz and resolved with 256. Right: zero padding a 32-point DFT interpolates the same
underlying spectrum more finely.}

\subsection{The fast Fourier transform}

A direct DFT takes $N^2$ complex multiplications. In 1965 James Cooley and John Tukey
published an algorithm that computes it in about $\frac{N}{2}\log_2N$ by recursively
splitting the sum into even- and odd-indexed samples. (Carl Friedrich Gauss had used the
same trick in 1805 to interpolate the orbits of asteroids, but the idea went unnoticed.)
For $N=4096$, the FFT is about 700 times faster. The FFT made digital spectrum analysis
routine, and forty years later it made OFDM practical: a Wi-Fi~7 receiver computes a
4096-point FFT every 12.8\,$\mu$s (Chapter~\ref{ch:ofdm}).

\begin{inpractice}[Swept and FFT spectrum analyzers]
A classic swept spectrum analyzer is a superheterodyne receiver whose local oscillator
sweeps across the band; its \emph{resolution bandwidth} (RBW) is the width of its IF filter,
and the sweep must be slow enough for that filter to settle, roughly
$\text{sweep time}\approx k\cdot\text{span}/\text{RBW}^2$. A real-time or FFT analyzer instead
digitises a band and computes overlapping windowed FFTs, capturing every event in the band.
The displayed noise floor rises by $10\log_{10}$ of the RBW: halving RBW lowers the noise floor
by 3\,dB and reveals weaker signals, at the cost of time. GNU Radio's \texttt{QT GUI
Frequency Sink} in the gr01 hardware example is an FFT analyzer, and its FFT size sets the
RBW.
\end{inpractice}

\section{Bandpass signals and the complex envelope}
\label{sec:complexenvelope}

Radio signals occupy a narrow band around a carrier frequency $f_c$ that is much larger
than their bandwidth: a 20\,MHz Wi-Fi channel at 5.5\,GHz, a 200\,kHz FM station at
100\,MHz. Such \term{bandpass signals} can be described completely by a low-frequency
complex signal, the \term{complex envelope} or \emph{baseband equivalent}.

\subsection{The analytic signal and the Hilbert transform}

Because a real signal's spectrum has Hermitian symmetry, its negative-frequency half is
redundant. The \term{analytic signal} removes it and doubles the positive half:
\begin{equation}
x_+(t)=x(t)+j\hat{x}(t),\qquad X_+(f)=2X(f)u(f),
\end{equation}
where $u(f)$ is the unit step and $\hat{x}(t)$ is the \term{Hilbert transform} of $x(t)$,
obtained by a filter with $H(f)=-j\,\mathrm{sgn}(f)$ that shifts every positive frequency by
$-90^\circ$. The Hilbert transform of $\cos$ is $\sin$. Shifting the analytic signal down by
$f_c$ gives the complex envelope,
\begin{equation}
\tilde{x}(t)=x_+(t)\,e^{-j2\pi f_c t}=x_I(t)+j\,x_Q(t),\qquad
x(t)=\re\{\tilde{x}(t)\,e^{j2\pi f_c t}\}.
\label{eq:ch02:envelope}
\end{equation}
Figure~\ref{fig:ch02_analytic} shows the three spectra. The complex envelope is centred on
zero frequency and has the same bandwidth as the original signal's positive half, so it can
be sampled at a rate set by the signal bandwidth rather than the carrier frequency.
Expanding the real part in~\eqref{eq:ch02:envelope} gives the I/Q form every radio engineer
knows,
\begin{equation}
x(t)=x_I(t)\cos(2\pi f_c t)-x_Q(t)\sin(2\pi f_c t).
\end{equation}
The in-phase component $x_I$ modulates a cosine carrier and the quadrature component $x_Q$
modulates a sine carrier; because the two carriers are orthogonal, the receiver can
separate them.

\mdcfig{ch02_analytic}{From a real bandpass spectrum (left) to the analytic signal (centre)
to the complex envelope (right). The complex envelope need not be symmetric about zero.}

\subsection{Baseband-equivalent systems}

If a bandpass signal passes through a bandpass system with impulse response
$h(t)=\re\{2\tilde{h}(t)e^{j2\pi f_c t}\}$, the complex envelope of the output is simply
$\tilde{y}(t)=\tilde{h}(t)*\tilde{x}(t)$. Every bandpass calculation can therefore be done at
baseband, with complex numbers and a sample rate set by the bandwidth. One consequence
deserves emphasis: a pure delay $\tau$ in the passband becomes, at baseband, a delay $\tau$
\emph{and} a phase rotation $e^{-j2\pi f_c\tau}$. At a carrier of 2.4\,GHz, a path-length
change of 12.5\,cm rotates the phase by a full cycle. That single fact explains carrier
recovery, fading and beamforming, the subjects of Chapters~\ref{ch:sync},
\ref{ch:channels} and~\ref{ch:mimo}.

\begin{keyidea}[Why receivers produce I and Q]
A quadrature downconverter multiplies the received signal by $\cos(2\pi f_c t)$ and by
$-\sin(2\pi f_c t)$ and low-pass filters both products. The two outputs are exactly
$x_I(t)$ and $x_Q(t)$. The B200's AD9364 transceiver does this in analog circuitry
before its ADCs; Chapter~\ref{ch:sdr} examines what happens when the two paths are not
perfectly matched.
\end{keyidea}
