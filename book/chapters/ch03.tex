\chapter{Random Signals and Noise}
\label{ch:noise}

\begin{chapterintro}
If there were no noise, a single sample of a voltage could carry an infinite number of
bits, and communication engineering would be trivial. Noise is what makes the subject
hard and interesting. This chapter builds, from the ground up, the probabilistic and
physical understanding of noise that every later chapter leans on. It starts with a
working engineer's refresher on probability---random variables, moments, transformations,
Bayes' rule---and then studies the Gaussian distribution in depth: why it appears
everywhere, why it is the ``worst'' noise, and how to compute and bound its tail, the
Q~function that sits inside nearly every error-rate formula in this book. From complex
Gaussian noise it derives the Rayleigh, Rice, exponential and chi-square laws that
reappear as fading models. It then turns to random processes---stationarity, ergodicity,
autocorrelation, the power spectral density and the Wiener--Khinchin theorem---and to
the noise that matters in radios: filtered noise, bandpass noise and its I/Q
representation, and the cyclostationary structure that distinguishes a signal from
noise. The middle of the chapter is physics: thermal, shot, flicker, impulsive, atmospheric
and cosmic noise, from Johnson and Nyquist's 1928 papers to the cosmic microwave
background. The last third is engineering: noise figure and noise temperature, their
measurement, the Friis cascade formula, receiver sensitivity, the bookkeeping of SNR,
$E_b/N_0$ and $C/N_0$, the detection and estimation of signals in noise, and the link
budget---the single calculation that decides whether a communication system will work at
all, from a Wi-Fi access point to a spacecraft at the edge of the solar system.
\end{chapterintro}

\section{The floor under everything}
\label{sec:ch03:floor}
%======================================================================

In 1928 John B.~Johnson, measuring amplifiers at Bell Labs, found that every resistor
produced a small fluctuating voltage whose power was proportional to its temperature and
to the measurement bandwidth, and independent of what the resistor was made of. Harry
Nyquist explained the result the same year from thermodynamics. Thermal noise is not a
defect of any particular device; it is the random motion of charge carriers in any
conductor above absolute zero, and it sets a floor below which no receiver can hear. Every
other impairment---distortion, interference, fading---can in principle be designed away.
Noise can only be outwitted: by using more power, more bandwidth, more antennas, longer
observation, or cleverer codes.

It is worth pausing on how small that floor is, and how close to it real systems work. A
mobile phone at the edge of a cell decodes a signal of around $10^{-13}$\,W; a GPS receiver
tracks satellites whose signals arrive at about $10^{-16}$\,W, well below the thermal
noise in their bandwidth; the Deep Space Network hears Voyager~1 at around
$10^{-19}$\,W. Each of these feats is a negotiation with noise, and each was designed with
the tools of this chapter. Shannon's capacity formula (Chapter~\ref{ch:infotheory}),
$C=B\log_2(1+\mathrm{SNR})$, says the same thing in one line: the signal-to-noise ratio, not
the signal power, is what limits communication.

To reason about noise we need two languages. The first is \emph{probability}: noise is
random, so we describe it by distributions, averages and correlations, and we design
receivers that make the best decision on average. The second is \emph{physics}: noise has
causes, and knowing them tells us how large it is, how it depends on temperature,
frequency and current, and how to minimise it. The chapter alternates between the two and
ends where they meet, in the noise figure, the sensitivity specification and the link
budget. Table~\ref{tab:ch03:roadmap} is a map.

\section{Probability for communication engineers}
\label{sec:ch03:prob}
%======================================================================

This section is a refresher, not a first course. It collects the results we use
repeatedly, each illustrated with an example from a real receiver. Readers who know their
probability well can skim it; readers who learned it once and forgot it should not, because
the rest of the book takes it for granted.

\subsection{Random variables, distributions and densities}

A \term{random variable} $X$ is a number whose value is determined by the outcome of a
random experiment: the voltage on a resistor at an instant, the number of bit errors in a
packet, the phase of a received carrier. It is described by its \term{cumulative
distribution function} (CDF)
\begin{equation}
F_X(x)=P(X\le x),
\end{equation}
which rises monotonically from 0 to 1. For a continuous variable the derivative
$p_X(x)=dF_X/dx$ is the \term{probability density function} (pdf), so that
$P(a<X\le b)=\int_a^b p_X(x)\,dx$; for a discrete variable we use the probability mass
function $P(X=x_k)$. Mixed variables, with both a density and point masses, occur in
practice: the output of an amplifier that clips is continuous inside the rails and has a
point mass at each rail.

Four distributions do most of the work in this book. The \emph{Bernoulli} variable, 1 with
probability $p$ and 0 otherwise, models one bit error. The \emph{binomial}, the number of
errors in $n$ independent bits, has $P(K=k)=\binom nk p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$; it tells you, for
example, that a 1000-bit packet on a link with bit-error rate $10^{-4}$ survives with
probability $(1-10^{-4})^{1000}\approx0.905$. The \emph{uniform} distribution models a carrier
phase about which nothing is known and the error of a fine quantiser. The \emph{Gaussian}
models noise, and occupies most of this chapter.

\subsection{Moments: mean, power and variance}

The \term{expectation} of a function $g(X)$ is $\E[g(X)]=\int g(x)p_X(x)\,dx$. The mean
$\mu=\E[X]$, the mean square $\E[X^2]$ and the variance $\sigma^2=\E[(X-\mu)^2]=\E[X^2]-\mu^2$
have direct electrical meanings. If $X$ is a voltage across a 1\,$\Omega$ resistor, $\E[X^2]$ is
the average power, $\mu^2$ is the power of the DC component and $\sigma^2$ is the power of
the fluctuations---the AC power. The standard deviation $\sigma$ is the RMS value of the
fluctuation, which is what a true-RMS voltmeter with AC coupling reads.

Higher moments describe shape. The normalised third moment (skewness) measures asymmetry;
the normalised fourth moment, the \term{kurtosis} $\E[(X-\mu)^4]/\sigma^4$, equals 3 for a
Gaussian and is a quick test for impulsive noise: a kurtosis well above 3 means heavy tails
and occasional large spikes. The ratio of peak to RMS value, the \term{crest factor}, is
the amplitude statistic that sets how much headroom an amplifier or ADC needs; its square is
the peak-to-average power ratio (PAPR) that haunts OFDM transmitters (Chapter~\ref{ch:ofdm}).

\subsection{Characteristic and moment-generating functions}

The \term{characteristic function} $\Phi_X(\omega)=\E[e^{j\omega X}]$ is the Fourier transform
of the pdf (with the sign of the exponent reversed), and the \term{moment-generating
function} $M_X(s)=\E[e^{sX}]$ is its Laplace-transform cousin. They earn their place for
three reasons.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Moments by differentiation}: $\E[X^n]=M_X^{(n)}(0)$. For the Gaussian,
$M_X(s)=e^{\mu s+\sigma^2s^2/2}$, from which every moment follows.
\item \emph{Sums become products}: if $X$ and $Y$ are independent, the pdf of $Z=X+Y$ is the
convolution $p_Z=p_X*p_Y$, and therefore $\Phi_Z=\Phi_X\Phi_Y$. Adding noise sources,
interferers or multipath components is multiplication in this domain. The logarithm of the
characteristic function, whose Taylor coefficients are the \term{cumulants}, turns the
product into a sum: cumulants of independent variables add.
\item \emph{Tail bounds}: for any $s>0$, Markov's inequality applied to $e^{sX}$ gives the
\term{Chernoff bound}
\begin{equation}
P(X\ge x)\le e^{-sx}M_X(s),\qquad\text{optimised over }s>0.
\label{eq:ch03:chernoff}
\end{equation}
This single inequality underlies the error-exponent analyses of coding theory
(Chapter~\ref{ch:infotheory}) and the union--Chernoff bounds used to design codes for
fading channels. We apply it to the Gaussian tail in Section~\ref{sec:ch03:qfunc}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Functions of a random variable}

A receiver is a long chain of operations on random variables: squaring, taking magnitudes,
converting to decibels, quantising. If $Y=g(X)$ with $g$ differentiable, the pdf of $Y$ is
\begin{equation}
p_Y(y)=\sum_{k}\frac{p_X(x_k)}{|g'(x_k)|},\qquad\text{summed over the roots }x_k\text{ of }g(x_k)=y .
\label{eq:ch03:transform}
\end{equation}
The derivative accounts for the stretching or compression of the axis: probability mass is
conserved, so where $g$ is steep the density of $Y$ is thin. Three examples recur.

\paragraph{Square-law detector.} If $X\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$ and $Y=X^2$, the two roots
$\pm\sqrt y$ give $p_Y(y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2 y}}e^{-y/2\sigma^2}$, $y>0$: the
chi-square density with one degree of freedom. The output of a diode power detector driven
by noise has this distribution.

\paragraph{Sinusoid with random phase.} If $\Theta$ is uniform on $[0,2\pi)$ and
$Y=A\cos\Theta$, then $p_Y(y)=1/(\pi\sqrt{A^2-y^2})$ for $|y|<A$. This \emph{arcsine}
density, peaked at the extremes because a sinusoid spends most of its time near its peaks,
is the amplitude distribution of a tone sampled at random instants. It explains why a sine
wave is a poor test signal for an ADC that will digitise noise-like OFDM, and why
quantiser histograms of a sine input have horns at both ends.

\paragraph{Decibels.} Engineers look at power in decibels, $Y=10\log_{10}X$. If $X$ is
exponential with mean 1 (the power of complex Gaussian noise, below), $Y$ has a
\emph{log-exponential} density with a long left tail: deep fades are much more common than
deep peaks, and the mean of $Y$ is $-2.5$\,dB, not 0\,dB. Averaging powers in decibels
rather than in watts is a classic source of a 2.5\,dB bias in measured noise floors; the
spectrum analyzer's ``log-average'' detector carries a correction for exactly this reason.

\subsection{Joint distributions, correlation and independence}

Two random variables are described jointly by $p_{XY}(x,y)$; the \emph{marginal}
$p_X(x)=\int p_{XY}(x,y)\,dy$ forgets $Y$, and the \emph{conditional}
$p_{X|Y}(x|y)=p_{XY}(x,y)/p_Y(y)$ describes $X$ once $Y$ is known. They are \term{independent}
if $p_{XY}=p_Xp_Y$. Their \term{covariance} $\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)=\E[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)]$
measures linear association, normalised to the \emph{correlation coefficient}
$\rho=\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)/\sigma_X\sigma_Y\in[-1,1]$.

Independent variables are uncorrelated, but the converse is false---and the
counterexample lives inside every phase-modulated signal. Let $\Theta$ be uniform and
$I=\cos\Theta$, $Q=\sin\Theta$. Then $\E[IQ]=\tfrac12\E[\sin2\Theta]=0$: $I$ and $Q$ are
uncorrelated. Yet they are completely dependent, since $I^2+Q^2=1$; know one and the other
is fixed up to its sign. Uncorrelated means ``no \emph{linear} relation''; independence means
``no relation at all''. The distinction matters whenever a receiver applies a nonlinearity,
and it is one reason the Gaussian is so convenient: for \emph{jointly Gaussian} variables,
uncorrelated does imply independent.

For a random vector $\vect x$ the second-order description is the mean vector and the
\term{covariance matrix} $\vect C=\E[(\vect x-\bm{\mu})(\vect x-\bm{\mu})^{\mathsf H}]$, which is
Hermitian and positive semidefinite. A linear transformation $\vect y=\vect A\vect x$ has
covariance $\vect A\vect C\vect A^{\mathsf H}$. Choosing $\vect A=\vect C^{-1/2}$ makes the
covariance the identity: this is \term{whitening}, the operation behind the whitened
matched filter of Chapter~\ref{ch:baseband} and the noise-whitening steps of MIMO receivers
in Chapter~\ref{ch:mimo}.

\subsection{Bayes' rule and the logic of decisions}

Every receiver infers a cause (the transmitted symbol) from an effect (the received
sample). The tool is \term{Bayes' rule},
\begin{equation}
P(s\mid r)=\frac{p(r\mid s)\,P(s)}{p(r)},\qquad p(r)=\sum_{s'}p(r\mid s')P(s').
\label{eq:ch03:bayes}
\end{equation}
Here $P(s)$ is the \emph{prior} probability of symbol $s$, $p(r\mid s)$ is the
\emph{likelihood}---the channel model, usually Gaussian noise around the transmitted
point---and $P(s\mid r)$ is the \emph{posterior}. Choosing the symbol with the largest
posterior is the \term{maximum a posteriori} (MAP) rule, which minimises the probability of
error; with equal priors it reduces to the \term{maximum-likelihood} (ML) rule. For a binary
decision the natural statistic is the \term{log-likelihood ratio}
\begin{equation}
\Lambda(r)=\ln\frac{P(s=+1\mid r)}{P(s=-1\mid r)}=\ln\frac{p(r\mid+1)}{p(r\mid-1)}+\ln\frac{P(+1)}{P(-1)},
\label{eq:ch03:llr}
\end{equation}
whose sign is the decision and whose magnitude is the confidence. For $r=\pm A+n$ with
Gaussian $n$ of variance $\sigma^2$, the likelihood term is simply $2Ar/\sigma^2$: the LLR is a
scaled copy of the received sample. These ``soft'' values are the currency of every modern
decoder---Viterbi, turbo, LDPC and polar (Chapters~\ref{ch:classiccodes}
and~\ref{ch:moderncodes}).

\begin{worked}[Base rates and false alarms]
A Wi-Fi receiver listens for packet preambles. Suppose a correlator detects a real preamble
with probability $P_D=0.99$ and fires on noise alone with probability $P_{FA}=10^{-3}$ per
test, and suppose that in a quiet channel only one test in $10^4$ actually contains a
preamble. When the detector fires, what is the probability that a packet is really there?
By Bayes' rule,
\[
P(\text{packet}\mid\text{fire})=\frac{0.99\times10^{-4}}{0.99\times10^{-4}+10^{-3}\times(1-10^{-4})}\approx0.090 .
\]
Ninety-one per cent of the alarms are false, even though the detector looks excellent on
paper. The cure is either a lower $P_{FA}$ (a higher threshold, at the cost of some $P_D$)
or a second, independent check---which is exactly why packet detectors confirm a
correlation peak with a second preamble field before committing the receiver. The same
arithmetic governs medical tests, radar and intrusion detection. We return to the trade-off
between $P_D$ and $P_{FA}$ in Section~\ref{sec:ch03:detection}.
\end{worked}

\section{The Gaussian distribution}
\label{sec:ch03:gauss}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Definition and properties}

The Gaussian (normal) random variable $X\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ has density
\begin{equation}
p_X(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\,e^{-(x-\mu)^2/2\sigma^2}.
\label{eq:ch03:gauss}
\end{equation}
A Gaussian random vector $\vect x\in\R^n$ with mean $\bm{\mu}$ and covariance $\vect C$ has
\begin{equation}
p(\vect x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}\det(\vect C)^{1/2}}\exp\!\left(-\tfrac12(\vect x-\bm{\mu})^{\mathsf T}\vect C^{-1}(\vect x-\bm{\mu})\right).
\label{eq:ch03:mvgauss}
\end{equation}
Four properties make it the natural model and the convenient one.
\begin{enumerate}
\item It is completely specified by its mean and covariance---by second-order statistics,
which are exactly what we can measure and propagate through linear systems.
\item Any linear combination of jointly Gaussian variables is Gaussian. Noise that passes
through filters, mixers (which are linear in the signal path), FFTs and matched filters stays
Gaussian; only its covariance changes.
\item Uncorrelated jointly Gaussian variables are independent, because the covariance matrix
in~\eqref{eq:ch03:mvgauss} becomes diagonal and the density factorises.
\item The ML decision in Gaussian noise is a minimum-\emph{distance} decision: maximising
\eqref{eq:ch03:mvgauss} over $\bm{\mu}$ means minimising $\|\vect x-\bm{\mu}\|^2$ (for white
noise). Signal-space geometry (Chapter~\ref{ch:modulation}) is Gaussian noise made visible.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Why the Gaussian is everywhere: the central limit theorem}

The \term{central limit theorem} (CLT) says that the normalised sum of $n$ independent,
identically distributed variables with finite variance tends to a Gaussian, whatever the
individual distribution:
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\mu)\ \xrightarrow{\ n\to\infty\ }\ \mathcal N(0,1).
\end{equation}
The proof is a one-liner with characteristic functions. The characteristic function of each
normalised term is $1-\omega^2/2n+o(1/n)$, so that of the sum is
$(1-\omega^2/2n+\dots)^n\to e^{-\omega^2/2}$, the Gaussian's. Independence turned the
convolution of $n$ densities into a product, and the product forgot everything but the
variance. Versions of the theorem (Lindeberg's, Lyapunov's) relax ``identically
distributed'' to ``no single term dominates''.

Thermal noise is the sum of the effects of an astronomical number of electrons; the
interference from many distant transmitters, the error of a quantiser with many levels, the
sum of many multipath components and the samples of an OFDM signal built from hundreds of
subcarriers all tend to Gaussian for the same reason. Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_clt} shows how
quickly the convergence happens near the centre: the sum of just four uniform random
variables is hard to tell from a Gaussian by eye.

\mdcfig{ch03_clt}{The central limit theorem in action: normalised sums of $n$ independent
uniform random variables (histograms) approach the Gaussian density (red curves).}

The tails are another matter, and the tails are where error probabilities live.
Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_clt_tails} plots the exact probability that the normalised sum exceeds
$x$ standard deviations. The sum of $n$ uniforms is bounded---it can never exceed
$\sqrt{3n}$ standard deviations---so its tail falls off a cliff where the Gaussian's
continues. Twelve uniforms (the old trick for generating Gaussian samples) match the
Gaussian to about $10^{-3}$, then fall far below it. Sums of coin flips converge more evenly
but in steps. The quantitative statement is the \emph{Berry--Esseen theorem}: the CDF error is
at most a constant times $\E|X-\mu|^3/(\sigma^3\sqrt n)$, an \emph{absolute} error. At a tail
probability of $10^{-9}$ an absolute error of $10^{-3}$ is meaningless.

\mdcfig{ch03_clt_tails}{Convergence of the CLT in the tails. Left: exact tail probabilities
of normalised sums of $n$ uniform variables, which are bounded and therefore fall away from
the Gaussian $Q(x)$ beyond a few standard deviations. Right: sums of $n$ random $\pm1$
values (binomial), which approach $Q(x)$ in steps. The centre of a distribution becomes
Gaussian quickly; the far tail, where bit errors live, does not.}

\begin{pitfall}[The CLT does not reach the tails]
The Gaussian model is excellent for thermal noise, whose ``number of terms'' is of order
$10^{20}$. It can be badly wrong for impairments built from a few terms: interference from
two or three strong neighbouring cells, the ISI from a few residual taps, the sum of a
handful of crosstalk aggressors in a SerDes link. Such sums have \emph{bounded} support,
and a Gaussian model then \emph{overestimates} the error rate---the reason high-speed link
engineers compute ``statistical eyes'' by exact convolution of the ISI distribution rather
than by a Gaussian approximation. Impulsive noise, conversely, has heavier tails than the
Gaussian and is \emph{underestimated} by it (Section~\ref{sec:ch03:impulsive}). Whenever an error
rate below $10^{-6}$ is predicted from a Gaussian assumption, ask how many terms built the
noise.
\end{pitfall}

\subsection{Why the Gaussian is the worst noise: maximum entropy}

The differential entropy $h(X)=-\int p\log_2p\,dx$ measures, in bits, the uncertainty of a
continuous variable (Chapter~\ref{ch:infotheory}). Among all densities with a given variance
$\sigma^2$, the Gaussian has the largest entropy,
\begin{equation}
h(X)\le\tfrac12\log_2\!\left(2\pi e\sigma^2\right),
\label{eq:ch03:maxent}
\end{equation}
with equality only for the Gaussian. The proof takes two lines. Let $g$ be the Gaussian
density with the same variance as $p$. Because $\ln g$ is a quadratic in $x$, $-\int p\ln g$
depends only on the variance, and so equals $-\int g\ln g=h_g$ (in nats). Then
$h_g-h_p=\int p\ln(p/g)\ge0$, the non-negativity of relative entropy.

Maximum entropy has two consequences that frame the whole of information theory. First,
among all noises of a given power, Gaussian noise is the \emph{least predictable} and
therefore the most harmful: the capacity of an additive-noise channel with noise power $N$ is
at least $B\log_2(1+S/N)$, with equality only for Gaussian noise. Designing for Gaussian noise
is designing for the worst case. Second, among all \emph{inputs} of a given power, the Gaussian
input maximises the mutual information---which is why capacity-approaching systems shape their
constellations to look Gaussian (probabilistic shaping, Chapter~\ref{ch:infotheory}).

\section{The Q function}
\label{sec:ch03:qfunc}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Definition and values}

Nearly every error probability in this book is a Gaussian tail probability. We define the
\term{Q function} as the probability that a zero-mean, unit-variance Gaussian exceeds $x$,
\begin{equation}
\Q(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_x^\infty e^{-u^2/2}\,du=\tfrac12\operatorname{erfc}\!\left(x/\sqrt2\right).
\label{eq:ch03:q}
\end{equation}
The tail of a general Gaussian is obtained by standardising:
$P(X>a)=\Q\big((a-\mu)/\sigma\big)$. If a receiver decides between $+A$ and $-A$ corrupted by
Gaussian noise of standard deviation $\sigma$, the error probability is $\Q(A/\sigma)$. Useful
identities are $\Q(-x)=1-\Q(x)$, $\Q(0)=\tfrac12$ and $\frac{d}{dx}\Q(x)=-\phi(x)$, where
$\phi(x)=e^{-x^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}$ is the standard Gaussian density. In software, use
\texttt{scipy.special.erfc} (or \texttt{scipy.stats.norm.sf}); never integrate the density
numerically in the tail, and never compute $1-\Phi(x)$ for large $x$, which loses every
significant digit to cancellation.

The function falls extremely fast (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_q} and
Table~\ref{tab:ch03:q}): $\Q(3.09)=10^{-3}$, $\Q(4.75)=10^{-6}$, $\Q(6.0)=10^{-9}$.
Increasing the argument by about 50\%---a 3.5\,dB increase in signal-to-noise
ratio---reduces the error rate by a factor of a thousand. This steepness is why a working
digital link has a ``cliff'': a few decibels separate perfect reception from none. It is also
why the reach of a link is so insensitive to the error-rate target: going from $10^{-6}$ to
$10^{-9}$ costs only $20\log_{10}(6.0/4.75)\approx2$\,dB.

\subsection{Bounds}

Three bounds cover most needs.

\paragraph{The Chernoff bound.} Applying~\eqref{eq:ch03:chernoff} to a unit Gaussian, with
$M(s)=e^{s^2/2}$, gives $\Q(x)\le e^{-sx+s^2/2}$, minimised at $s=x$:
\begin{equation}
\Q(x)\le e^{-x^2/2},\qquad x\ge0.
\end{equation}
It captures the exponent exactly but is loose by a factor that grows with $x$.

\paragraph{Craig's formula and the factor of one half.} In 1991 John Craig found an
alternative \emph{exact} expression with finite limits and the argument in the integrand,
not the limit:
\begin{equation}
\Q(x)=\frac1\pi\int_0^{\pi/2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{x^2}{2\sin^2\theta}\right)d\theta,\qquad x\ge0.
\label{eq:ch03:craig}
\end{equation}
The derivation is pleasing. $\Q(x)$ is the probability that a 2-D standard Gaussian vector
lies in the half-plane $\{u_1>x\}$. In polar coordinates the boundary is the line
$r\cos\varphi=x$, and integrating the radial part $\int_{x/\cos\varphi}^\infty re^{-r^2/2}dr
=e^{-x^2/2\cos^2\varphi}$ over $|\varphi|<\pi/2$ (with the $1/2\pi$ normalisation) gives
\eqref{eq:ch03:craig} after the substitution $\theta=\pi/2-\varphi$. Bounding the integrand by its
value at $\theta=\pi/2$ gives the tighter
$\Q(x)\le\tfrac12e^{-x^2/2}$. More importantly, because $x^2$ now appears in an exponent,
averaging $\Q(\sqrt{2\gamma})$ over a fading distribution of the SNR $\gamma$ turns into an
integral of the moment-generating function of $\gamma$ over a finite range---the basis of the
moment-generating-function method for error rates in fading (Chapter~\ref{ch:channels}).

\paragraph{The density bounds.} Integrating by parts gives
\begin{equation}
\frac{x}{1+x^2}\,\phi(x)<\Q(x)<\frac{\phi(x)}{x},\qquad x>0,
\label{eq:ch03:qdensitybounds}
\end{equation}
which sandwich $\Q(x)$ to within 10\% for $x>3$ and to within 3\% for $x>6$. The upper bound
$\phi(x)/x$ is also the leading term of the asymptotic expansion
$\Q(x)\sim\frac{\phi(x)}{x}\big(1-\frac1{x^2}+\frac{3}{x^4}-\cdots\big)$, which diverges for every
fixed $x$ if continued indefinitely but is excellent when truncated early at large $x$.

\subsection{Approximations}

When a closed form is needed---in a link-budget spreadsheet, a fixed-point receiver or an
analysis---several approximations are available (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_qapprox}). The
B\"orjesson--Sundberg approximation (1979),
\begin{equation}
\Q(x)\approx\frac{\phi(x)}{(1-a)x+a\sqrt{x^2+b}},\qquad a=0.339,\ b=5.510,
\end{equation}
is accurate to about 0.3\% for all $x\ge0$. The two-exponential approximation of Chiani,
Dardari and Simon (2003), $\Q(x)\approx\frac1{12}e^{-x^2/2}+\frac14e^{-2x^2/3}$, is much less
accurate but has the enormous advantage of being a sum of exponentials in $x^2$, so that
averages over fading reduce to sums of moment-generating functions in closed form.

\mdcfig{ch03_qapprox}{How good are the bounds and approximations? Left: ratio of four bounds
to $\Q(x)$, in decibels. The density bounds pinch together as $x$ grows; the Chernoff-type
bounds have the right exponent but a growing prefactor error. Right: relative error of three
approximations. B\"orjesson--Sundberg is indistinguishable from zero on this scale.}

\begin{keyidea}[Think in decibels of argument]
Between error rates of $10^{-3}$ and $10^{-9}$, $\Q(x)$ falls by one decade for every 1.6\,dB
(near $10^{-3}$) down to 0.6\,dB (near $10^{-9}$) increase of $x^2$---that is, of SNR. A
receiver that is ``2\,dB worse'' is therefore between one and three decades worse in error
rate, and an ``implementation loss'' of half a decibel is far from negligible. Conversely, error-rate requirements should be
argued in decibels of margin, not in factors of ten of probability.
\end{keyidea}

\begin{worked}[How much SNR does $10^{-12}$ cost?]
A 25\,Gb/s optical link with binary signalling needs a bit-error rate of $10^{-12}$ without
FEC. Table~\ref{tab:ch03:q} gives $\Q^{-1}(10^{-12})=7.03$, so the ratio of eye half-opening to
RMS noise must be at least 7.03, an SNR of $20\log_{10}7.03\approx16.9$\,dB at the decision
point. At $10^{-6}$ the requirement is $20\log_{10}4.75\approx13.5$\,dB. Six orders of
magnitude cost 3.4\,dB. Alternatively, add a forward-error-correction code that turns an
input BER of $2\times10^{-4}$ (KP4 Reed--Solomon in 100G and 400G Ethernet,
Chapter~\ref{ch:classiccodes}) into an output below $10^{-13}$: the link now needs only
$\Q^{-1}(2\times10^{-4})\approx3.54$, an SNR of 11\,dB---nearly 6\,dB saved, which is why every
modern high-speed link has FEC.
\end{worked}

\section{Complex Gaussian noise and its family}
\label{sec:ch03:complex}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Circular symmetry}

Because receivers work with complex baseband signals (Section~\ref{sec:complexenvelope}),
noise is complex: $n=n_I+jn_Q$. When the two parts are independent, zero-mean Gaussian and of
equal variance $\sigma^2$, the noise is \term{circularly symmetric complex Gaussian}, written
$n\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,2\sigma^2)$, with density
\begin{equation}
p(n)=\frac{1}{\pi N}\,e^{-|n|^2/N},\qquad N=\E|n|^2=2\sigma^2 .
\label{eq:ch03:cn}
\end{equation}
Its distribution depends only on $|n|$, so it does not change under rotation: $e^{j\theta}n$ has
the same distribution as $n$ for every $\theta$. Two consequences follow immediately. A
receiver's carrier phase offset does not change the noise statistics, only the signal's; and
the phase of the noise is uniformly distributed on $[-\pi,\pi)$. Formally, circular symmetry
requires the \emph{pseudo-covariance} $\E[n^2]=\E[n_I^2]-\E[n_Q^2]+2j\E[n_In_Q]$ to vanish; a
receiver with IQ gain or phase imbalance produces noise whose pseudo-covariance is not zero,
which is one way to detect and correct the imbalance (Chapter~\ref{ch:sdr}).

\subsection{Rayleigh, exponential and uniform phase}

The magnitude $R=|n|$ has the \term{Rayleigh distribution}
\begin{equation}
p_R(r)=\frac{r}{\sigma^2}\,e^{-r^2/2\sigma^2},\qquad r\ge0,
\label{eq:ch03:rayleigh}
\end{equation}
obtained by transforming the joint Gaussian density of $(n_I,n_Q)$ to polar coordinates: the
Jacobian $r$ creates the factor $r$, and the density does not depend on the angle, which is
therefore uniform and independent of $R$. The Rayleigh mean is $\sigma\sqrt{\pi/2}$, its mode is
$\sigma$, and its mean square is $2\sigma^2$. The power $P=|n|^2$ is \term{exponentially
distributed},
\begin{equation}
p_P(x)=\frac{1}{\bar P}\,e^{-x/\bar P},\qquad P(P<x)=1-e^{-x/\bar P},\qquad \bar P=2\sigma^2 .
\label{eq:ch03:expo}
\end{equation}
The CDF answers the most important practical question about Rayleigh fading
(Chapter~\ref{ch:channels}): the probability of a fade $10$\,dB below the mean is
$1-e^{-0.1}\approx9.5\%$, $20$\,dB below is about $1\%$ and $30$\,dB below about $0.1\%$---one
decade of probability per 10\,dB of fade depth.

\subsection{Chi-square: sums of squared Gaussians}

The sum of squares of $k$ independent standard Gaussians is \term{chi-square} with $k$ degrees of
freedom,
\begin{equation}
p_Y(y)=\frac{y^{k/2-1}e^{-y/2}}{2^{k/2}\Gamma(k/2)},\qquad y\ge0,
\label{eq:ch03:chisq}
\end{equation}
with mean $k$ and variance $2k$. The square-law detector output is $k=1$; the power of one
complex Gaussian sample is $k=2$ (the exponential); the energy collected by an energy detector
from $N$ complex samples, or the combined power of $L$ diversity branches, is $k=2N$ or $2L$.
Adding nonzero means gives the \emph{noncentral} chi-square, whose noncentrality parameter is
the sum of the squared means---the energy of a signal hidden in the noise. Its tail is the
generalised Marcum Q function, which we meet in noncoherent detection
(Section~\ref{sec:ch03:detection}).

The right panel of Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_chisq} shows the most important application. The
probability that the normalised power of $L$ combined independent complex Gaussians falls
below a small value $x$ behaves like $x^L$: a slope of one decade per 10\,dB for $L=1$, one
decade per 5\,dB for $L=2$, and so on. That slope is the \term{diversity order}, and it is the
reason two receive antennas improve a fading link far more than 3\,dB of extra power would
(Chapters~\ref{ch:channels} and~\ref{ch:mimo}).

\mdcfig{ch03_chisq}{Left: chi-square densities. Right: CDF of the normalised power of the
sum of $L$ independent complex Gaussians (chi-square with $2L$ degrees of freedom). Deep
fades become rarer by one decade per $10/L$\,dB---the diversity effect.}

\subsection{The Rice distribution}

If a constant component $s$ is added---a sinusoid plus noise, or a line-of-sight path plus
scattered paths---the envelope follows the \term{Rice distribution},
\begin{equation}
p_R(r)=\frac{r}{\sigma^2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{r^2+s^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)I_0\!\left(\frac{rs}{\sigma^2}\right),
\label{eq:ch03:rice}
\end{equation}
where $I_0$ is the modified Bessel function of order zero. It is characterised by the
\emph{K-factor} $K=s^2/2\sigma^2$, the ratio of the steady power to the random power
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_envelopes}). For $K=0$ it is Rayleigh; for large $K$ it approaches a
Gaussian of mean $s$ and variance $\sigma^2$, because only the noise component in phase with
the constant changes the envelope to first order. Stephen Rice derived these distributions in
his 1944--45 \emph{Bell System Technical Journal} papers to describe a sinusoid in
noise; they returned as the models of multipath fading in Chapter~\ref{ch:channels}. When the
envelope is not well described by either, the two-parameter \emph{Nakagami-$m$} distribution,
whose power is gamma-distributed, is the usual empirical fit.

\mdcfig{ch03_envelopes}{Left: envelope distributions of a constant plus circular Gaussian
noise, for three Rice K-factors (histograms from simulation, curves from theory).
Right: the phase of circularly symmetric Gaussian noise is uniformly distributed.}

\paragraph{The log-normal.} One more distribution completes the family. A signal that passes
through many independent attenuating obstacles has a power that is a \emph{product} of random
factors; its logarithm is a sum, and by the CLT is Gaussian. A power whose value in decibels
is Gaussian is \term{log-normal}. This is the standard model of \emph{shadowing}, with a
standard deviation of 4--12\,dB in mobile radio (Chapter~\ref{ch:channels}).

\section{Random processes}
\label{sec:ch03:processes}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Ensembles and sample paths}

A \term{random process} $x(t)$ is a random variable at every instant---equivalently, a
random choice of an entire waveform from an \emph{ensemble} of possible waveforms. The
oscilloscope trace of the noise at a receiver output is one \emph{sample path}; switch the
receiver off and on again and you see a different one, drawn from the same ensemble. A
complete description would require the joint distribution of $x(t_1),\dots,x(t_n)$ for
every $n$ and every choice of times. Fortunately, for the Gaussian processes that model noise,
the mean $\mu_x(t)=\E[x(t)]$ and the \term{autocorrelation function}
\begin{equation}
R_x(t_1,t_2)=\E[x(t_1)\,x^*(t_2)]
\end{equation}
suffice: they fix every joint distribution. (The conjugate makes the definition work for
complex baseband processes; for real ones it does nothing.)

\subsection{Stationarity}

A process is \emph{strict-sense stationary} if its statistics do not change with time
origin. The weaker and far more useful condition is \term{wide-sense stationarity} (WSS):
the mean is constant and the autocorrelation depends only on the time difference
$\tau=t_1-t_2$, so that we can write $R_x(\tau)=\E[x(t+\tau)x^*(t)]$. For Gaussian processes
the two notions coincide. The autocorrelation of a WSS process has three properties worth
memorising: $R_x(0)=\E|x|^2$ is the average power; $|R_x(\tau)|\le R_x(0)$, so nothing is more
similar to the process than itself; and $R_x(-\tau)=R_x^*(\tau)$.

The autocorrelation measures how quickly a process ``forgets'' its past. The time over which
$R_x(\tau)$ stays near $R_x(0)$---its \emph{correlation time}---is the time over which you can
predict the process from its present value. Thermal noise forgets in picoseconds; the fading
gain of a pedestrian's mobile channel remembers for milliseconds (the coherence time of
Chapter~\ref{ch:channels}); the phase noise of a crystal oscillator wanders over seconds.

\subsection{Ergodicity: one record instead of an ensemble}

In the laboratory we never see an ensemble. We see one long record and compute time
averages. A process is \term{ergodic} (in the mean, or in the autocorrelation) if these
time averages converge to the ensemble averages:
\begin{equation}
\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac1T\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}x(t)\,dt=\mu_x,\qquad
\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac1T\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}x(t+\tau)x^*(t)\,dt=R_x(\tau).
\end{equation}
Ergodicity is what makes measurement possible: a spectrum analyzer, a power meter and the
noise-variance estimator in a modem all replace expectations by time averages and trust the
answer. A sufficient condition for ergodicity in the mean is that the autocovariance decays
to zero, so that distant parts of the record are effectively independent and the time
average behaves like an average of many independent samples.

The canonical counterexample is a process whose every sample path is a constant, chosen at
random: $x(t)=A$ with $A\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$. It is perfectly stationary, but the time average
of any one path is just $A$, never the ensemble mean 0. Physically, this is a DC offset that
differs from unit to unit: averaging longer on one receiver will never reveal the
distribution across the production line. The random-phase sinusoid
$x(t)=\cos(2\pi f_0t+\Theta)$ is, by contrast, ergodic in mean and autocorrelation---each
sample path visits every phase.

\subsection{The power spectral density and the Wiener--Khinchin theorem}
\label{sec:ch03:psd}

The \term{power spectral density} (PSD) $S_x(f)$ says how a process's power is spread over
frequency: the power in a narrow band $df$ is $S_x(f)\,df$. The natural definition is
operational---filter the process through an ideal narrow band-pass filter and measure the
output power---and the equivalent mathematical definition is through the truncated Fourier
transform $X_T(f)=\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}x(t)e^{-j2\pi ft}\,dt$ of a record of length $T$:
\begin{equation}
S_x(f)=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac1T\,\E\big[|X_T(f)|^2\big].
\label{eq:ch03:psddef}
\end{equation}
The \term{Wiener--Khinchin theorem} says that for a WSS process this limit is the Fourier
transform of the autocorrelation:
\begin{equation}
S_x(f)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}R_x(\tau)e^{-j2\pi f\tau}d\tau,\qquad \E|x(t)|^2=R_x(0)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} S_x(f)\,df.
\label{eq:ch03:wk}
\end{equation}
The derivation is short and illuminating. Expanding the squared magnitude,
\[
\frac1T\E|X_T(f)|^2=\frac1T\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}\!\!\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}R_x(t-s)\,e^{-j2\pi f(t-s)}\,dt\,ds
=\int_{-T}^{T}\Big(1-\frac{|\tau|}{T}\Big)R_x(\tau)e^{-j2\pi f\tau}\,d\tau ,
\]
where the second step changes variables to $\tau=t-s$ and counts how much of the square
$[-T/2,T/2]^2$ lies on each diagonal $t-s=\tau$: a length proportional to $T-|\tau|$. The
triangular weight $1-|\tau|/T$ tends to one as $T\to\infty$ (provided $R_x$ decays fast
enough), and the Fourier transform of $R_x$ remains. Two lessons hide in the derivation. First,
the PSD is a property of the \emph{ensemble}; for a single record, $|X_T(f)|^2/T$ (the
\term{periodogram}) does not converge as $T$ grows---its standard deviation stays equal to its
mean at every frequency. Spectral estimates must average: Welch's method splits a record into
overlapping windowed segments and averages their periodograms, trading frequency resolution
for variance, and a spectrum analyzer does the same with its resolution and video bandwidths.
Second, the finite record convolves the true spectrum with the transform of the triangle (or of
whatever window is used): resolution is limited to about $1/T$.

The PSD is real and non-negative, and for a real process it is even, $S_x(-f)=S_x(f)$.
Engineers then often quote the \emph{one-sided} PSD $2S_x(f)$ over $f>0$, so that the total power
is the integral over positive frequencies only. The PSD of a complex baseband process need
not be even: a signal can have more power above its centre frequency than below.

A process that changes quickly has a narrow autocorrelation and a wide spectrum, and vice
versa---the same time--bandwidth reciprocity as for deterministic signals
(Chapter~\ref{ch:signals}). A useful pair: a process with $R_x(\tau)=\sigma^2e^{-|\tau|/\tau_c}$
(the output of an RC filter driven by white noise, or the Ornstein--Uhlenbeck model of a
drifting oscillator frequency) has the Lorentzian spectrum
$S_x(f)=2\sigma^2\tau_c/(1+(2\pi f\tau_c)^2)$, whose 3\,dB bandwidth is $1/(2\pi\tau_c)$.

\begin{history}[Wiener, Khinchin and Kolmogorov]
Norbert Wiener, at MIT, developed his \emph{generalized harmonic analysis} in a long 1930
paper in \emph{Acta Mathematica}: a way to give a meaning to the spectrum of an irregular
signal that has no Fourier transform, through the transform of its autocorrelation.
Aleksandr Khinchin, in Moscow, proved the corresponding theorem for stationary random processes
in 1934, and Andrei Kolmogorov laid the axiomatic foundations of probability itself (1933) and
solved the problem of predicting a stationary sequence (1941). During the Second World War
Wiener applied the theory to anti-aircraft fire control, deriving the optimal linear filter for
separating a signal from noise; his classified report, nicknamed ``the yellow peril'' for its
yellow covers and its difficulty, was published in 1949 as \emph{Extrapolation, Interpolation,
and Smoothing of Stationary Time Series}. The Wiener filter (Section~\ref{sec:ch03:estimation}
and Chapter~\ref{ch:equalization}) and the Kalman filter that generalised it in 1960 descend
from this work, and so does the habit of thinking about noise in terms of spectra.
\end{history}

\subsection{Filtering random processes}

When a WSS process passes through an LTI filter with impulse response $h(t)$ and frequency
response $H(f)$, the output is WSS with autocorrelation
$R_y(\tau)=h(\tau)*h^*(-\tau)*R_x(\tau)$, and taking Fourier transforms,
\begin{equation}
S_y(f)=|H(f)|^2S_x(f).
\label{eq:ch03:filtpsd}
\end{equation}
This single equation carries most of the noise analysis in this book. It says, for example,
that noise power at a receiver output is the input noise PSD integrated over the
receiver's \emph{power} response, which is why the noise-equivalent bandwidth of
Section~\ref{sec:bandwidth} is the right bandwidth to use. The phase response of the filter
is irrelevant to the output spectrum; it affects only the waveform. The cross-correlation
between input and output, $R_{yx}(\tau)=h(\tau)*R_x(\tau)$, is equally useful: drive an unknown
system with white noise, and the input--output cross-correlation \emph{is} the impulse
response. Channel sounders and the least-mean-squares equalisers of
Chapter~\ref{ch:equalization} exploit exactly this.

\subsection{White noise}

Thermal noise has a flat PSD from DC to far beyond any radio frequency (up to about
6\,THz at room temperature, where quantum effects set in; Section~\ref{sec:ch03:thermal}). We
idealise it as \term{white noise} with two-sided PSD $N_0/2$\,W/Hz, whose autocorrelation is an
impulse: $R_n(\tau)=\frac{N_0}{2}\delta(\tau)$. Samples taken at any two distinct instants are
uncorrelated---and, being Gaussian, independent. White noise has infinite power and does not
exist, but once filtered it becomes perfectly well behaved: through a filter of noise
bandwidth $B$, the output power is $N_0B$ (counting both positive and negative frequencies of a
real filter). Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_filtered_noise} shows white noise and low-pass filtered
noise: filtering smooths the sample paths, narrows the spectrum and widens the autocorrelation.

\mdcfig{ch03_filtered_noise}{White Gaussian noise before and after a 50\,Hz low-pass filter:
sample paths, power spectral densities and autocorrelation functions.}

\paragraph{White noise in discrete time.} A simulation cannot generate continuous-time white
noise, and does not need to. Sampling at rate $f_s$ is preceded (implicitly, in a simulation;
physically, in a radio) by an anti-aliasing filter of bandwidth $f_s/2$. The filtered noise has
power $N_0f_s/2$, and its samples at spacing $1/f_s$ are uncorrelated, because the
autocorrelation of ideally low-pass filtered white noise is a sinc with zeros at multiples of
$1/f_s$. So \emph{discrete-time white noise} is a sequence of independent Gaussian samples of
variance $\sigma^2=N_0f_s/2$ (real) or $N_0f_s$ (complex baseband, in the convention of
Section~\ref{sec:ch03:bandpass}). The variance per sample grows with the sample rate while the
density $N_0$ stays fixed---a fact that causes more simulation bugs than any other, and to which
we return in Section~\ref{sec:ch03:snr}.

\subsection{Noise-equivalent bandwidth}

Real filters do not have rectangular responses, so ``noise in bandwidth $B$'' needs a
definition. The \term{noise-equivalent bandwidth} $B_N$ of a filter is the width of the
rectangular filter, with the same peak power gain, that passes the same noise power:
\begin{equation}
B_N=\frac{1}{|H(f_0)|^2}\int_0^\infty|H(f)|^2\,df
\label{eq:ch03:neb}
\end{equation}
for a real low-pass filter ($f_0=0$) or band-pass filter (peak at $f_0$). Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_neb}
compares $B_N$ with the 3\,dB bandwidth. A single-pole RC filter has
$B_N=\frac{\pi}{2}f_{3\,\mathrm{dB}}$, 57\% wider than its 3\,dB bandwidth, because its skirts fall
only at 6\,dB per octave and keep admitting noise far from the passband. An $n$-pole Butterworth
filter has $B_N/f_{3\,\mathrm{dB}}=(\pi/2n)/\sin(\pi/2n)$: 1.11 for two poles and 1.03 for four. A
spectrum analyzer's Gaussian-shaped resolution filter has $B_N\approx1.06\times$ its 3\,dB
bandwidth, which is why noise-marker readings include a correction of about 0.27\,dB in
addition to the log-averaging correction mentioned above. The most important example is the
matched filter: a root-raised-cosine receive filter for symbol rate $R_s$ has a noise bandwidth
of exactly $R_s$ at complex baseband, \emph{whatever its roll-off}, because $|H(f)|^2$ is a
raised-cosine spectrum, whose area equals that of the ideal Nyquist rectangle.

\mdcfig{ch03_neb}{Noise-equivalent bandwidth. Left: power responses of Butterworth filters with
the same 3\,dB bandwidth and the rectangles that pass the same noise power. Right: the ratio
$B_N/f_{3\,\mathrm{dB}}$ approaches one as the filter becomes sharper.}

\begin{worked}[The noise of a capacitor: $kT/C$]
A resistor $R$ at temperature $T$ in parallel with a capacitor $C$ forms a single-pole
low-pass filter for the resistor's noise. Section~\ref{sec:ch03:thermal} shows that the resistor
has a one-sided open-circuit noise-voltage PSD $4kTR$\,V$^2$/Hz. The filter has
$f_{3\,\mathrm{dB}}=1/(2\pi RC)$ and so $B_N=\frac\pi2f_{3\,\mathrm{dB}}=1/(4RC)$. The mean-square
voltage on the capacitor is therefore
\[
\overline{v^2}=4kTR\cdot\frac{1}{4RC}=\frac{kT}{C},
\]
independent of $R$: a larger resistor makes more noise but in a narrower band. For $C=1$\,pF at
300\,K the RMS voltage is $\sqrt{kT/C}\approx64\,\mu$V. This \term{$kT/C$ noise} is frozen onto
the sampling capacitor of every switched-capacitor ADC each time its switch opens, and it sets
the capacitor sizes of high-resolution converters: to keep the noise of a 2\,V full-scale,
14-bit converter below its quantisation noise ($\Delta/\sqrt{12}\approx35\,\mu$V), the sampling
capacitance must exceed roughly 3.4\,pF, and each extra bit quadruples it.
\end{worked}

\section{Bandpass noise}
\label{sec:ch03:bandpass}
%======================================================================

\subsection{The I/Q representation}

At the output of a radio's IF or channel filter, noise occupies a band of width $B$ around a
carrier $f_c$. Like any bandpass signal (Section~\ref{sec:complexenvelope}), it can be written
in terms of a complex envelope $\tilde n(t)=n_I(t)+jn_Q(t)$:
\begin{equation}
n(t)=\re\{\tilde n(t)e^{j2\pi f_ct}\}=n_I(t)\cos2\pi f_ct-n_Q(t)\sin2\pi f_ct .
\label{eq:ch03:bpnoise}
\end{equation}
The in-phase and quadrature components $n_I(t)$ and $n_Q(t)$ are what a quadrature
downconverter produces: multiply by $2\cos2\pi f_ct$ and $-2\sin2\pi f_ct$ and low-pass filter.
If $n(t)$ is a zero-mean WSS Gaussian process, then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $n_I$ and $n_Q$ are jointly Gaussian, zero-mean, low-pass processes with the same PSD,
\begin{equation}
S_{n_I}(f)=S_{n_Q}(f)=S_n(f-f_c)+S_n(f+f_c),\qquad |f|<B/2,
\end{equation}
obtained by sliding the positive- and negative-frequency halves of the bandpass spectrum to
zero and adding them.
\item Each has the same power as $n(t)$ itself: $\E[n_I^2]=\E[n_Q^2]=\E[n^2]$. (The cosine
and sine carriers each carry half of their component's power, and the two halves add up.)
\item Their cross-spectrum is proportional to the \emph{asymmetry} of $S_n(f)$ about $f_c$. If
the bandpass spectrum is symmetric about the carrier---the usual case for a filter centred on
$f_c$---$n_I$ and $n_Q$ are uncorrelated at every lag and hence, being Gaussian, independent.
\end{enumerate}
For white noise of two-sided PSD $N_0/2$ passed through an ideal band-pass filter of width $B$,
$n_I$ and $n_Q$ therefore have PSD $N_0$ over $|f|<B/2$ and power $N_0B$ each, and the complex
envelope $\tilde n$ has PSD $2N_0$ and power $\E|\tilde n|^2=2N_0B$. Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_bandpass}
shows all of this in a simulation: the bandpass noise looks like a carrier whose amplitude and
phase wander at a rate set by $B$, its I and Q components are slowly varying, and their scatter
plot is a circular cloud.

\mdcfig{ch03_bandpass}{Narrowband Gaussian noise. Top: the bandpass waveform $n(t)$ with its
envelope $|\tilde n(t)|$ and in-phase component $n_I(t)$; the noise looks like a carrier whose
amplitude and phase wander on a time scale of $1/B$. Bottom left: the passband PSD (two-sided)
and the PSDs of $n_I$ and of the complex envelope, each 3\,dB above the previous one---the
factors of two in action. Bottom right: decorrelated samples of $(n_I,n_Q)$ form a circular cloud
with negligible correlation.}

\begin{pitfall}[Factors of two]
Noise PSDs are quoted one-sided ($N_0$\,W/Hz over $f>0$) or two-sided ($N_0/2$ over all
$f$); with the envelope convention~\eqref{eq:ch03:bpnoise}, which this book uses, the complex
envelope has PSD $2N_0$ in the band---and the envelope of a signal of passband energy $E$ also has
energy $2E$, so the \emph{ratio} $E/N_0$ is unchanged. Many authors, and all the labs, rescale the
envelope by $1/\sqrt2$ to make energies match; then the complex noise has PSD $N_0$, with $N_0/2$
in each of I and Q, and $\E|n|^2=N_0$ per complex sample at unit sample rate. Both conventions
give the same error rates if used consistently. Most errors of exactly 3\,dB in simulations come
from mixing them. The labs use one convention throughout: unit-energy symbols and complex noise
of variance $N_0$ per symbol-spaced sample, so that $E_s/N_0$ is the ratio of symbol energy to
noise variance at the matched-filter output.
\end{pitfall}

\subsection{Envelope and phase}

Writing the complex envelope in polar form, $\tilde n=r(t)e^{j\psi(t)}$, gives the
\emph{envelope--phase} representation $n(t)=r(t)\cos(2\pi f_ct+\psi(t))$. At any instant
$(n_I,n_Q)$ is a circular Gaussian pair, so, from Section~\ref{sec:ch03:complex}, $r$ is Rayleigh,
$\psi$ is uniform, and the two are independent. An envelope detector (a diode and RC filter in an
AM radio, or $|\cdot|$ in software) driven by noise alone produces a Rayleigh-distributed output;
a squarer produces an exponential one.

Now add a carrier: $x(t)=A\cos2\pi f_ct+n(t)$. The complex envelope is $A+\tilde n$, a constant
plus circular Gaussian noise, so the envelope is Rice-distributed with $K=A^2/2\sigma^2$, where
$\sigma^2$ is the variance of each noise component---exactly the left panel of
Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_envelopes}. At high SNR, the envelope is approximately Gaussian with mean $A$,
and the phase error is approximately Gaussian with variance $\sigma^2/A^2=1/(2K)$: only the
quadrature noise component rotates the phasor. At low SNR, the noise dominates and the envelope
becomes Rayleigh; the signal is ``captured'' by the noise. This transition is the \emph{threshold
effect} of envelope-detected AM and of FM (Chapter~\ref{ch:analog}): above threshold, output SNR
is proportional to input SNR; below it, it collapses.

\subsection{Simulating bandpass noise at baseband}

Because bandpass noise is completely described by its complex envelope, simulations never need
to generate it at the carrier. The recipe used in every lab is:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Choose the complex sample rate $f_s$ (e.g.\ $f_s=N_{\text{sps}}R_s$ with $N_{\text{sps}}$
samples per symbol).
\item Generate independent complex samples $n_k=\sigma(u_k+jv_k)/\sqrt2$, with $u_k,v_k$
independent $\mathcal N(0,1)$, so that $\E|n_k|^2=\sigma^2$.
\item Set $\sigma^2=N_0f_s$ in the energy-matched convention of the pitfall above, with $N_0$
obtained from the required $E_s/N_0$ and the symbol energy.
\end{enumerate}
Section~\ref{sec:ch03:snr} turns this into a formula. Coloured or band-limited noise is obtained by
filtering this white sequence, and~\eqref{eq:ch03:filtpsd} predicts its spectrum.

\section{Cyclostationarity: the hidden periodicity of signals}
\label{sec:ch03:cyclo}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Why modulated signals are not stationary}

A random process is \term{cyclostationary} (in the wide sense) if its mean and autocorrelation
are periodic in time: $R_x(t+T_0+\tau,\,t+T_0)=R_x(t+\tau,\,t)$ for some period $T_0$. Noise is
stationary. Man-made signals almost never are. A PAM signal $s(t)=\sum_ka_kp(t-kT)$ has
statistics that repeat every symbol period: its variance is largest at the instants where pulses
peak and smallest in between. A signal on a carrier has statistics that repeat at the carrier
period. A TDMA frame repeats at the frame rate, an OFDM signal with a cyclic prefix at the OFDM
symbol rate, a spread-spectrum signal at the code period. Every one of these periodicities is a
fingerprint that noise does not share.

For spectral analysis the periodicity is usually averaged away. Averaging $R_x(t+\tau,t)$ over one
period gives a stationary autocorrelation whose Fourier transform is the familiar PSD; this is
exactly how the PSD of PAM, $S_s(f)=\frac{\sigma_a^2}{T}|P(f)|^2$ for uncorrelated zero-mean
symbols, is derived in Chapter~\ref{ch:baseband}. But averaging throws information away.

\subsection{Cyclic autocorrelation and spectral correlation}

Expand the periodic autocorrelation in a Fourier series in $t$:
\begin{equation}
R_x(t+\tau/2,\,t-\tau/2)=\sum_{\alpha}R_x^{\alpha}(\tau)\,e^{j2\pi\alpha t},\qquad
R_x^{\alpha}(\tau)=\big\langle x(t+\tau/2)x^*(t-\tau/2)e^{-j2\pi\alpha t}\big\rangle_t .
\label{eq:ch03:cyclicac}
\end{equation}
The coefficients $R_x^\alpha(\tau)$ are the \term{cyclic autocorrelation} functions, and the
frequencies $\alpha$ at which they are nonzero are the \term{cycle frequencies}: multiples of
$1/T_0$. The coefficient at $\alpha=0$ is the ordinary (time-averaged) autocorrelation. The
Fourier transform of $R_x^\alpha(\tau)$ in $\tau$ is the \term{spectral correlation function}
$S_x^\alpha(f)$, and it has a beautifully concrete meaning: it is the correlation between the
spectral components of $x$ at frequencies $f+\alpha/2$ and $f-\alpha/2$. For a stationary process
all distinct frequency components are uncorrelated, so $S_x^\alpha(f)=0$ for every $\alpha\ne0$.
For a cyclostationary process, components separated by a cycle frequency are correlated: they
carry the same information.

For PAM with uncorrelated symbols of variance $\sigma_a^2$, the result is
\begin{equation}
S_s^{\alpha}(f)=\frac{\sigma_a^2}{T}\,P\!\left(f+\tfrac\alpha2\right)P^*\!\left(f-\tfrac\alpha2\right),
\qquad \alpha=\frac kT .
\label{eq:ch03:pamscf}
\end{equation}
A feature at $\alpha=1/T$ exists only where the pulse spectrum and its copy shifted by $1/T$
overlap---that is, in the \emph{excess bandwidth} of the pulse. A pulse with zero roll-off
(an ideal sinc) has no symbol-rate cyclostationarity at all, and a timing-recovery circuit that
relies on it (a squarer followed by a narrow filter at $1/T$, Chapter~\ref{ch:sync}) has nothing to
lock to. The roll-off chosen in Chapter~\ref{ch:baseband} is therefore also a choice about how
easy the signal is to synchronise.

There is a second, \emph{conjugate} spectral correlation, built from $\E[x(t+\tau/2)x(t-\tau/2)]$
without the conjugate. It is zero for circular processes---complex Gaussian noise, QPSK,
16-QAM---but nonzero for signals with real-valued constellations such as BPSK and PAM, whose
conjugate features sit at $\alpha=2f_0+k/T$, where $f_0$ is the residual carrier offset. Squaring
a BPSK signal produces a spectral line at twice the carrier; that is the Costas loop's
secret (Chapter~\ref{ch:sync}), and the presence or absence of conjugate features is the first
test of an automatic modulation classifier.

\mdcfig{ch03_cyclo}{Cyclostationarity reveals a signal buried in noise. A BPSK signal
(RRC, $\beta=0.35$, 8 samples per symbol, carrier offset $f_0$) at an overall SNR of $-10$\,dB
barely raises the PSD (left). The spectral coherence, maximised over $f$ and plotted against
cycle frequency, shows sharp features at $\alpha=\pm R_s$ (non-conjugate, centre) and at
$\alpha=2f_0$ and $2f_0\pm R_s$ (conjugate, right). Noise alone (grey) shows only the
estimation floor.}

\subsection{Detecting and classifying signals}

Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_cyclo} shows why this matters. A BPSK signal 10\,dB below the noise in the
analysis band is invisible in a short PSD estimate and would be hard to detect with an energy
detector, especially if the noise level is not known precisely---an uncertainty of only 0.5\,dB in
the noise floor makes an energy detector useless below a certain SNR, the so-called \emph{SNR wall}.
The spectral correlation, by contrast, measures structure, not level: noise contributes nothing at
$\alpha\ne0$ however strong it is, so the features emerge from the estimation floor as the
observation grows. The locations of the features identify the symbol rate, the carrier offset and
whether the constellation is real or complex.

\begin{inpractice}[Cyclostationary processing at work]
Cyclostationary feature detection was proposed in the 1980s for signal interception and has
since been used for signal intelligence, spectrum monitoring and, more recently, spectrum
sensing in cognitive radio, where a secondary user must detect a licensed transmitter far below
the noise floor. Practical receivers use the idea constantly without naming it: the square-law and
Gardner timing detectors and the Costas and squaring carrier loops of Chapter~\ref{ch:sync} are
cycle-frequency estimators; the cyclic-prefix correlator that finds the OFDM symbol boundary in
Wi-Fi and LTE (Chapter~\ref{ch:ofdm}) detects the cyclostationarity the prefix creates; and
blind modulation recognition in spectrum-monitoring equipment begins by estimating the cycle
frequencies. The FFT accumulation method and the strip spectral correlation analyser make the
computation affordable for wideband searches.
\end{inpractice}

\begin{keyidea}[Noise is stationary; signals are not]
A stationary process has uncorrelated spectral components. Every man-made signal has
periodicities---symbol rate, carrier, frame, code---that correlate its spectral components at
specific separations. Measuring those correlations detects, synchronises and classifies signals
without knowing their content and without knowing the noise level. Much of synchronisation is
applied cyclostationarity.
\end{keyidea}

\section{Physical noise sources}
\label{sec:ch03:physical}
%======================================================================

The probabilistic models above say nothing about how \emph{much} noise there is. For that we
need physics. This section catalogues the mechanisms in rough order of importance to a radio
engineer, from the inescapable thermal floor to the noise of the universe itself.

\subsection{Thermal noise}
\label{sec:ch03:thermal}

Any conductor at absolute temperature $T$ contains charge carriers in random thermal motion.
The net current their motion produces averages to zero but fluctuates, and across a resistor $R$
it appears as an open-circuit noise voltage with one-sided PSD
\begin{equation}
\overline{v_n^2}=4kTR\ \ \mathrm{V^2/Hz},\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \overline{i_n^2}=\frac{4kT}{R}\ \ \mathrm{A^2/Hz},
\label{eq:ch03:johnson}
\end{equation}
the second form being the Norton equivalent. Connect the resistor to a matched load (another
resistor $R$) and it delivers the \term{available noise power}
\begin{equation}
P_n=kTB,\qquad k=1.380649\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J/K},
\label{eq:ch03:ktb}
\end{equation}
in a bandwidth $B$, independent of $R$ and of the material. At the reference temperature
$T_0=290$\,K, $kT_0=4.00\times10^{-21}$\,W/Hz, or $-174$\,dBm/Hz. This number deserves memorising,
together with its common scalings (Table~\ref{tab:ch03:ktb}).

\paragraph{Nyquist's derivation.} Nyquist's argument is a model of physical reasoning. Connect
two identical resistors $R$, both at temperature $T$, by a lossless transmission line of
characteristic impedance $R$ and length $\ell$. Each resistor launches noise power $P$ per unit
bandwidth into the line, and since the line is matched, all of it is absorbed by the other
resistor. In thermal equilibrium the exchange must balance (otherwise one resistor would heat the
other, violating the second law), so $P$ is the same for every resistor regardless of its
material. To find $P$, short both ends of the line suddenly, trapping the energy in flight. The
line now supports standing-wave modes at frequencies $f_m=mv/2\ell$, so there are $2\ell\,\Delta
f/v$ modes in a band $\Delta f$. By the equipartition theorem each mode holds energy $kT$ (half
electric, half magnetic). The trapped energy $kT\cdot2\ell\Delta f/v$ was travelling equally in
both directions and took $\ell/v$ to traverse the line, so the power each resistor was launching
was
\[
P=\frac{\tfrac12\,kT\cdot2\ell\Delta f/v}{\ell/v}=kT\Delta f .
\]
A source of open-circuit voltage $v$ and resistance $R$ delivers $v^2/4R$ to a matched load, so
$\overline{v^2}=4kTR\Delta f$. Nothing in the argument depends on the conduction mechanism: the
result is as general as thermodynamics.

\paragraph{The quantum correction.} Equipartition fails when the energy quantum $hf$ of a mode
becomes comparable with $kT$. Nyquist's paper already gave the correction: replace $kT$ by the
mean energy of a quantum oscillator,
\begin{equation}
kT\ \longrightarrow\ \frac{hf}{e^{hf/kT}-1},
\label{eq:ch03:planck}
\end{equation}
which is Planck's law for a single mode. Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_planck} shows the ratio. At 290\,K,
$hf=kT$ at $f\approx6$\,THz, so the classical formula holds to better than 3\% up to 300\,GHz. At
2.7\,K, the temperature of the cosmic background, the available noise power is already halved near 90\,GHz.
At optical frequencies ($hf/k\approx9000$\,K at 193\,THz) thermal noise vanishes entirely, and what
remains is the graininess of light itself: optical receivers are limited by shot noise and by the
quantum noise of amplifiers, not by $kT$. Quantum mechanics also sets a floor for amplifiers: a
linear amplifier that treats both quadratures equally must add noise equivalent to about half a
photon per mode at its input (Caves, 1982), a noise temperature of order $hf/k$---about 0.05\,K
at 1\,GHz, 5\,K at 100\,GHz, and the dominant limitation in the superconducting parametric amplifiers
now used for quantum computing readout.

\begin{history}[Johnson, Nyquist and the noise of a resistor, 1928]
John Bertrand Johnson, a Swedish-born physicist at Bell Telephone Laboratories, set out in 1926
to understand the irreducible hiss of high-gain vacuum-tube amplifiers. He found that the noise
at an amplifier's output depended on the resistance of the input circuit and its temperature, and
measured it carefully for resistors of carbon, metal films and electrolytes: the mean-square
voltage per unit bandwidth was proportional to $RT$, with the same constant for every material.
From his measurements he could even estimate Boltzmann's constant. He described the result to
the American Physical Society in 1927 and published it in the \emph{Physical Review} in July 1928
as ``Thermal agitation of electricity in conductors''. Harry Nyquist, his colleague, published the
theory in the same issue: ``Thermal agitation of electric charge in conductors'', four pages that
derived $4kTR\,\Delta f$ from the second law of thermodynamics and the transmission-line argument
given above, including the quantum correction. Thermal noise is still called
\emph{Johnson--Nyquist noise}, and Nyquist went on to the sampling and stability results that also
carry his name. Few pairs of papers have been cited by so many engineers who have never read them.
\end{history}

\begin{worked}[How big is thermal noise?]
A 50\,$\Omega$ resistor at 290\,K in a 1\,MHz bandwidth has an open-circuit RMS noise voltage
$\sqrt{4kT_0RB}=\sqrt{4\times4.00\times10^{-21}\times50\times10^6}\approx0.89\,\mu$V, and delivers
$kT_0B=4.0\times10^{-15}$\,W $=-114$\,dBm to a matched load. A 1\,k$\Omega$ resistor has a noise
density $\sqrt{4kTR}\approx4$\,nV/$\sqrt{\text{Hz}}$, the figure audio designers know by heart;
over the 20\,kHz audio band it gives 0.57\,$\mu$V RMS. A microphone preamplifier with
1\,nV/$\sqrt{\text{Hz}}$ input noise is therefore quieter than a 1\,k$\Omega$ source---and a
4\,nV/$\sqrt{\text{Hz}}$ op-amp is already as noisy as one. In a radio, the same physics appears as
$-174$\,dBm/Hz: a GPS signal arrives at about $-130$\,dBm spread over 2\,MHz, some 19\,dB
\emph{below} the $-111$\,dBm of thermal noise in that band---yet receivers track it routinely, by
despreading (Chapter~\ref{ch:spreadspectrum}).
\end{worked}

\subsection{Shot noise}

Current is not a fluid; it is a stream of discrete charges. When they cross a potential barrier
independently and at random times---electrons leaving a hot cathode, carriers crossing a pn
junction, photoelectrons released in a photodiode---the current is a train of impulses
$i(t)=\sum_iq\,\delta(t-t_i)$ whose arrival times form a Poisson process of rate $\lambda=I/q$. By
Campbell's theorem, the fluctuation about the mean $I$ is white with two-sided PSD $q^2\lambda=qI$,
or one-sided
\begin{equation}
\overline{i_n^2}=2qI\ \ \mathrm{A^2/Hz},
\label{eq:ch03:shot}
\end{equation}
up to frequencies comparable with the inverse transit time of a carrier. This is \term{shot
noise}, predicted by Walter Schottky in 1918. A 1\,mA diode current carries
$\sqrt{2qI}\approx17.9$\,pA/$\sqrt{\text{Hz}}$ of shot noise---the same as the thermal noise current
of a 50\,$\Omega$ resistor. Unlike thermal noise, shot noise needs a current and a barrier: a
resistor carrying DC in thermal equilibrium shows only thermal noise, because the carriers'
motion is correlated by their frequent collisions.

Shot noise dominates in optical receivers (Chapter~\ref{ch:wireline}). The photocurrent of a
photodiode is a Poisson stream of photoelectrons, so even a perfect detector observing a perfectly
steady laser sees fluctuations. With direct detection of on--off keyed light, a ``one'' that
produces no photoelectrons at all is an error; with a Poisson mean of $N$ photons per ``one'' the
probability of that is $e^{-N}$, and a $10^{-9}$ error rate needs $N\approx20$, an average of about
10 photons per bit---the \emph{quantum limit} of direct detection. Practical receivers, limited by
the thermal noise of their transimpedance amplifiers, need hundreds to thousands; avalanche
photodiodes and optical preamplifiers exist to close the gap.

\begin{history}[Schottky and the small-shot effect]
Walter Schottky, working at Siemens and the University of Berlin, predicted in a 1918 paper in the
\emph{Annalen der Physik} that the current of a vacuum tube must fluctuate because electrons are
emitted independently, like lead shot---hence \emph{Schroteffekt}, the shot effect. He suggested
that the effect could be used to measure the charge of the electron, and experimenters in the
1920s did exactly that. Johnson, looking for the shot effect at audio frequencies, found far more
low-frequency noise than Schottky's formula predicted; in 1926 Schottky explained it as a slow
fluctuation of the cathode's emitting surface, the \emph{flicker effect}. Schottky also invented
the screen-grid tube and, independently of Armstrong, the superheterodyne principle, and his name
lives on in the Schottky barrier diode in every mixer and RF detector.
\end{history}

\subsection{Flicker (1/$f$) noise}

Nearly every active device, and many passive ones, shows excess noise whose PSD rises at low
frequency roughly as $1/f^\gamma$ with $\gamma\approx1$---\term{flicker noise} or \emph{pink
noise}. Its physical origins vary (trapping and release of carriers by defects at the
silicon--oxide interface of a MOSFET is the usual explanation in CMOS) and are still debated in
general; its effects do not. It is characterised by its \emph{corner frequency}, where it equals
the white noise floor: typically below a few kilohertz for bipolar transistors and from tens of
kilohertz to megahertz for small modern MOSFETs.

Flicker noise matters in three places. In \emph{direct-conversion} (zero-IF) receivers, which move
the wanted channel to DC (Chapter~\ref{ch:sdr}), the baseband amplifiers' $1/f$ noise lands on top of
the signal; narrowband zero-IF receivers therefore use bipolar or large-area devices, or a
low-IF architecture that keeps the signal away from DC. In \emph{oscillators}, flicker noise is
upconverted into close-in phase noise (below). In \emph{precision instrumentation}, it limits how
long averaging helps: white noise averages down as $1/\sqrt{T}$, but $1/f$ noise has as much power
in each decade of frequency as in the next, so the variance of a long average stops falling.
Chopper stabilisation and correlated double sampling move the measurement away from DC to escape
it.

\subsection{Phase noise: a preview}

An oscillator's output is not a perfect sinusoid but $A\cos(2\pi f_ct+\phi(t))$, where the
phase $\phi(t)$ wanders randomly. Its spectrum is a line broadened into skirts, specified by the
single-sideband phase-noise density $\mathcal L(\Delta f)$ in dBc/Hz at offset $\Delta f$ from the
carrier. Leeson's model (1966) explains the typical shape: white noise in the oscillator's
sustaining amplifier, filtered by the resonator, gives a $1/\Delta f^2$ region; flicker noise
upconverted by the amplifier's nonlinearity gives a steeper $1/\Delta f^3$ region close in; far
from the carrier the spectrum flattens to the amplifier's white floor. Phase noise hurts in two
ways: it rotates the constellation of the wanted signal, which limits high-order QAM and OFDM
(Chapters~\ref{ch:modulation} and~\ref{ch:ofdm}), and through \emph{reciprocal mixing} it transfers
a strong neighbour's power into the wanted channel, adding noise of approximately
$P_{\text{blocker}}+\mathcal L(\Delta f)+10\log_{10}B$ dBm. A $-30$\,dBm blocker 1\,MHz away and an
LO with $-130$\,dBc/Hz at that offset put about $-107$\,dBm into a 200\,kHz channel, comparable with
the sensitivity of a GSM receiver. Chapter~\ref{ch:sdr} treats oscillators, synthesisers and
their phase noise in detail.

\subsection{Quantisation noise: a preview}

Every digital receiver ends in an analog-to-digital converter, and every ADC rounds. A uniform
quantiser with step $\Delta$, driven by a busy signal, makes an error that is approximately uniform
on $[-\Delta/2,\Delta/2)$, uncorrelated with the signal and white, with power $\Delta^2/12$. For an
$N$-bit converter driven by a full-scale sinusoid, this gives the familiar signal-to-quantisation-noise
ratio
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SQNR}=6.02N+1.76\ \mathrm{dB},
\label{eq:ch03:sqnr}
\end{equation}
spread uniformly from DC to $f_s/2$. Oversampling spreads the same noise power over a wider band,
so a receiver that keeps only bandwidth $B$ gains $10\log_{10}(f_s/2B)$\,dB of processing gain;
noise shaping in sigma--delta converters pushes it further out of band. Chapter~\ref{ch:pcm} develops
quantisation, companding and sigma--delta conversion; Section~\ref{sec:ch03:adcfloor} below shows how
to treat an ADC as a noisy stage in the receiver's noise budget.

\subsection{Impulsive and man-made noise}
\label{sec:ch03:impulsive}

Below a few hundred megahertz the dominant noise in most places is not thermal at all but
man-made: ignition systems, electric motors, switch-mode power supplies, LED lighting drivers,
plasma displays, power-line arcing and the clock harmonics of every digital device. Much of it is
\emph{impulsive}: long quiet intervals punctuated by bursts. Its amplitude distribution is far from
Rayleigh. ITU-R P.372 characterises such noise by its amplitude probability distribution and by the
parameter $V_d$, the ratio in decibels of the RMS to the average envelope: $V_d=1.05$\,dB for
Gaussian noise ($20\log_{10}(2/\sqrt\pi)$), and values of 5--15\,dB are common for man-made noise.

A widely used model is Middleton's \emph{class A} noise, a Poisson-weighted mixture of Gaussians:
at any instant the noise is Gaussian with variance $\sigma_m^2$ with probability $e^{-A}A^m/m!$,
where $m$ is the number of active impulse sources and the \emph{impulsive index} $A$ (typically
0.01--1) sets how often impulses occur. Small $A$ means rare, strong impulses. A receiver designed
for Gaussian noise is badly mismatched to such noise: one impulse can wipe out a block of symbols,
and the error-rate curve develops a floor. Remedies include blanking or clipping before the
matched filter (a crude approximation of the optimal detector, which applies a nonlinearity to
the samples), interleaving so that FEC sees isolated errors, and OFDM, which spreads an impulse's
energy across all subcarriers. Power-line communication and DSL live with impulse noise; VDSL2
specifies an \emph{impulse noise protection} parameter in symbols (Chapter~\ref{ch:wireline}).

\subsection{Antenna temperature: the noise of the sky}
\label{sec:ch03:antenna}

An antenna is a receiver of thermal radiation as well as of signals. A body at physical
temperature $T$ radiates; in the Rayleigh--Jeans limit ($hf\ll kT$) its brightness is
proportional to $T$, and radio engineers describe any radiation by its \term{brightness
temperature} $T_B(\theta,\phi)$, the temperature a black body would need to produce it. An antenna
with power pattern $G(\theta,\phi)$ looking at a scene of brightness $T_B$ delivers to a matched
receiver the noise power $kT_AB$, where the \term{antenna temperature} is the pattern-weighted
average
\begin{equation}
T_A=\frac{1}{4\pi}\oint G(\theta,\phi)\,T_B(\theta,\phi)\,d\Omega .
\label{eq:ch03:tant}
\end{equation}
An antenna inside a closed box at uniform temperature $T$ therefore has $T_A=T$, whatever its
pattern---the transmission-line argument again. An antenna pointed at the sky sees the
sum of several contributions, plotted in Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_skynoise}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Man-made noise} (above), dominant below about 30\,MHz in cities and important up to a
few hundred megahertz. ITU-R P.372 gives median values of the external noise figure
$F_a=10\log_{10}(T_A/T_0)$ as $F_a=c-d\log_{10}f_{\text{MHz}}$ with, for example, $(c,d)=(76.8,27.7)$
for business (city) areas, $(72.5,27.7)$ residential, $(67.2,27.7)$ rural and $(53.6,28.6)$ quiet rural,
valid from 0.3 to 250\,MHz.
\item \textbf{Atmospheric noise} in the ITU's sense: radiation from lightning, propagated worldwide by
the ionosphere. It dominates at LF to HF, especially at night and in the tropics.
\item \textbf{Galactic noise}: synchrotron radiation from relativistic electrons spiralling in the
Milky Way's magnetic field, with $F_a\approx52-23\log_{10}f_{\text{MHz}}$ (P.372) and strongest
towards the galactic centre in Sagittarius. It dominates the quiet sky from about 20\,MHz to
1\,GHz: at 100\,MHz the cold sky is near 1000\,K.
\item \textbf{The cosmic microwave background}: a 2.725\,K black body filling the sky, the afterglow
of the hot early universe.
\item \textbf{The atmosphere}: oxygen and water vapour absorb and therefore emit. In clear air the
zenith contribution is only a few kelvin between 1 and 10\,GHz, but rises near the 22\,GHz water
line, becomes total (the full 270--290\,K of the air) in the 60\,GHz oxygen band, and grows at low
elevation angles, where the path through the air is longer. A lossy medium with loss $L$ at
physical temperature $T_m$ contributes $T_m(1-1/L)$; rain is such a medium, so a rain fade
both weakens the signal \emph{and} raises the noise (Chapter~\ref{ch:satellite}).
\item \textbf{The ground and the Sun}: sidelobes that see the 290\,K ground add tens of kelvin to a
dish's temperature; the Sun's microwave brightness temperature is around $10^4$\,K at 10\,GHz and
higher at lower frequencies, enough to blind a satellite receiver when the Sun passes behind the
satellite---the \emph{sun outage} that interrupts geostationary TV reception for a few minutes a
day around the equinoxes.
\end{itemize}

The figure explains three facts of radio engineering. At HF (3--30\,MHz), external noise is so
strong that receiver noise figure hardly matters: a 10\,dB noise-figure receiver is as good as a
perfect one, and the art lies in antennas that reject local man-made noise. From VHF to the low
gigahertz, man-made and galactic noise still often exceed a good receiver's noise, which is why
a TV or FM receiver's noise figure is rarely critical. In the ``microwave window'' from about 1 to
10\,GHz, the sky is quiet and receiver noise dominates, which is why satellite, deep-space and
radio-astronomy systems live there and use cryogenically cooled amplifiers.

\begin{history}[Jansky's star noise and Penzias and Wilson's excess temperature]
In 1930 Bell Labs asked a young engineer, Karl Jansky, to find the sources of the static that
plagued the new transatlantic radiotelephone service. At Holmdel, New Jersey, he built a rotating
directional antenna for 20.5\,MHz---the ``merry-go-round''---and over more than a year catalogued
the static from nearby and distant thunderstorms. A third component remained: a steady hiss whose
direction drifted through the day. It returned to the same direction not every 24 hours but every
23 hours and 56 minutes, a sidereal day, so its source was fixed among the stars; it was strongest
towards Sagittarius, the centre of the Milky Way. Jansky published ``Electrical disturbances
apparently of extraterrestrial origin'' in the \emph{Proceedings of the IRE} in 1933, founding radio
astronomy; the unit of radio flux density, the jansky, honours him.

Three decades later, at the same Holmdel site, Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson were using a 20-foot
horn antenna, built for the Echo and Telstar satellite experiments, and a maser receiver
calibrated against a liquid-helium cold load---the most carefully understood low-noise system of
its day. After accounting for the atmosphere, the antenna's losses and the ground, they were left
with an excess antenna temperature of about 3.5\,K at 4.08\,GHz, the same in every direction and
at every time of year. They cleaned the horn, famously evicting a pair of pigeons and their
``white dielectric material'', and the excess remained. A group at Princeton led by Robert Dicke was
at that moment building an instrument to search for the predicted relic radiation of a hot early
universe; the two papers appeared together in the \emph{Astrophysical Journal} in 1965. Penzias and
Wilson shared the 1978 Nobel Prize in Physics. The cosmic microwave background was discovered
as an entry in a noise-temperature budget---a reminder that careful noise accounting is a scientific
instrument.
\end{history}

\section{Noise figure and noise temperature}
\label{sec:ch03:nf}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Definitions}

Every real amplifier, mixer and filter adds noise of its own. Following D.~O.~North (RCA, 1942)
and H.~T.~Friis (Bell Labs, 1944), we characterise a two-port by how much it degrades the
signal-to-noise ratio. Its \term{noise factor} is
\begin{equation}
F=\frac{\mathrm{SNR}_{\text{in}}}{\mathrm{SNR}_{\text{out}}}\Bigg|_{\,T_{\text{source}}=T_0=290\,\mathrm K},
\label{eq:ch03:nfdef}
\end{equation}
and its \term{noise figure} is $\mathrm{NF}=10\log_{10}F$\,dB. The condition matters: the noise
factor is defined with the input noise being the thermal noise $kT_0B$ of a matched source at
the standard temperature $T_0=290$\,K. (The value was chosen as a typical room temperature;
it also makes $kT_0$ a round $4.00\times10^{-21}$\,W/Hz.) An equivalent form follows from
writing the output noise as amplified input noise plus the device's own contribution $N_a$:
$F=(GkT_0B+N_a)/(GkT_0B)=1+N_a/(GkT_0B)$. A noiseless device has $F=1$ (0\,dB).

The same device can be modelled as \emph{noiseless}, with an extra noise source at its input of
\term{equivalent noise temperature} $T_e$, chosen so that $N_a=GkT_eB$
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_noisemodel}). Comparing the two descriptions,
\begin{equation}
T_e=(F-1)\,T_0,\qquad F=1+\frac{T_e}{T_0}.
\label{eq:ch03:te}
\end{equation}
A 0.5\,dB noise figure corresponds to $T_e=35$\,K; 1\,dB to 75\,K; 2\,dB to 170\,K; 3\,dB to
290\,K; 6\,dB to 865\,K; 10\,dB to 2610\,K. Noise temperature is the natural unit when the input
noise is not at 290\,K---for example, an antenna looking at the cold sky---and it is the unit
satellite and radio-astronomy engineers prefer, because it adds: a source at $T_s$ feeding a
device with noise temperature $T_e$ produces output noise $Gk(T_s+T_e)B$, and the output SNR is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SNR}_{\text{out}}=\frac{P_s}{k(T_s+T_e)B}.
\label{eq:ch03:snrtsys}
\end{equation}
Noise figure is convenient for terrestrial systems whose antennas see the 290\,K environment;
for anything else, convert to temperatures, add, and convert back.

\subsection{Passive lossy components}

A passive component with power loss $L\ge1$ (a cable, filter, attenuator, switch or the
radome in front of an antenna) at physical temperature $T_p$ is not noiseless: anything that
absorbs also emits. Terminate its input in a matched load at the same temperature $T_p$. Everything
is in thermal equilibrium, so the output noise must be $kT_pB$. Of that, the load contributes
$kT_pB/L$ through the attenuation; the component itself must therefore contribute
$kT_pB(1-1/L)$, which referred to its input (multiply by $L$) is $kT_p(L-1)B$. Hence
\begin{equation}
T_e=(L-1)\,T_p,\qquad F=1+(L-1)\frac{T_p}{T_0},
\label{eq:ch03:lossy}
\end{equation}
and at $T_p=T_0$ simply $F=L$: a 2\,dB cable in front of a receiver adds exactly 2\,dB to the
noise figure. Cooling the loss helps: the same 2\,dB at 20\,K contributes only 12\,K instead of
169\,K, which is why radio telescopes cool not only their amplifiers but their feed horns,
polarisers and couplers.

\subsection{Cascades: the Friis formula}

Consider two stages with gains $G_1,G_2$ and noise temperatures $T_{e1},T_{e2}$, driven by a
source at $T_s$. The output noise is the source noise and first-stage noise amplified by both
stages, plus the second stage's noise amplified by the second stage alone:
\[
N_{\text{out}}=G_1G_2k(T_s+T_{e1})B+G_2kT_{e2}B=G_1G_2k\Big(T_s+T_{e1}+\frac{T_{e2}}{G_1}\Big)B .
\]
The bracket identifies the cascade's noise temperature as $T_{e1}+T_{e2}/G_1$. Repeating the
argument for more stages, and converting with~\eqref{eq:ch03:te}, gives the formula Harald Friis
published in 1944:
\begin{equation}
F=F_1+\frac{F_2-1}{G_1}+\frac{F_3-1}{G_1G_2}+\cdots,\qquad
T_e=T_{e1}+\frac{T_{e2}}{G_1}+\frac{T_{e3}}{G_1G_2}+\cdots
\label{eq:ch03:friis}
\end{equation}
The gains are \emph{available} power gains, and the formula assumes each stage's noise figure was
measured with the source impedance it actually sees in the cascade. The noise of each stage is
divided by the total gain before it. The first stage dominates, provided it has enough gain; this
is the reason every sensitive receiver starts with a low-noise amplifier (LNA), and why the LNA
should be as close to the antenna as possible. A lossy stage has $G=1/L<1$, so it
\emph{multiplies} the contribution of everything after it. Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_friis} makes the
point numerically: moving a 1\,dB LNA from after a 2\,dB cable and a 1.5\,dB filter to directly at
the antenna lowers the system noise figure from about 5.1\,dB to 2.2\,dB, the equivalent of
doubling the transmitter power.

A cellular receive chain has, in order: an antenna switch (0.5\,dB loss), a SAW duplexer filter

The 2\,dB of passive loss ahead of the LNA costs more than the LNA itself, and the mixer, despite its

\begin{inpractice}[Mast-head amplifiers and satellite LNBs]
Cellular base stations often place a tower-mounted amplifier at the top of the mast, before
tens of metres of lossy coaxial cable. A satellite TV dish goes further: its low-noise block
downconverter (LNB) at the focus amplifies the 10.7--12.75\,GHz signal with a noise
temperature under 100\,K and immediately converts it to 950--2150\,MHz, where cable losses
are tolerable. The Ettus B200, whose only LNA is the one inside its AD9364 RFIC, is specified
with a receive noise figure under 8\,dB---fine for bench work, but an external LNA with 1\,dB NF will improve weak-signal
reception by several decibels if placed at the antenna.
\end{inpractice}

\subsection{Antenna, line and system noise temperature}
\label{sec:ch03:tsys}

The \term{system noise temperature} combines the antenna's temperature and the receiver's, referred
to a single point. For an antenna at $T_{\text{ant}}$ connected through a feed of loss $L$ at physical
temperature $T_p$ to an LNA of noise temperature $T_{\text{LNA}}$ and gain $G$, followed by the rest of
the receiver at $T_{\text{rest}}$, the system temperature referred to the LNA input is
\begin{equation}
T_{\text{sys}}=\frac{T_{\text{ant}}}{L}+T_p\Big(1-\frac1L\Big)+T_{\text{LNA}}+\frac{T_{\text{rest}}}{G},
\label{eq:ch03:tsys}
\end{equation}
and the receiver's noise power is $kT_{\text{sys}}B$. The first term is the antenna noise attenuated by
the feed; the second is the feed's own emission. (Referred to the antenna terminals instead, every
term is multiplied by $L$; the signal is too, so the SNR is the same.) Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_lossyline}
shows why satellite and deep-space engineers obsess over a tenth of a decibel. With a cold sky
($T_{\text{ant}}=20$\,K) and a good LNA, each 0.1\,dB of loss at 290\,K adds about 6.6\,K to $T_{\text{sys}}$,
on top of losing 0.1\,dB of signal: half a decibel of feed loss degrades the SNR by nearly 3\,dB.
With a warm antenna looking at 290\,K (a terrestrial link), the same loss simply costs its own value.

\mdcfig{ch03_lossyline}{The cost of loss ahead of the LNA. Left: system noise temperature at the LNA
input from~\eqref{eq:ch03:tsys}. Right: total SNR degradation (signal loss plus noise increase)
relative to a lossless feed. For a cold-sky antenna a few tenths of a decibel of warm loss cost
several decibels; for an antenna seeing 290\,K the penalty equals the loss.}

For a satellite ground station the figure of merit is $G/T_{\text{sys}}$ in dB/K, the antenna gain divided
by the system temperature, both referred to the same point. Since the received carrier power is
proportional to $G$ and the noise density is $kT_{\text{sys}}$, $C/N_0$ is proportional to $G/T$ and the
link budget separates into transmitter, path and receiver terms (Section~\ref{sec:ch03:linkbudget} and
Chapter~\ref{ch:satellite}).

\begin{inpractice}[Cryogenic receivers: radio astronomy and the Deep Space Network]
When the sky contributes only a few kelvin, the receiver's noise is everything, and the answer is
to cool it. Radio telescopes and the antennas of NASA's Deep Space Network use HEMT (high-electron-mobility
transistor) amplifiers cooled by closed-cycle helium refrigerators to around 4--20\,K, at which
temperatures their noise temperatures are only a few kelvin at microwave frequencies; earlier
generations used ruby masers cooled in liquid helium. The resulting system temperatures, including
the atmosphere, the CMB and the antenna's own losses and spillover, are of order 20\,K at X-band
(8.4\,GHz) for a large DSN antenna at high elevation. A room-temperature LNA with a 0.5\,dB noise
figure (35\,K) would roughly double that, halving the data rate from a distant spacecraft. Cryogenic
receivers pay for themselves many times over when the alternative is a larger antenna.
\end{inpractice}

\subsection{Mixers, image noise and the source impedance}

Two refinements matter in practice. First, \emph{mixers} convert two input bands to the same
output: the wanted signal band and the \emph{image} band on the other side of the LO. A
single-sideband (SSB) noise figure counts noise from both bands but signal from only one; a
double-sideband (DSB) noise figure, appropriate for radiometers and zero-IF receivers whose
signal occupies both sidebands, counts signal in both. For a mixer with equal response in both
bands, the SSB figure is 3\,dB higher than the DSB figure. Comparing a datasheet DSB number with an
SSB requirement is a classic 3\,dB error. An image-reject filter or image-reject mixer architecture
removes the image noise (Chapter~\ref{ch:sdr}).

Second, the noise figure of an active device depends on the impedance of the source driving it:
\begin{equation}
F=F_{\min}+\frac{R_n}{G_s}\,\big|Y_s-Y_{\text{opt}}\big|^2,
\label{eq:ch03:fmin}
\end{equation}
where $Y_s=G_s+jB_s$ is the source admittance, $Y_{\text{opt}}$ the optimum, $F_{\min}$ the
minimum noise factor and $R_n$ the noise resistance. The optimum source for noise is generally
not the conjugate match that maximises gain, so LNA design is a compromise between noise figure,
gain, input return loss and stability, made on the Smith chart with circles of constant noise
figure and constant gain (Pozar's \emph{Microwave Engineering} is the standard treatment).
Inductive source degeneration in CMOS LNAs is a popular trick that brings the noise and power
matches close together.

\subsection{Measuring noise figure: the Y-factor method}
\label{sec:ch03:yfactor}

How do you measure a quantity defined by a ratio of SNRs when you cannot see the signal and the
noise separately? The standard answer is the \term{Y-factor method}. Connect to the device under
test (DUT) a noise source that can be switched between two known temperatures, $T_h$ (hot) and $T_c$
(cold), and measure the output noise power in each state. Because the output power is a straight
line in the source temperature, $N=GkB(T_s+T_e)$, two points determine the line, and its intercept
on the temperature axis is $-T_e$ (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_yfactor}). With the measured ratio
$Y=N_h/N_c$,
\begin{equation}
Y=\frac{T_h+T_e}{T_c+T_e}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad T_e=\frac{T_h-YT_c}{Y-1}.
\label{eq:ch03:yfactor}
\end{equation}
Neither the gain nor the bandwidth needs to be known: they cancel in the ratio. Commercial noise
sources are avalanche diodes specified by their \term{excess noise ratio},
$\mathrm{ENR}=(T_h-T_0)/T_0$ (in dB), with $T_c=T_0$ when the diode is off. Then~\eqref{eq:ch03:yfactor}
simplifies to
\begin{equation}
F=\frac{\mathrm{ENR}}{Y-1}\qquad\text{(linear units)}.
\end{equation}

\mdcfig{ch03_yfactor}{The Y-factor method. Left: output noise is linear in source temperature, so
measurements with a hot and a cold load fix the line and its intercept, $-T_e$. Right: error in the
measured noise figure caused by a 0.05\,dB error in measuring $Y$, for two noise-source ENRs. When
the DUT's noise is comparable with the hot source's excess noise, $Y$ approaches one and errors
explode.}

\begin{worked}[Measuring an LNA]
A noise source with ENR 15.0\,dB (a factor of 31.6) drives an LNA of 20\,dB gain, followed by a
noise-figure analyzer whose own noise figure is 8\,dB. Switching the source on raises the measured
power by $Y=13.5$\,dB (22.4). The system noise factor is $31.6/(22.4-1)=1.478$, or 1.70\,dB. That
includes the analyzer, the second stage of the cascade; removing it with the Friis formula,
$F_1=1.478-(10^{0.8}-1)/100=1.425$, so the LNA's noise figure is 1.54\,dB, its noise temperature
123\,K. For a cryogenic amplifier, by contrast, the hot--cold method with physical loads is more
accurate: with an ambient load at 290\,K and a liquid-nitrogen load at 77\,K, a measured $Y$ of
5.05\,dB (3.20) gives $T_e=(290-3.20\times77)/(3.20-1)\approx20$\,K. Note how sensitive this is: an error
of 0.1\,dB in $Y$ changes the answer by about 3\,K, 15\%.
\end{worked}

\begin{inpractice}[Y-factor measurements in the lab]
Noise-figure analyzers and spectrum analyzers with a noise-figure option automate the procedure:
they pulse a 28\,V supply to a calibrated noise source (ENR of about 5--6\,dB or about 15\,dB are
common models), measure the hot and cold powers across a list of frequencies, look up the
source's ENR calibration table, and apply the second-stage correction automatically after a
calibration step without the DUT. Good practice, much of it in Keysight's application note 57-2:
use a low-ENR source for low-noise-figure DUTs (it changes the source's impedance less between
states and keeps the analyzer linear); make sure $Y$ is well above 1 (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_yfactor},
right); keep the DUT's output well below compression, since the measured power is broadband noise;
add an isolator when the DUT's noise figure is sensitive to the source match; and remember that
the noise source's ENR assumes it is at 290\,K---on a hot bench, correct for it. For frequency-converting
DUTs, decide whether you want the SSB or the DSB figure before you start. An alternative, the
\emph{cold-source} method, measures only the DUT's output noise with a cold (ambient) termination and
relies on an accurately known gain; vector network analyzers use it because they already measure
gain precisely.
\end{inpractice}

\begin{inpractice}[Noise figures of real receivers]
Representative, approximate values: a discrete GaAs pHEMT LNA at 2\,GHz, 0.4--0.8\,dB; a GPS
active-antenna LNA, about 1\,dB; an LNA integrated in a CMOS transceiver, 1.5--3\,dB; the complete
receive chain of a smartphone, including the antenna switch and duplexer, roughly 3--7\,dB depending
on the band; a Wi-Fi access point, about 4--6\,dB; a software-defined radio such as the Ettus B200,
5--8\,dB depending on frequency and gain setting; a Ku-band satellite LNB, 0.5--1\,dB; a
millimetre-wave 5G phased-array front end at 28\,GHz, 5--8\,dB; a cryogenic radio-astronomy
receiver, a few kelvin. The specifications are more conservative: 3GPP's reference sensitivity
derivations assume a UE noise figure of about 9\,dB and the IEEE 802.11 minimum sensitivities are
consistent with about 10\,dB (Section~\ref{sec:ch03:sensitivity}). The gap is margin for production
spread, temperature, the worst band and the loss of the antenna itself.
\end{inpractice}

\section{Receiver sensitivity}
\label{sec:ch03:sensitivity}
%======================================================================

\subsection{The sensitivity equation}

The \term{sensitivity} of a receiver is the smallest input power at which it meets a specified
quality---a bit-error rate, packet-error rate or throughput. Three numbers determine it: the
thermal noise in the receiver's noise bandwidth, the receiver's noise figure, and the SNR the
demodulator needs:
\begin{equation}
P_{\text{sens}}\,[\mathrm{dBm}]=-174+10\log_{10}B+\mathrm{NF}+\mathrm{SNR}_{\text{req}}\,(+\,\text{implementation margin}).
\label{eq:ch03:sens}
\end{equation}
Each term has a home in this book. The $-174$ is physics (Section~\ref{sec:ch03:thermal}). The
bandwidth is the \emph{noise} bandwidth, which for a matched filter equals the symbol rate
(Section~\ref{sec:ch03:processes}). The noise figure is the cascade of
Section~\ref{sec:ch03:nf}. The required SNR comes from the modulation and coding
(Chapters~\ref{ch:modulation}, \ref{ch:classiccodes} and~\ref{ch:moderncodes}) and from the
channel---static, or faded and with diversity. Chapter~\ref{ch:channels} turns this equation into
the maximum allowable path loss of a cellular link budget.

\subsection{Sensitivity in the standards}

Every air-interface standard specifies a minimum sensitivity, and the numbers can be reconstructed
from~\eqref{eq:ch03:sens} with the assumptions the standards' authors used. Two examples:

\paragraph{IEEE 802.11a/g.} The standard (IEEE 802.11, OFDM PHY clause) requires a packet-error rate
below 10\% for 1000-byte packets at $-82$\,dBm for the 6\,Mb/s mode and $-65$\,dBm for 54\,Mb/s, in
a 20\,MHz channel. The thermal noise in 20\,MHz is $-101$\,dBm; the usual reconstruction adds a
10\,dB noise figure and a 5\,dB implementation margin, leaving a required SNR of about 4\,dB for
BPSK rate-1/2 and about 21\,dB for 64-QAM rate-3/4. A real access point beats these figures by
5--10\,dB, which is why vendors quote their own sensitivities.

\paragraph{LTE and NR.} For an LTE UE in a 10\,MHz carrier (band 1), 3GPP TS 36.101 specifies a
\emph{reference sensitivity} (REFSENS) of about $-97$\,dBm for 95\% of maximum throughput with QPSK
and a low code rate, received on two antennas. A commonly quoted reconstruction: thermal noise in
the 9\,MHz occupied by 50 resource blocks, $-104.5$\,dBm; UE noise figure 9\,dB; required SNR about
$-1$\,dB; a two-antenna diversity gain of 3\,dB; and an implementation margin of 2.5\,dB:
$-104.5+9-1-3+2.5=-97$\,dBm. TS 38.101-1 specifies NR's REFSENS per band and bandwidth in the same
way. These numbers are minimums that every phone must meet in every band; they are also the
starting point of operators' link budgets.

Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_sensitivity} places these and other systems against the thermal floor. Most
receivers sit 5--20\,dB above $kT_0B$. Two sit far below it: LoRa, whose chirp spread spectrum
with spreading factor 12 is demodulated about 20\,dB below the noise in its 125\,kHz channel
(datasheet sensitivities near $-137$\,dBm), and GPS, whose signal is received about 19\,dB below
the noise in its 2\,MHz bandwidth. Neither violates physics: both trade bandwidth for SNR by
spreading, and their \emph{post-despreading} SNR, in the much smaller bandwidth of the information,
is positive (Chapter~\ref{ch:spreadspectrum}).

\subsection{The ADC as a noisy stage}
\label{sec:ch03:adcfloor}

An ADC adds quantisation and thermal noise, and it is convenient to describe it, like any other
stage, by a noise figure. If its full-scale input power is $P_{\text{FS}}$ (dBm, into the specified
impedance) and its measured SNR for a full-scale signal is $\mathrm{SNR}_{\text{ADC}}$, spread
uniformly over the Nyquist band $f_s/2$, its noise density is
$P_{\text{FS}}-\mathrm{SNR}_{\text{ADC}}-10\log_{10}(f_s/2)$\,dBm/Hz, and its effective noise figure is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{NF}_{\text{ADC}}=P_{\text{FS}}-\mathrm{SNR}_{\text{ADC}}-10\log_{10}(f_s/2)+174\ \ \mathrm{dB}.
\label{eq:ch03:nfadc}
\end{equation}
An ideal 12-bit converter (SNR 74\,dB) at 100\,MS/s with a full scale of $+4$\,dBm has a noise
density of $-147$\,dBm/Hz and an effective noise figure of 27\,dB; a 14-bit converter at
250\,MS/s, about 11\,dB in the ideal case and more in practice. These are poor noise figures, which is
why the analog chain must provide enough gain to lift the antenna's thermal noise comfortably above
the ADC's floor---typically by 10\,dB or more, so that the ADC adds a few tenths of a decibel to the
cascade. Too little gain and the ADC dominates the noise figure; too much and strong signals clip.
The usable range between is the receiver's \emph{dynamic range}, the central trade-off of
software-defined radio design (Chapter~\ref{ch:sdr}). Equation~\eqref{eq:ch03:nfadc} also shows the
reward of a high sample rate: doubling $f_s$ improves $\mathrm{NF}_{\text{ADC}}$ by 3\,dB, because the
same noise is spread more thinly and the digital filters discard most of it.

\section{SNR, \texorpdfstring{$E_b/N_0$}{Eb/N0}, \texorpdfstring{$E_s/N_0$}{Es/N0} and \texorpdfstring{$C/N_0$}{C/N0}}
\label{sec:ch03:snr}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Four ratios}

Four ratios describe how strong a signal is relative to noise, and translating between them is
daily work.
\begin{itemize}
\item The \textbf{signal-to-noise ratio} $\mathrm{SNR}=P/(N_0B)$ depends on the bandwidth $B$
in which the noise is measured. Always ask: which bandwidth?
\item The \textbf{carrier-to-noise-density ratio} $C/N_0=P/N_0$, in dB-Hz, is independent of
bandwidth. It is the natural figure of merit for a link budget, because it can be computed
before the modulation is chosen. GPS receivers report it for each satellite; 45\,dB-Hz is a
strong GPS signal and 30\,dB-Hz a weak one.
\item The \textbf{energy per symbol to noise density} $E_s/N_0=P/(R_sN_0)$ is what the
demodulator sees: with a matched filter, the SNR at the sampler.
\item The \textbf{energy per bit to noise density} $E_b/N_0=P/(R_bN_0)$ normalises by the
information bit rate $R_b$. It is the fair basis for comparing modulation and coding schemes,
because it charges each scheme for the energy it spends per bit of information.
\end{itemize}
Since $E_b=P/R_b$ and $E_s=P/R_s$, and an $M$-ary scheme with code rate $R_c$ carries
$R_b=R_s\log_2M\cdot R_c$ information bits per second, they are related by
\begin{equation}
\frac{E_b}{N_0}=\frac{C}{N_0}\cdot\frac{1}{R_b}=\mathrm{SNR}\cdot\frac{B}{R_b},
\qquad
\frac{E_s}{N_0}=\frac{E_b}{N_0}\cdot\log_2M\cdot R_c ,
\label{eq:ch03:ebno}
\end{equation}
where $E_s$ is the energy per symbol. The energy per \emph{coded} bit is $E_c=R_cE_b$; a
rate-1/2 code spends half of each information bit's energy on each of two coded bits, which is
why a coded system's raw (pre-decoder) bit-error rate is worse than an uncoded one's at the same
$E_b/N_0$---and its decoded error rate far better. In decibels everything is addition:
$E_b/N_0=C/N_0-10\log_{10}R_b$, and
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SNR}=\frac{E_b}{N_0}+10\log_{10}\frac{R_b}{B}=\frac{E_s}{N_0}+10\log_{10}\frac{R_s}{B}\quad\text{(dB)}.
\label{eq:ch03:snrdb}
\end{equation}
The ratio $R_b/B$ is the \emph{spectral efficiency} $\eta$ in b/s/Hz, so $\mathrm{SNR}=\eta\,E_b/N_0$.
Substituting into Shannon's formula gives the minimum $E_b/N_0$ for reliable communication at
efficiency $\eta$, $(2^\eta-1)/\eta$, which tends to $\ln2$, or $-1.59$\,dB, as $\eta\to0$---the
ultimate limit on energy per bit (Chapter~\ref{ch:infotheory}).

\begin{worked}[From $C/N_0$ to bit rate]
A receiver measures $C/N_0=60$\,dB-Hz. A QPSK link with a rate-1/2 code needs
$E_b/N_0=2$\,dB at the target error rate. The maximum bit rate is
$R_b=(C/N_0)/(E_b/N_0)=10^{(60-2)/10}\approx630$\,kb/s. With 3\,dB of implementation margin,
plan for 315\,kb/s. The symbol rate is then $R_s=R_b/(2\times\tfrac12)=315$\,kBd, and with RRC
filtering of roll-off 0.25 the occupied bandwidth is about 394\,kHz. The SNR in that occupied
bandwidth is $E_b/N_0+3+10\log_{10}(315/394)\approx4$\,dB, a number that would look alarmingly
low on a spectrum analyzer and is perfectly adequate for the modem.
\end{worked}

\subsection{Getting the noise right in simulation}

A simulation at $N_{\text{sps}}$ samples per symbol, with symbols of energy $E_s$ (in units of
sample-power $\times$ sample-period), must add complex noise of variance
\begin{equation}
\sigma^2=\frac{N_{\text{sps}}\,P_{\text{sample}}}{E_s/N_0}=\frac{N_{\text{sps}}\,P_{\text{sample}}}{(E_b/N_0)\,R_c\log_2M}
\label{eq:ch03:simnoise}
\end{equation}
per sample, where $P_{\text{sample}}$ is the average signal power per sample (usually normalised to
one). The factor $N_{\text{sps}}$ is the one most often forgotten: oversampling spreads the
same noise density over a wider simulated bandwidth, so each sample carries more noise, and the
matched filter, which integrates over $N_{\text{sps}}$ samples, collects it back. Measuring the SNR
per sample of an oversampled waveform and calling it $E_s/N_0$ is a 9\,dB error at 8 samples per
symbol.

\begin{pitfall}[What counts as a bit?]
$E_b$ is the energy per \emph{information} bit, and every overhead must be charged to it:
the cyclic prefix of OFDM (about 7\% in LTE), pilot and reference symbols, preambles,
guard bands that carry no data, CRC and header bits, and the energy in pulse-shaping
excess bandwidth if the noise bandwidth is wider than the symbol rate. Published curves of
BER versus $E_b/N_0$ for coded systems are comparable only if they agree on this
accounting. When comparing a datasheet's ``SNR'' with a textbook's $E_b/N_0$, convert
explicitly with~\eqref{eq:ch03:snrdb} and state the bandwidth.
\end{pitfall}

\section{Detecting a signal in noise}
\label{sec:ch03:detection}
%======================================================================

\subsection{The binary hypothesis test}

The simplest question a receiver can ask is: is there a signal at all? A radar asks it in every
range cell, a Wi-Fi receiver at every sample while waiting for a preamble, a GPS receiver for
every code phase and Doppler bin, a 5G base station for every random-access opportunity. Formally,
the receiver observes $\vect r$ and chooses between two hypotheses,
\begin{equation}
H_0:\ \vect r=\vect n,\qquad H_1:\ \vect r=\vect s+\vect n .
\end{equation}
Two errors are possible: a \term{false alarm} (deciding $H_1$ when $H_0$ is true), with probability
$P_{FA}$, and a \emph{miss} (deciding $H_0$ under $H_1$), with probability $1-P_D$, where $P_D$ is the
\term{probability of detection}. Any detector can trade one for the other by moving its
threshold. The curve of $P_D$ against $P_{FA}$ traced out as the threshold moves is the
\term{receiver operating characteristic} (ROC), a name that comes from the radar receivers of the
Second World War.

\subsection{The Neyman--Pearson lemma}

If the priors and the costs of the two errors are known, the Bayes decision rule of
Section~\ref{sec:ch03:prob} applies: compare the likelihood ratio with a threshold set by the
priors and costs. In detection problems the priors are usually unknown (how likely is an enemy
aircraft?), and the natural criterion is that of Neyman and Pearson (1933): \emph{maximise $P_D$
subject to $P_{FA}\le\alpha$}. The \term{Neyman--Pearson lemma} says the optimal detector is again a
likelihood-ratio test,
\begin{equation}
\Lambda(\vect r)=\frac{p(\vect r\mid H_1)}{p(\vect r\mid H_0)}\ \underset{H_0}{\overset{H_1}{\gtrless}}\ \gamma,
\label{eq:ch03:lrt}
\end{equation}
with the threshold $\gamma$ chosen to make $P_{FA}=\alpha$. The proof is a short exchange argument:
any other decision region with the same $P_{FA}$ must give up some points with high likelihood
ratio in exchange for points with lower likelihood ratio, and so loses detection probability. Every
optimal detector, MAP or Neyman--Pearson, computes the same statistic; they differ only in the
threshold. The ROC of a likelihood-ratio test is concave, lies above the diagonal $P_D=P_{FA}$
(the performance of a coin toss), and has slope $\gamma$ at the operating point.

\subsection{Known signal in white Gaussian noise}

For a known signal $s(t)$ of energy $E$ in white noise of density $N_0/2$, the log-likelihood ratio
reduces (Chapter~\ref{ch:modulation}) to the correlation $y=\int r(t)s(t)\,dt$: a matched filter
sampled at the right instant. Under $H_0$, $y$ is Gaussian with mean 0 and variance $EN_0/2$; under
$H_1$ the mean is $E$. Setting the threshold for a given $P_{FA}$ and computing $P_D$,
\begin{equation}
P_{FA}=\Q\!\left(\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{EN_0/2}}\right),\qquad
P_D=\Q\!\big(\Q^{-1}(P_{FA})-d\big),\qquad d=\sqrt{\frac{2E}{N_0}} .
\label{eq:ch03:pd}
\end{equation}
The \emph{deflection} $d$ is the separation of the two Gaussian hypotheses in standard deviations,
and it depends only on the signal energy, not its shape (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_roc}). To reach
$P_D=0.9$ at $P_{FA}=10^{-6}$ requires $d=4.75+1.28=6.03$, an $E/N_0$ of 12.6\,dB.

\mdcfig{ch03_roc}{Detection in Gaussian noise. Left: the test statistic under the two hypotheses;
the threshold $\gamma$ sets $P_{FA}$ (dark area) and $P_D$ (light area). Centre: ROC curves for a known
signal at deflections $d=\sqrt{2E/N_0}$ from 1 to 4. Right: $P_D$ versus $E/N_0$ at
$P_{FA}=10^{-6}$ for coherent detection, envelope detection of a signal of unknown phase, and an
energy detector integrating 16 complex samples of an unknown signal.}

\subsection{Unknown phase, unknown signal}

A radar echo or a burst preamble arrives with an unknown carrier phase. The optimal detector then
correlates with both the in-phase and quadrature versions of the expected waveform and compares the
\emph{envelope} $|y|$ with a threshold---a noncoherent detector. Under $H_0$ the envelope is
Rayleigh, so the threshold for a given false-alarm rate follows from $P_{FA}=e^{-\gamma^2/2\sigma^2}$;
under $H_1$ it is Rice, and
\begin{equation}
P_D=Q_1\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{2E}{N_0}},\ \sqrt{-2\ln P_{FA}}\right),
\label{eq:ch03:marcum}
\end{equation}
where $Q_1(a,b)$ is the \term{Marcum Q function}, the tail of the Rice distribution, introduced by J.~I.~Marcum
in his 1948 RAND report on radar detection. The penalty for not knowing the phase is surprisingly
small at high $P_D$: $P_D=0.9$, $P_{FA}=10^{-6}$ needs 13.2\,dB instead of 12.6\,dB. Radar engineers
estimate it with Albersheim's empirical equation, which for a single pulse reads
$\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{dB}}\approx\big(6.2+4.54/\sqrt{1.44}\big)\log_{10}(A+0.12AB+1.7B)$, with
$A=\ln(0.62/P_{FA})$ and $B=\ln(P_D/(1-P_D))$; for $P_D=0.9$, $P_{FA}=10^{-6}$ it gives 13.1\,dB.

When nothing is known about the signal except its band, the best one can do is measure energy: the
\emph{energy detector} (radiometer) sums $|r_k|^2$ over $N$ samples and compares with a threshold.
Under $H_0$ the sum is chi-square with $2N$ degrees of freedom; under $H_1$ it is noncentral
chi-square. It needs several decibels more energy than a coherent detector
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_roc}, right) and, worse, its threshold depends on knowing the noise level
precisely---the weakness that cyclostationary detectors avoid (Section~\ref{sec:ch03:cyclo}).

\subsection{False alarms in many cells}

A detector is rarely run once. A surveillance radar tests perhaps $10^6$ range--Doppler cells a
second; at $P_{FA}=10^{-6}$ per cell that is one false target per second, which is why radar
designers quote false-alarm \emph{rates} and set per-cell probabilities of $10^{-6}$ to $10^{-10}$. Because
the noise and clutter level is never known exactly, practical radars use \emph{constant false-alarm
rate} (CFAR) detectors that estimate the local noise level from neighbouring cells and scale the
threshold accordingly.

\begin{inpractice}[Acquisition: GPS, Wi-Fi and 5G random access]
A GPS receiver searching for a satellite without assistance tests 1023 code phases (in half-chip
steps, 2046) times a few tens of Doppler bins, of order $10^5$ cells. To keep the probability of a
false acquisition over the whole search to a few per cent, the per-cell $P_{FA}$ must be near
$10^{-7}$, and with the signal at $C/N_0=40$\,dB-Hz a 1\,ms coherent integration gives only about
$10$\,dB of $E/N_0$---not enough. Receivers therefore integrate coherently for several milliseconds
and then noncoherently over many blocks, and verify each detection before declaring lock
(Chapter~\ref{ch:spreadspectrum}). A Wi-Fi receiver runs a short-preamble autocorrelation detector
continuously and must not fire on noise more than occasionally, because each false trigger occupies
the receiver; it confirms with the long preamble. A 5G gNB searches for random-access preambles in
every PRACH occasion with a threshold set for a specified false-alarm probability (the
performance requirements in TS 38.104 specify a false-alarm probability of $10^{-3}$ or less and a
detection probability of at least 99\% at a stated SNR). Each is a Neyman--Pearson design.
\end{inpractice}

\section{Estimating parameters in noise}
\label{sec:ch03:estimation}
%======================================================================

Detection asks \emph{whether}; estimation asks \emph{how much}. A receiver must estimate the carrier
frequency and phase, the symbol timing, the channel gain and the noise level before it can
demodulate a single bit, and Chapters~\ref{ch:sync} and~\ref{ch:equalization} are largely about
how. Here are the principles.

\subsection{Maximum likelihood and MMSE}

An \emph{estimator} $\hat\theta(\vect r)$ is any function of the observations. Its quality is
measured by its bias $\E[\hat\theta]-\theta$ and its mean-square error
$\mathrm{MSE}=\E[(\hat\theta-\theta)^2]=\text{variance}+\text{bias}^2$. Two philosophies dominate.

The \term{maximum-likelihood} (ML) estimator treats $\theta$ as an unknown constant and chooses the
value that makes the observations most probable, $\hat\theta_{\text{ML}}=\arg\max_\theta p(\vect r;\theta)$.
For a constant level $A$ in white Gaussian noise, $r_k=A+n_k$, the likelihood is maximised by the
sample mean $\bar r=\frac1N\sum r_k$, with variance $\sigma^2/N$. For a complex tone of unknown
frequency in white noise, the ML estimate is the location of the peak of the periodogram---which is
why an FFT followed by peak-picking and interpolation is the standard coarse frequency estimator.

The \term{minimum mean-square error} (MMSE) estimator treats $\theta$ as random with a known prior and
minimises the average MSE; the answer is the posterior mean, $\hat\theta=\E[\theta\mid\vect r]$. With a
Gaussian prior of variance $\sigma_A^2$ on the level $A$ above, it is
$\hat A=\frac{\sigma_A^2}{\sigma_A^2+\sigma^2/N}\,\bar r$: the sample mean shrunk towards the prior mean,
strongly when data are few and hardly at all when they are many (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_crb}, right).
Restricting the MMSE estimator to be linear gives the \emph{linear MMSE} estimator, which needs only
second-order statistics; applied to a stationary signal in stationary noise, it is the
\term{Wiener filter}, $H(f)=S_s(f)/(S_s(f)+S_n(f))$, which passes frequencies where the signal dominates
and suppresses those where the noise does. The MMSE equaliser of Chapter~\ref{ch:equalization} and
the LMMSE channel estimator of OFDM receivers are Wiener filters.

\subsection{The Cram\'er--Rao bound}

How good can an unbiased estimator be? The \term{Cram\'er--Rao bound} (CRB) answers:
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{var}(\hat\theta)\ \ge\ \frac{1}{I(\theta)},\qquad
I(\theta)=-\E\!\left[\frac{\partial^2\ln p(\vect r;\theta)}{\partial\theta^2}\right],
\label{eq:ch03:crb}
\end{equation}
where $I(\theta)$ is the \emph{Fisher information}: the curvature of the log-likelihood, a measure of how
sharply the data pin down the parameter. For the level in Gaussian noise, $I=N/\sigma^2$, so the sample
mean, with variance $\sigma^2/N$, is \emph{efficient}: it meets the bound. For the frequency $f$ (in
cycles per sample) of a complex tone with SNR $A^2/\sigma^2$ observed for $N$ samples, Rife and Boorstyn
(1974) showed that
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{var}(\hat f)\ \ge\ \frac{6}{(2\pi)^2\,\mathrm{SNR}\,N(N^2-1)} .
\label{eq:ch03:crbfreq}
\end{equation}
The variance falls as $N^{-3}$, not $N^{-1}$: a longer observation both collects more energy and
measures a phase that has rotated further. Doubling the length of a frequency-estimation preamble
reduces the RMS error by a factor of $2^{3/2}\approx2.8$.

The CRB is a small-error bound and ML estimators achieve it asymptotically---at high SNR or long
observation. At low SNR something else happens: the periodogram's peak is occasionally captured by a
noise peak far from the true frequency, the error variance jumps by orders of magnitude, and the
estimator departs from the bound (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_crb}, left). This \term{threshold effect} is
universal in nonlinear estimation---the same phenomenon as the FM threshold of
Chapter~\ref{ch:analog} and the cycle slips of a phase-locked loop in Chapter~\ref{ch:sync}---and it is
why synchronisation algorithms are designed to work well above their threshold.

\mdcfig{ch03_crb}{Estimation in noise. Left: mean-square error of the ML frequency estimate (peak of
a finely interpolated periodogram) of a complex tone, $N=64$ samples, against the Cram\'er--Rao bound.
Above about 0\,dB SNR per sample the estimator is efficient; below, outliers create a threshold.
Right: estimating a level in noise. The sample mean meets the CRB $\sigma^2/N$; with a Gaussian prior
(variance 0.25) the MMSE estimator does better when $N$ is small, by shrinking towards the prior.}

\begin{keyidea}[Energy decides; the bound tells you how close you are]
Both detection and estimation performance are governed by the total signal energy relative to
$N_0$---$2E/N_0$ for detection, $N\cdot\mathrm{SNR}$ for estimating a level---not by the SNR per sample.
A weak signal observed for a long time is as detectable and as measurable as a strong one observed
briefly, provided the receiver can integrate coherently. The Cram\'er--Rao bound gives the target
for any estimator before it is designed; a synchronisation algorithm within a decibel of the
bound is finished.
\end{keyidea}

\section{The link budget}
\label{sec:ch03:linkbudget}
%======================================================================

\subsection{The Friis transmission equation}

Every piece of this chapter now comes together. A transmitter of power $P_t$ feeding an antenna of
gain $G_t$ produces, at distance $d$ in free space, a power flux density $P_tG_t/4\pi d^2$\,W/m$^2$.
A receiving antenna of gain $G_r$ has an effective area $A_e=G_r\lambda^2/4\pi$, so it collects
\begin{equation}
P_r=P_tG_tG_r\left(\frac{\lambda}{4\pi d}\right)^2,
\qquad
L_{\text{fs}}=\left(\frac{4\pi d}{\lambda}\right)^2 .
\label{eq:ch03:friistx}
\end{equation}
This is the Friis transmission equation (Friis, 1946). The free-space path loss $L_{\text{fs}}$
appears to grow with frequency, but that is an artefact of using antennas of fixed gain: an antenna
of fixed \emph{area} $A$ has gain $4\pi A/\lambda^2$, so between two dishes of fixed size the received
power actually \emph{rises} with frequency. This is why point-to-point microwave links and satellites
use high frequencies and large dishes, and why a small phone antenna, whose gain is fixed at
about 0\,dBi whatever the frequency, collects less power at millimetre wave. In decibels, the link
budget is a sum:
\begin{equation}
P_r\,[\mathrm{dBm}]=P_t+G_t+G_r-L_{\text{fs}}-L_{\text{other}},\qquad
L_{\text{fs}}\,[\mathrm{dB}]=32.45+20\log_{10}f_{\mathrm{MHz}}+20\log_{10}d_{\mathrm{km}}.
\label{eq:ch03:fspl}
\end{equation}

\subsection{From received power to \texorpdfstring{$C/N_0$}{C/N0}}

Dividing the received power by the noise density $N_0=kT_{\text{sys}}$ gives the form used by
satellite engineers, with the transmitter, path and receiver separated:
\begin{equation}
\frac{C}{N_0}=\mathrm{EIRP}-L_{\text{fs}}-L_{\text{other}}+\frac{G_r}{T_{\text{sys}}}-k\quad\text{[dB-Hz]},
\qquad k=-228.6\ \mathrm{dBW/K/Hz},
\label{eq:ch03:cn0}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{EIRP}=P_tG_t$ is the effective isotropic radiated power (in dBW) and
$G_r/T_{\text{sys}}$ is in dB/K. Chapter~\ref{ch:satellite} develops this equation with atmospheric
losses, rain and interference; Chapter~\ref{ch:channels} replaces free space by the empirical
path-loss models of terrestrial radio.

\begin{worked}[A satellite TV downlink]
A geostationary satellite transmits a 27.5\,MBd DVB-S2 carrier at 12\,GHz with an EIRP of 52\,dBW
towards a home 38\,000\,km away (the slant range is longer than the 35\,786\,km altitude for any
receiver not directly beneath the satellite). The wavelength is 2.5\,cm and the free-space loss is
$20\log_{10}(4\pi\times3.8\times10^7/0.025)=205.6$\,dB. A 60\,cm dish with 65\% efficiency has gain
$10\log_{10}[0.65(\pi\times0.6/0.025)^2]=35.7$\,dBi; with an LNB and a clear sky,
$T_{\text{sys}}\approx120$\,K (20.8\,dBK), so $G/T=14.9$\,dB/K. Allowing 0.5\,dB of atmospheric loss,
\[
C/N_0=52-205.6-0.5+14.9+228.6=89.4\ \text{dB-Hz},
\]
\[
E_s/N_0=89.4-10\log_{10}(27.5\times10^6)=15.0\ \text{dB}.
\]
DVB-S2 QPSK rate 3/4 needs an $E_s/N_0$ of about 4\,dB (ETSI EN 302 307), so this carrier could use a
higher-order mode---8PSK or 16APSK---and still keep several decibels of margin for rain, which at Ku-band
both attenuates the signal and, by~\eqref{eq:ch03:lossy}, raises $T_{\text{sys}}$.
\end{worked}

\subsection{Margins}

A real link budget adds losses for cables, polarisation mismatch, rain, walls, body
blockage and, above all, \emph{fading}. Multipath fading can drop the received power 20--30\,dB
below its average for a small fraction of the time (Section~\ref{sec:ch03:complex} and
Chapter~\ref{ch:channels}), so the budget must include a \emph{fade margin} chosen for the
required availability. Interference adds to noise; when it is noise-like, the budget uses the
signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio (SINR) and an \emph{interference margin} (the noise rise).
The final comparison is between the received $E_b/N_0$ or SNR and the value the modem needs, plus
margin. Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_linkbudget} draws an indoor Wi-Fi budget as a waterfall: 20\,dBm
from the access point, antenna gains of 3\,dBi, about 98\,dB of path loss at 50\,m with a
path-loss exponent of 3 at 5.5\,GHz (free space to 1\,m, then 30\,dB per decade), and an 8\,dB wall, leaves
about $-80$\,dBm---14\,dB above the noise
floor of a 20\,MHz receiver with 7\,dB noise figure. That supports 16-QAM with moderate
coding (Chapter~\ref{ch:wifi}).

\mdcfig{ch03_linkbudget}{A link budget drawn as a waterfall. Gains lift the level, losses lower it;
the gap between the received power and the noise floor is the SNR available to the modem.}

\begin{pitfall}[Margins are statistics, not safety factors]
Every margin in a link budget is a statement about a probability: a 10\,dB Rayleigh fade margin
gives about 90\% availability at a point (Section~\ref{sec:ch03:complex}), an 8.7\,dB shadowing margin
a stated coverage probability (Chapter~\ref{ch:channels}), a rain margin an annual availability
(Chapter~\ref{ch:satellite}). Adding margins chosen independently for the worst case of each
impairment over-designs the link; omitting the statistics behind them makes the budget impossible to
defend. Write down, for each margin, the event it protects against and the probability it buys.
\end{pitfall}

\begin{history}[Talking to Voyager]
Voyager~1, launched in 1977, is now more than 160 times as far from the Sun as the Earth
is. It transmits roughly 20\,W at 8.4\,GHz from a 3.7\,m dish, and its signal is received by
the 70\,m antennas of NASA's Deep Space Network, whose cryogenic receivers have system noise
temperatures of a few tens of kelvin. At more than $2.4\times10^{10}$\,km, the free-space
path loss exceeds 300\,dB, and the received power is of order $10^{-19}$\,W---about a million
times weaker than the weakest signal a phone can decode. The link still carries 160\,b/s of
science data, thanks to high antenna gains, very low noise temperatures, a narrow bandwidth and
powerful concatenated error-correcting codes---a convolutional code and a Reed--Solomon code, the
same pair of codes that protected digital television for a generation
(Chapter~\ref{ch:classiccodes}). Every term of Equation~\eqref{eq:ch03:friistx} and every kelvin of
Equation~\eqref{eq:ch03:tsys} has been pushed to its limit.
\end{history}

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:ch03:summary}
%======================================================================

\begin{itemize}
\item Noise is described by probability. Moments have electrical meanings (mean = DC, variance = AC
power); characteristic functions turn sums into products; transformations and Bayes' rule underlie
every detector and soft-decision decoder. Uncorrelated is not independent, except for jointly
Gaussian variables.
\item The Gaussian is everywhere because of the central limit theorem and is the worst-case noise
because it maximises entropy. The CLT converges slowly in the tails, so few-term impairments are not
Gaussian where error rates live.
\item $\Q(x)$ is the Gaussian tail. Remember $\Q^{-1}(10^{-3})=3.09$, $\Q^{-1}(10^{-6})=4.75$,
$\Q^{-1}(10^{-9})=6.0$; bound it with $\tfrac12e^{-x^2/2}$ or $\phi(x)/x$; use Craig's form to average
over fading.
\item Circular complex Gaussian noise has a uniform phase, a Rayleigh envelope and an exponential
power; a constant plus such noise has a Rice envelope; sums of $L$ powers are chi-square with $2L$
degrees of freedom, the root of diversity.
\item A WSS process is described by its autocorrelation and PSD, a Fourier pair (Wiener--Khinchin).
Filters shape the PSD by $|H(f)|^2$; the noise-equivalent bandwidth is the right bandwidth for noise.
White noise sampled at $f_s$ has variance proportional to $f_s$.
\item Bandpass noise has independent, identically distributed I and Q components when its spectrum is
symmetric; watch the factors of two between passband, I/Q and complex-envelope PSDs.
\item Modulated signals are cyclostationary; their spectral correlation reveals symbol rate and
carrier offset even far below the noise, and underlies synchronisation and spectrum sensing.
\item Thermal noise is $kTB$, $-174$\,dBm/Hz at 290\,K, derived by Nyquist from thermodynamics and
corrected by Planck's law at high frequency and low temperature. Shot noise is $2qI$; flicker noise
rises as $1/f$; man-made and galactic noise dominate below about 1\,GHz; the sky is cold between 1 and
10\,GHz.
\item Noise figure and noise temperature describe added noise: $T_e=(F-1)T_0$; a passive loss $L$ at
$T_0$ has $F=L$; cascades follow Friis, so the first stage and any loss ahead of it dominate. Measure
with the Y-factor method.
\item Sensitivity $=-174+10\log_{10}B+\mathrm{NF}+\mathrm{SNR}_{\text{req}}$. Keep $C/N_0$, $E_s/N_0$, $E_b/N_0$
and SNR straight with the bandwidths and the code rate; in simulation, remember the samples per symbol.
\item Detection is a likelihood-ratio test; Neyman--Pearson sets the threshold by the false-alarm
rate; the ROC summarises the trade-off; the deflection $\sqrt{2E/N_0}$ decides performance. Estimation
is bounded by the Cram\'er--Rao bound, with a threshold at low SNR.
\item The link budget combines EIRP, path loss, $G/T$ and Boltzmann's constant into $C/N_0$, and margins
turn it into a probability of success.
\end{itemize}
