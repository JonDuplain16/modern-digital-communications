\chapter{Random Signals and Noise}
\label{ch:noise}

\begin{chapterintro}
Stand in the middle of a crowded party and try to follow a friend who is whispering to you from
across the room. You hear the clink of glasses, forty conversations, a song you half recognise,
and somewhere inside all of it a voice you know. You catch most of the words. When the room gets
louder you lean in, ask them to repeat, guess from context, or give up. Without knowing it you are
doing everything a radio receiver does: estimating how loud the crowd is, deciding whether a sound
was a word or just the room, and trading speed for reliability.

That crowd is \emph{noise}, and this chapter is its biography. If there were no noise, a single
sample of a voltage could carry an unlimited number of bits and communication engineering would
be trivial. Noise is what makes the subject hard---and interesting. We start with the language
needed to talk about it: a working engineer's refresher on probability, the Gaussian bell curve
and why nature loves it, and the Q~function that sits inside nearly every error-rate formula in
this book. From complex noise we meet the Rayleigh and Rice laws that return as fading. Then we
study noise as a waveform: random processes, spectra, filtered and bandpass noise, and the
hidden heartbeat that lets a receiver find a signal buried far beneath the noise.

The middle of the chapter is physics: every warm resistor whispering $kTB$, the hail of
electrons that makes shot noise, the crackle of lightning and spark plugs, and the faint hiss of
the galaxy and of the Big Bang itself, discovered by engineers who were simply trying to account
for every kelvin in their receivers. The last third is engineering: noise figure and noise
temperature, the Friis cascade, how to measure them, receiver sensitivity, the bookkeeping of
SNR, $E_b/N_0$ and $C/N_0$, detecting and measuring signals in noise, and the link budget---the
one calculation that decides whether a system will work at all, from a Wi-Fi access point to a
spacecraft at the edge of the solar system.
\end{chapterintro}

\section{The floor under everything}
\label{sec:ch03:floor}
%======================================================================

In 1926 John B.~Johnson, a physicist at Bell Telephone Laboratories, was trying to make
amplifiers quieter. However carefully he built them, a faint irreducible hiss remained at the
output. Johnson noticed that it depended on the resistance at the amplifier's input and on its
temperature, and on nothing else: carbon, metal film or a tube of salt water, it made no
difference. In 1928 he published the measurements, and in the same issue of the \emph{Physical
Review} his colleague Harry Nyquist explained them from thermodynamics. Every conductor above
absolute zero produces noise, simply because its electrons are warm and jiggling. That noise is
not a defect of any particular device. It sets a floor below which no receiver can hear.

Every other impairment in this book---distortion, interference, fading---can in principle be
designed away. Noise cannot. It can only be \emph{outwitted}: by using more power, more
bandwidth, more antennas, longer observation, or cleverer codes. Shannon's capacity formula
(Chapter~\ref{ch:infotheory}), $C=B\log_2(1+\mathrm{SNR})$, says the same thing in one line: the
signal-to-noise ratio, not the signal power, is what limits communication.

\mdcfig[0.82]{ch03_floor_ladder}{A ladder of weakness. The powers that real receivers work with,
on a logarithmic scale spanning twenty orders of magnitude, against the thermal noise floor
(dashed). A phone at the edge of a cell works with about $10^{-13}$\,W; a GPS receiver tracks
signals \emph{below} the noise in its bandwidth; NASA's Deep Space Network hears Voyager~1 at a
power of order $10^{-19}$\,W, only a few dozen times $kT_0$ in one hertz.}

It is worth pausing on just how small that floor is and how close to it real systems work
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_floor_ladder}). A mobile phone at the edge of a cell decodes a signal of
around $10^{-13}$\,W---a ten-millionth of a millionth of a watt. A GPS receiver tracks satellites
whose signals arrive at about $10^{-16}$\,W, well \emph{below} the thermal noise in their
bandwidth. The Deep Space Network hears Voyager~1 at around $10^{-19}$\,W. If that last number is
hard to feel, try this: it would take the Voyager signal, collected by a 70-metre dish, longer
than the age of the universe to deliver the energy needed to warm a cup of tea by one degree.
Each of these feats is a negotiation with noise, and each was designed with the tools of this
chapter.

\begin{analogy}[Noise is the hiss of a crowded party]
Think of the signal as a friend's voice and the noise as the babble of the crowd
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_party}). How well you understand depends on the \emph{ratio} of voice to
babble, not on how loud your friend is in absolute terms---a shout at a rock concert is harder to
follow than a whisper in a library. Forty voices added together sound like a featureless hiss
with no single voice standing out, which is exactly why noise looks Gaussian
(Section~\ref{sec:ch03:gauss}). And a trained ear can still pick out a familiar voice well below
the crowd's level, because it knows what to listen for---the matched filter and the despreading
receiver of later chapters. The analogy's limit: party babble is made of signals that carry
information; true thermal noise carries none, which makes it, as we shall see, the hardest noise
of all to beat.
\end{analogy}

\mdcfig{ch03_party}{The crowded party in numbers. Left: a whispered word (a short tone burst).
Centre: the crowd, forty independent ``voices'' of random pitch and rhythm added together---already
indistinguishable from random noise. Right: what reaches the ear, with the whisper about 3\,dB
weaker than the crowd. The signal is all there; the receiver's job is to dig it out.}

To reason about noise we need two languages. The first is \emph{probability}: noise is
random, so we describe it by distributions, averages and correlations, and we design receivers
that make the best decision on average. The second is \emph{physics}: noise has causes, and
knowing them tells us how large it is, how it depends on temperature, frequency and current, and
how to minimise it. The chapter alternates between the two and ends where they meet, in the noise
figure, the sensitivity specification and the link budget. Table~\ref{tab:ch03:roadmap} is a map
of where each idea reappears, and Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_timeline} shows how the ideas arrived.

\section{Probability for communication engineers}
\label{sec:ch03:prob}
%======================================================================

Why does a radio engineer care whether two numbers are ``uncorrelated'' or ``independent''? Because
the difference has cost real receivers several decibels, and because the receivers that win are
the ones that think in probabilities. This section is a refresher, not a first course. It collects
the results we use again and again, each illustrated with something from a real receiver. If you
know your probability well, skim it; if you learned it once and forgot it, do not, because the rest
of the book takes it for granted.

\subsection{Random variables, distributions and densities}

A \term{random variable} $X$ is a number whose value is decided by the outcome of a random
experiment: the voltage on a resistor at an instant, the number of bit errors in a packet, the
phase of a received carrier. We describe it by its \term{cumulative distribution function} (CDF)
\begin{equation}
F_X(x)=P(X\le x),
\end{equation}
which climbs from 0 to 1 as $x$ goes from left to right. For a continuous variable the slope
$p_X(x)=dF_X/dx$ is the \term{probability density function} (pdf): probability per unit of $x$, so
that $P(a<X\le b)=\int_a^b p_X(x)\,dx$. For a discrete variable we use the probability mass
function $P(X=x_k)$. Mixed variables, with both a density and point masses, occur in practice: the
output of an amplifier that clips is continuous inside the rails and has a lump of probability
sitting at each rail.

Four distributions do most of the work in this book. The \emph{Bernoulli} variable, 1 with
probability $p$ and 0 otherwise, models one bit error. The \emph{binomial}, the number of errors in
$n$ independent bits, has $P(K=k)=\binom nk p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$; it tells you, for example, that a
1000-bit packet on a link with bit-error rate $10^{-4}$ survives intact with probability
$(1-10^{-4})^{1000}\approx0.905$---one packet in ten is lost to a seemingly tiny error rate. The
\emph{uniform} distribution models a carrier phase about which nothing is known, and the error of
a fine quantiser. The \emph{Gaussian} models noise, and occupies most of this chapter.

\subsection{Moments: mean, power and variance}

Here is a pleasant surprise: the abstract moments of probability theory are things you can read
off an oscilloscope (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_moments}). The \term{expectation} of a function $g(X)$
is the probability-weighted average $\E[g(X)]=\int g(x)p_X(x)\,dx$. The mean $\mu=\E[X]$, the mean
square $\E[X^2]$ and the variance $\sigma^2=\E[(X-\mu)^2]=\E[X^2]-\mu^2$ all have electrical
meanings. If $X$ is a voltage across a 1\,$\Omega$ resistor, $\E[X^2]$ is the average power,
$\mu^2$ is the power of the DC component and $\sigma^2$ is the power of the fluctuations---the AC
power. The standard deviation $\sigma$ is the RMS value of the fluctuation, which is exactly what a
true-RMS voltmeter on AC coupling reads.

\mdcside[0.44]{ch03_moments}{Moments on a scope. The mean is the DC level, $\sigma$ is the RMS of
the AC part, and the peak sets the crest factor that an amplifier or ADC must survive.}

Higher moments describe the \emph{shape}. The normalised third moment (skewness) measures
lopsidedness. The normalised fourth moment, the \term{kurtosis} $\E[(X-\mu)^4]/\sigma^4$, equals
3 for a Gaussian and is a quick test for impulsive noise: a kurtosis well above 3 means heavy tails
and occasional large spikes, the signature of a car ignition or a switching power supply rather
than of a warm resistor. The ratio of peak to RMS value, the \term{crest factor}, is the amplitude
statistic that decides how much headroom an amplifier or ADC needs; its square is the
peak-to-average power ratio (PAPR) that haunts OFDM transmitters (Chapter~\ref{ch:ofdm}). A
designer who knows only the RMS level of a signal knows how hot the amplifier will run; one who
also knows the crest factor knows how often it will clip. In practice both matter, and a good
measurement campaign reports the mean, the RMS, the crest factor and the full amplitude
histogram, not just a single ``level''.

\subsection{Characteristic and moment-generating functions}

Adding two independent random quantities---noise plus interference, one multipath echo plus
another---\emph{convolves} their densities, which is tedious. There is a transform that turns the
convolution into a product, just as the Fourier transform does for filters. The
\term{characteristic function} $\Phi_X(\omega)=\E[e^{j\omega X}]$ is the Fourier transform of the
pdf (with the sign of the exponent reversed), and the \term{moment-generating function}
$M_X(s)=\E[e^{sX}]$ is its Laplace-transform cousin. They earn their place for three reasons.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Moments by differentiation}: $\E[X^n]=M_X^{(n)}(0)$. For the Gaussian,
$M_X(s)=e^{\mu s+\sigma^2s^2/2}$, from which every moment follows.
\item \emph{Sums become products}: if $X$ and $Y$ are independent, the pdf of $Z=X+Y$ is the
convolution $p_Z=p_X*p_Y$, and therefore $\Phi_Z=\Phi_X\Phi_Y$. Adding noise sources,
interferers or multipath components is multiplication in this domain. The logarithm of the
characteristic function, whose Taylor coefficients are the \term{cumulants}, turns the product
into a sum: cumulants of independent variables add.
\item \emph{Tail bounds}: for any $s>0$, Markov's inequality applied to $e^{sX}$ gives the
\term{Chernoff bound}
\begin{equation}
P(X\ge x)\le e^{-sx}M_X(s),\qquad\text{optimised over }s>0.
\label{eq:ch03:chernoff}
\end{equation}
This single inequality underlies the error-exponent analyses of coding theory
(Chapter~\ref{ch:infotheory}) and the union--Chernoff bounds used to design codes for fading
channels. We apply it to the Gaussian tail in Section~\ref{sec:ch03:qfunc}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Functions of a random variable}

A receiver is a long chain of operations on random variables: squaring, taking magnitudes,
converting to decibels, quantising. Each operation reshapes the distribution, and the rule for how
is pleasingly physical. If $Y=g(X)$ with $g$ differentiable, then
\begin{equation}
p_Y(y)=\sum_{k}\frac{p_X(x_k)}{|g'(x_k)|},\qquad\text{summed over the roots }x_k\text{ of }g(x_k)=y .
\label{eq:ch03:transform}
\end{equation}
In words: probability is conserved, like sand poured through a funnel. Where $g$ is steep it
stretches the axis and spreads the sand thin; where $g$ is flat it piles the sand up. Three
examples recur throughout the book, and Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_transforms} shows all three.

\mdcfig{ch03_transforms}{Three transformations every receiver performs, by simulation (histograms)
and by~\eqref{eq:ch03:transform} (curves). Left: squaring Gaussian noise gives a chi-square density
that piles up near zero. Centre: a sinusoid sampled at random instants spends most of its time near
its peaks---the arcsine law with ``horns'' at both ends. Right: the power of complex Gaussian noise,
expressed in decibels, has a long left tail, so the average of the decibel values sits 2.5\,dB below
the true mean power.}

\paragraph{Square-law detector.} If $X\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$ and $Y=X^2$, the two roots
$\pm\sqrt y$ give $p_Y(y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2 y}}e^{-y/2\sigma^2}$, $y>0$: the chi-square
density with one degree of freedom. The output of a diode power detector driven by noise has
this distribution.

\paragraph{Sinusoid with random phase.} If $\Theta$ is uniform on $[0,2\pi)$ and
$Y=A\cos\Theta$, then $p_Y(y)=1/(\pi\sqrt{A^2-y^2})$ for $|y|<A$. This \emph{arcsine} density,
peaked at the extremes because a sinusoid lingers near its crests and rushes through zero, is the
amplitude distribution of a tone sampled at random instants. It explains why a sine wave is a poor
test signal for an ADC that will digitise noise-like OFDM, and why quantiser histograms of a sine
input have horns at both ends.

\paragraph{Decibels.} Engineers look at power in decibels, $Y=10\log_{10}X$. If $X$ is
exponential with mean 1 (the power of complex Gaussian noise, Section~\ref{sec:ch03:complex}), $Y$
has a \emph{log-exponential} density with a long left tail: deep dips are much more common than
tall peaks, and the mean of $Y$ is $-2.5$\,dB, not 0\,dB. Averaging powers in decibels rather than
in watts is a classic source of a 2.5\,dB bias in measured noise floors; the spectrum analyzer's
``log-average'' detector carries a correction for exactly this reason.

\subsection{Joint distributions, correlation and independence}

Two random variables are described jointly by $p_{XY}(x,y)$; the \emph{marginal}
$p_X(x)=\int p_{XY}(x,y)\,dy$ forgets $Y$, and the \emph{conditional}
$p_{X|Y}(x|y)=p_{XY}(x,y)/p_Y(y)$ describes $X$ once $Y$ is known. They are \term{independent} if
$p_{XY}=p_Xp_Y$: knowing one tells you nothing about the other. Their \term{covariance}
$\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)=\E[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)]$ measures linear association, normalised to the
\emph{correlation coefficient} $\rho=\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)/\sigma_X\sigma_Y\in[-1,1]$.

\mdcside[0.46]{ch03_uncorrelated}{Uncorrelated is not independent. Left: a point on the unit
circle at a random angle---zero correlation, yet knowing $I$ fixes $|Q|$. Right: a Gaussian pair,
where zero correlation really does mean independence.}

Independent variables are always uncorrelated, but the converse is false---and the
counterexample lives inside every phase-modulated signal. Let $\Theta$ be uniform and
$I=\cos\Theta$, $Q=\sin\Theta$. Then $\E[IQ]=\tfrac12\E[\sin2\Theta]=0$: $I$ and $Q$ are
uncorrelated. Yet they are completely dependent, since $I^2+Q^2=1$; know one and the other is
fixed up to its sign (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_uncorrelated}). Uncorrelated means ``no \emph{linear}
relation''; independence means ``no relation at all''. The distinction matters whenever a
receiver applies a nonlinearity---a squarer, a limiter, a decision device---and it is one reason the
Gaussian is so convenient: for \emph{jointly Gaussian} variables, and only for them among the
common models, uncorrelated does imply independent. When an engineer says ``the I and Q noise
samples are independent'', the Gaussian assumption is doing quiet but essential work.

For a random vector $\vect x$ the second-order description is the mean vector and the
\term{covariance matrix} $\vect C=\E[(\vect x-\bm{\mu})(\vect x-\bm{\mu})^{\mathsf H}]$, which is
Hermitian and positive semidefinite. A linear transformation $\vect y=\vect A\vect x$ has
covariance $\vect A\vect C\vect A^{\mathsf H}$. Choosing $\vect A=\vect C^{-1/2}$ makes the
covariance the identity: this is \term{whitening}, the operation behind the whitened matched filter
of Chapter~\ref{ch:baseband} and the noise-whitening steps of MIMO receivers in
Chapter~\ref{ch:mimo}. Whitening is the mathematical version of tidying a room before searching
it: once the noise is the same in every direction, the best search is a simple one.

\subsection{Bayes' rule and the logic of decisions}

Every receiver is a detective. It sees an effect (the received sample) and must infer the cause
(the transmitted symbol). The detective's tool is \term{Bayes' rule},
\begin{equation}
P(s\mid r)=\frac{p(r\mid s)\,P(s)}{p(r)},\qquad p(r)=\sum_{s'}p(r\mid s')P(s').
\label{eq:ch03:bayes}
\end{equation}
Here $P(s)$ is the \emph{prior} probability of symbol $s$---what we believed before looking;
$p(r\mid s)$ is the \emph{likelihood}---the channel model, usually Gaussian noise around the
transmitted point; and $P(s\mid r)$ is the \emph{posterior}---what we believe after looking.
Choosing the symbol with the largest posterior is the \term{maximum a posteriori} (MAP) rule, which
minimises the probability of error; with equal priors it reduces to the
\term{maximum-likelihood} (ML) rule. For a binary decision the natural statistic is the
\term{log-likelihood ratio}
\begin{equation}
\Lambda(r)=\ln\frac{P(s=+1\mid r)}{P(s=-1\mid r)}=\ln\frac{p(r\mid+1)}{p(r\mid-1)}+\ln\frac{P(+1)}{P(-1)},
\label{eq:ch03:llr}
\end{equation}
whose sign is the decision and whose magnitude is the confidence. For $r=\pm A+n$ with Gaussian
$n$ of variance $\sigma^2$, the likelihood term is simply $2Ar/\sigma^2$: the LLR is a scaled copy
of the received sample (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_llr}). These ``soft'' values are the currency of every
modern decoder---Viterbi, turbo, LDPC and polar (Chapters~\ref{ch:classiccodes}
and~\ref{ch:moderncodes}). A sample that lands near zero says ``I'm not sure''; a decoder that
listens to that hesitation gains about 2\,dB over one that is only told ``$+1$'' or ``$-1$''.

\mdcfig[0.5]{ch03_llr}{The log-likelihood ratio for binary antipodal signalling in Gaussian noise
is a straight line through the origin: its sign is the hard decision and its size the confidence
that soft-decision decoders exploit.}

\begin{worked}[Base rates and false alarms]
A Wi-Fi receiver listens for packet preambles. Suppose a correlator detects a real preamble with
probability $P_D=0.99$ and fires on noise alone with probability $P_{FA}=10^{-3}$ per test, and
suppose that in a quiet channel only one test in $10^4$ actually contains a preamble. When the
detector fires, what is the probability that a packet is really there? By Bayes' rule,
\[
P(\text{packet}\mid\text{fire})=\frac{0.99\times10^{-4}}{0.99\times10^{-4}+10^{-3}\times(1-10^{-4})}\approx0.090 .
\]
Ninety-one per cent of the alarms are false, even though the detector looks excellent on paper
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_bayes_alarm}). The cure is either a lower $P_{FA}$ (a higher threshold, at
the cost of some $P_D$) or a second, independent check---which is exactly why packet detectors
confirm a correlation peak with a second preamble field before committing the receiver. The same
arithmetic governs medical tests, radar and intrusion detection. We return to the trade-off between
$P_D$ and $P_{FA}$ in Section~\ref{sec:ch03:detection}.
\end{worked}

\mdcfig[0.85]{ch03_bayes_alarm}{The base-rate trap, drawn to scale. Each grey dot is one test of
the preamble detector. Among 10\,000 tests only one contains a real preamble (green), but about ten
fire on noise alone (red). A detector that is 99\% sensitive and 99.9\% specific is still wrong
about most of its alarms when real events are rare.}

\section{The Gaussian distribution}
\label{sec:ch03:gauss}
%======================================================================

Drop a few thousand steel balls through a board studded with pegs. Each ball bounces left or right
at every peg, as randomly as a coin toss, and lands in one of a row of bins at the bottom. You
cannot predict where any one ball will go. But the heap that builds up is always the same shape:
a smooth, symmetric hump, high in the middle and falling away to almost nothing at the edges
(Figures~\ref{fig:ch03_galton} and~\ref{fig:ch03_galton_sim}). That hump is the bell curve, the
Gaussian, and the device is Francis Galton's \emph{quincunx} or bean machine. It is the best
explanation ever built for why noise looks the way it does.

\mdcpairany{ch03_galton}{A modern Galton board. Every ball makes a dozen random left-or-right
bounces; the pile they build is close to a Gaussian, printed on the board for comparison.}{ch03_galton_sim}{The
same experiment in software: four sample paths through twelve rows of pegs, and the histogram of
4000 balls against the Gaussian curve predicted by the central limit theorem.}

\subsection{Definition and properties}

The Gaussian (normal) random variable $X\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ has density
\begin{equation}
p_X(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\,e^{-(x-\mu)^2/2\sigma^2}.
\label{eq:ch03:gauss}
\end{equation}
Read it as ``the probability of a deviation falls off as the exponential of its \emph{square}'':
moving from one to two standard deviations lowers the density by a factor of about 4.5, but
moving from two to four lowers it by a factor of about 400, and further out the fall becomes
astronomical. A Gaussian random vector $\vect x\in\R^n$ with mean $\bm{\mu}$ and
covariance $\vect C$ has
\begin{equation}
p(\vect x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}\det(\vect C)^{1/2}}\exp\!\left(-\tfrac12(\vect x-\bm{\mu})^{\mathsf T}\vect C^{-1}(\vect x-\bm{\mu})\right).
\label{eq:ch03:mvgauss}
\end{equation}
Four properties make it both the natural model and the convenient one.
\begin{enumerate}
\item It is completely specified by its mean and covariance---by second-order statistics, which are
exactly what we can measure and propagate through linear systems.
\item Any linear combination of jointly Gaussian variables is Gaussian. Noise that passes through
filters, mixers (which are linear in the signal path), FFTs and matched filters stays Gaussian;
only its covariance changes.
\item Uncorrelated jointly Gaussian variables are independent, because the covariance matrix
in~\eqref{eq:ch03:mvgauss} becomes diagonal and the density factorises.
\item The ML decision in Gaussian noise is a minimum-\emph{distance} decision: maximising
\eqref{eq:ch03:mvgauss} over $\bm{\mu}$ means minimising $\|\vect x-\bm{\mu}\|^2$ (for white
noise). Signal-space geometry (Chapter~\ref{ch:modulation}) is Gaussian noise made visible.
\end{enumerate}

\mdcfig{ch03_clt}{The central limit theorem in action: normalised sums of $n$ independent uniform
random variables (histograms) approach the Gaussian density (red curves). By $n=4$ the eye can
barely tell the difference.}

\begin{history}[From gamblers to Galton's bean machine]
The bell curve was first found by a mathematician helping gamblers. In 1733 Abraham de Moivre, a
French Huguenot refugee in London who earned part of his living advising on games of chance,
showed that the binomial probabilities of many coin tosses are closely approximated by a smooth
curve of the form $e^{-x^2/2}$. Carl Friedrich Gauss made the curve famous in 1809 as the law of
errors in astronomical measurements, which is why engineers call it Gaussian; Laplace proved an
early version of the central limit theorem soon after. In 1889 Francis Galton, Darwin's
half-cousin, described his \emph{quincunx}---a board of pins through which shot pours into
bins---in his book \emph{Natural Inheritance}, as a mechanical demonstration that many small
independent causes add up to the normal law. Galton wrote admiringly of the law reigning serenely
amid the wildest confusion. Engineers would meet the same order sixty years later in the hiss of
every amplifier.
\end{history}

\subsection{Why the Gaussian is everywhere: the central limit theorem}

The Galton board works because each ball's final position is the \emph{sum} of many small,
independent nudges. The \term{central limit theorem} (CLT) makes this precise: the normalised sum
of $n$ independent, identically distributed variables with finite variance tends to a Gaussian,
\emph{whatever} the distribution of the individual terms:
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\mu)\ \xrightarrow{\ n\to\infty\ }\ \mathcal N(0,1).
\end{equation}
The proof is a one-liner with characteristic functions. The characteristic function of each
normalised term is $1-\omega^2/2n+o(1/n)$, so that of the sum is
$(1-\omega^2/2n+\dots)^n\to e^{-\omega^2/2}$, the Gaussian's. Independence turned the convolution
of $n$ densities into a product, and the product forgot everything but the variance. Versions of the
theorem (Lindeberg's, Lyapunov's) relax ``identically distributed'' to ``no single term dominates''.

Thermal noise is the summed effect of an astronomical number of electrons; the interference from
many distant transmitters, the error of a quantiser with many levels, the sum of many multipath
components and the samples of an OFDM signal built from hundreds of subcarriers all tend to Gaussian
for the same reason. Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_clt} shows how quickly the convergence happens near the
centre: the sum of just four uniform random variables is hard to tell from a Gaussian by eye.

The tails are another matter, and the tails are where error probabilities live.
Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_clt_tails} plots the exact probability that the normalised sum exceeds $x$
standard deviations. The sum of $n$ uniforms is bounded---it can never exceed $\sqrt{3n}$ standard
deviations---so its tail falls off a cliff where the Gaussian's continues. Twelve uniforms (the old
trick for generating Gaussian samples) match the Gaussian to about $10^{-3}$, then fall far below
it. Sums of coin flips converge more evenly but in steps. The quantitative statement is the
\emph{Berry--Esseen theorem}: the CDF error is at most a constant times
$\E|X-\mu|^3/(\sigma^3\sqrt n)$, an \emph{absolute} error. At a tail probability of $10^{-9}$ an
absolute error of $10^{-3}$ is meaningless.

\mdcfig{ch03_clt_tails}{Convergence of the CLT in the tails. Left: exact tail probabilities of
normalised sums of $n$ uniform variables, which are bounded and therefore fall away from the
Gaussian $Q(x)$ beyond a few standard deviations. Right: sums of $n$ random $\pm1$ values
(binomial), which approach $Q(x)$ in steps. The centre of a distribution becomes Gaussian quickly;
the far tail, where bit errors live, does not.}

\emph{Each term is} to \emph{Exponential}---a badly lopsided distribution---and drag \emph{Terms

\end{tryit}

\begin{pitfall}[The CLT does not reach the tails]
The Gaussian model is excellent for thermal noise, whose ``number of terms'' is of order
$10^{20}$. It can be badly wrong for impairments built from a few terms: interference from two or
three strong neighbouring cells, the ISI from a few residual taps, the sum of a handful of crosstalk
aggressors in a SerDes link. Such sums have \emph{bounded} support, and a Gaussian model then
\emph{overestimates} the error rate---the reason high-speed link engineers compute ``statistical
eyes'' by exact convolution of the ISI distribution rather than by a Gaussian approximation.
Impulsive noise, conversely, has heavier tails than the Gaussian and is \emph{underestimated} by it
(Section~\ref{sec:ch03:impulsive}). Whenever an error rate below $10^{-6}$ is predicted from a
Gaussian assumption, ask how many terms built the noise.
\end{pitfall}

\subsection{Why the Gaussian is the worst noise: maximum entropy}

Here is a paradox. Gaussian noise is the friendliest noise to \emph{analyse}, yet it is the
\emph{hardest} noise to communicate through. The reason is surprise. The differential entropy
$h(X)=-\int p\log_2p\,dx$ measures, in bits, how unpredictable a continuous variable is
(Chapter~\ref{ch:infotheory}). Among all densities with a given variance $\sigma^2$---a given noise
power---the Gaussian has the largest entropy,
\begin{equation}
h(X)\le\tfrac12\log_2\!\left(2\pi e\sigma^2\right),
\label{eq:ch03:maxent}
\end{equation}
with equality only for the Gaussian (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_maxent}). Noise with any structure---a
bounded range, a preferred value, a pattern---leaves a clever receiver something to exploit;
Gaussian noise of the same power leaves nothing.

\mdcside[0.44]{ch03_maxent}{Three noises of identical power. The Gaussian has the highest
entropy---it is the least predictable, and therefore the most damaging to communication.}

\begin{deeper}[The two-line proof of maximum entropy]
Let $g$ be the Gaussian density with the same variance as $p$. Because $\ln g$ is a quadratic in
$x$, the cross-entropy $-\int p\ln g$ depends only on the second moment of $p$, and so equals
$-\int g\ln g=h_g$ (in nats). Then
\[
h_g-h_p=-\int p\ln g+\int p\ln p=\int p\ln\frac{p}{g}\ \ge\ 0,
\]
the non-negativity of relative entropy (the Kullback--Leibler divergence), with equality only when
$p=g$.
\end{deeper}

Maximum entropy has two consequences that frame the whole of information theory. First, among all
noises of a given power, Gaussian noise is the \emph{least predictable} and therefore the most
harmful: the capacity of an additive-noise channel with noise power $N$ is at least
$B\log_2(1+S/N)$, with equality only for Gaussian noise. Designing for Gaussian noise is designing
for the worst case. Second, among all \emph{inputs} of a given power, the Gaussian input maximises
the mutual information---which is why capacity-approaching systems shape their constellations to
look Gaussian (probabilistic shaping, Chapter~\ref{ch:infotheory}). The universe, it seems, both
attacks and defends with the bell curve.

\section{The Q function}
\label{sec:ch03:qfunc}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Definition and values}

A receiver decides between $+A$ and $-A$. Noise nudges every sample one way or the other, and most
nudges are harmless. An error happens only when the noise reaches all the way across the decision
threshold---when it strays deep into the tail of the bell curve (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_q_tail}). So
nearly every error probability in this book is a question of the form: \emph{how far into the tail
must the noise reach to fool you, and how often does it get that far?}

\mdcside[0.44]{ch03_q_tail}{The tail that fools the receiver. Antipodal symbols $\pm A$ in Gaussian
noise: an error occurs when noise carries a $+A$ sample across the threshold. Its probability is the
shaded area, $\Q(A/\sigma)$.}

We give the answer a name. The \term{Q function} is the probability that a zero-mean,
unit-variance Gaussian exceeds $x$:
\begin{equation}
\Q(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_x^\infty e^{-u^2/2}\,du=\tfrac12\operatorname{erfc}\!\left(x/\sqrt2\right).
\label{eq:ch03:q}
\end{equation}
The tail of a general Gaussian is obtained by standardising---measuring the distance to the
threshold in units of the noise's standard deviation: $P(X>a)=\Q\big((a-\mu)/\sigma\big)$. If a
receiver decides between $+A$ and $-A$ corrupted by Gaussian noise of standard deviation $\sigma$,
the error probability is $\Q(A/\sigma)$. Useful identities are $\Q(-x)=1-\Q(x)$, $\Q(0)=\tfrac12$
and $\frac{d}{dx}\Q(x)=-\phi(x)$, where $\phi(x)=e^{-x^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}$ is the standard Gaussian
density.

\begin{pitfall}[Computing tails in software]
Use \texttt{scipy.special.erfc} (or \texttt{scipy.stats.norm.sf}). Never integrate the density
numerically in the tail, and never compute $1-\Phi(x)$ for large $x$: the subtraction of two
numbers that agree to twelve digits loses every significant digit to cancellation, and your
``$10^{-15}$'' comes out as zero or as rounding garbage.
\end{pitfall}

The function falls extremely fast (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_q} and Table~\ref{tab:ch03:q}):
$\Q(3.09)=10^{-3}$, $\Q(4.75)=10^{-6}$, $\Q(6.0)=10^{-9}$. Increasing the argument by about
50\%---a 3.5\,dB increase in signal-to-noise ratio---reduces the error rate by a factor of a
thousand. This steepness is why a working digital link has a ``cliff'': a few decibels separate
perfect reception from none (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_cliff}). Anyone who watched digital television
in a storm has seen it: the picture is perfect, perfect, perfect, then frozen blocks, then nothing,
over a change in signal strength you could not hear on an analog set. It is also why the reach of
a link is so insensitive to the error-rate target: going from $10^{-6}$ to $10^{-9}$ costs only
$20\log_{10}(6.0/4.75)\approx2$\,dB.

\subsection{Bounds}

The Q function has no closed form, so engineers carry a pocketful of bounds. Three cover most
needs.

\paragraph{The Chernoff bound.} Applying~\eqref{eq:ch03:chernoff} to a unit Gaussian, with
$M(s)=e^{s^2/2}$, gives $\Q(x)\le e^{-sx+s^2/2}$, minimised at $s=x$:
\begin{equation}
\Q(x)\le e^{-x^2/2},\qquad x\ge0.
\end{equation}
It captures the exponent exactly but is loose by a factor that grows with $x$.

\paragraph{Craig's formula and the factor of one half.} In 1991 John Craig found an alternative
\emph{exact} expression with finite limits and the argument in the integrand, not the limit:
\begin{equation}
\Q(x)=\frac1\pi\int_0^{\pi/2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{x^2}{2\sin^2\theta}\right)d\theta,\qquad x\ge0.
\label{eq:ch03:craig}
\end{equation}
Bounding the integrand by its value at $\theta=\pi/2$ gives the tighter $\Q(x)\le\tfrac12e^{-x^2/2}$.
More importantly, because $x^2$ now appears in an exponent, averaging $\Q(\sqrt{2\gamma})$ over a
fading distribution of the SNR $\gamma$ turns into an integral of the moment-generating function of
$\gamma$ over a finite range---the basis of the moment-generating-function method for error rates in
fading (Chapter~\ref{ch:channels}).

\mdcfig[0.5]{ch03_craig}{Craig's formula pictured: the integrand over $0\le\theta\le\pi/2$ for three
values of $x$. Each shaded area is exactly $\Q(x)$; replacing the integrand by its largest value, at
$\theta=\pi/2$, gives the bound $\Q(x)\le\tfrac12e^{-x^2/2}$.}

\begin{deeper}[Where Craig's formula comes from]
$\Q(x)$ is the probability that a 2-D standard Gaussian vector $(u_1,u_2)$ lies in the half-plane
$\{u_1>x\}$. In polar coordinates the boundary is the line $r\cos\varphi=x$. For each angle
$|\varphi|<\pi/2$, the radial part of the 2-D Gaussian integrates to
\[
\int_{x/\cos\varphi}^\infty re^{-r^2/2}\,dr=e^{-x^2/2\cos^2\varphi},
\]
and integrating over $\varphi$ with the $1/2\pi$ normalisation, then substituting
$\theta=\pi/2-\varphi$, gives~\eqref{eq:ch03:craig}. The trick---move to two dimensions, where the
Gaussian is circularly symmetric, and integrate the easy radial direction first---is the same one
that evaluates $\int e^{-x^2}dx=\sqrt\pi$.
\end{deeper}

\paragraph{The density bounds.} Integrating by parts gives
\begin{equation}
\frac{x}{1+x^2}\,\phi(x)<\Q(x)<\frac{\phi(x)}{x},\qquad x>0,
\label{eq:ch03:qdensitybounds}
\end{equation}
which sandwich $\Q(x)$ to within 10\% for $x>3$ and to within 3\% for $x>6$. The upper bound
$\phi(x)/x$ is also the leading term of the asymptotic expansion
$\Q(x)\sim\frac{\phi(x)}{x}\big(1-\frac1{x^2}+\frac{3}{x^4}-\cdots\big)$, which diverges for every
fixed $x$ if continued indefinitely but is excellent when truncated early at large $x$. In words:
far out in the tail, the area beyond $x$ is roughly the height of the curve at $x$ divided by $x$,
because the curve is falling so fast that only a sliver of width $1/x$ matters.

\mdcfig{ch03_qapprox}{How good are the bounds and approximations? Left: ratio of four bounds to
$\Q(x)$, in decibels. The density bounds pinch together as $x$ grows; the Chernoff-type bounds have
the right exponent but a growing prefactor error. Right: relative error of three approximations.
B\"orjesson--Sundberg is indistinguishable from zero on this scale.}

\subsection{Approximations}

When a closed form is needed---in a link-budget spreadsheet, a fixed-point receiver or an
analysis---several approximations are available (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_qapprox}). The
B\"orjesson--Sundberg approximation (1979),
\begin{equation}
\Q(x)\approx\frac{\phi(x)}{(1-a)x+a\sqrt{x^2+b}},\qquad a=0.339,\ b=5.510,
\end{equation}
is accurate to about 0.3\% for all $x\ge0$. The two-exponential approximation of Chiani, Dardari
and Simon (2003), $\Q(x)\approx\frac1{12}e^{-x^2/2}+\frac14e^{-2x^2/3}$, is much less accurate but
has the enormous advantage of being a sum of exponentials in $x^2$, so that averages over fading
reduce to sums of moment-generating functions in closed form.

\begin{keyidea}[Think in decibels of argument]
Between error rates of $10^{-3}$ and $10^{-9}$, $\Q(x)$ falls by one decade for every 1.6\,dB (near
$10^{-3}$) down to 0.6\,dB (near $10^{-9}$) increase of $x^2$---that is, of SNR. A receiver that is
``2\,dB worse'' is therefore between one and three decades worse in error rate, and an
``implementation loss'' of half a decibel is far from negligible. Conversely, error-rate
requirements should be argued in decibels of margin, not in factors of ten of probability.
\end{keyidea}

\begin{worked}[How much SNR does $10^{-12}$ cost?]
A 25\,Gb/s optical link with binary signalling needs a bit-error rate of $10^{-12}$ without FEC.
Table~\ref{tab:ch03:q} gives $\Q^{-1}(10^{-12})=7.03$, so the ratio of eye half-opening to RMS noise
must be at least 7.03, an SNR of $20\log_{10}7.03\approx16.9$\,dB at the decision point. At
$10^{-6}$ the requirement is $20\log_{10}4.75\approx13.5$\,dB. Six orders of magnitude cost
3.4\,dB. Alternatively, add a forward-error-correction code that turns an input BER of
$2\times10^{-4}$ (KP4 Reed--Solomon in 100G and 400G Ethernet, Chapter~\ref{ch:classiccodes}) into
an output below $10^{-13}$: the link now needs only $\Q^{-1}(2\times10^{-4})\approx3.54$, an SNR of
11\,dB---nearly 6\,dB saved, which is why every modern high-speed link has FEC.
\end{worked}

\mdcfig[0.5]{ch03_fec_gain}{The SNR needed by binary antipodal signalling as a function of the target
bit-error rate. Going from $10^{-6}$ to $10^{-12}$ costs only 3.4\,dB; letting a KP4 FEC code clean up a
raw $2\times10^{-4}$ saves nearly 6\,dB against an uncoded $10^{-12}$.}

\section{Complex Gaussian noise and its family}
\label{sec:ch03:complex}
%======================================================================

\mdcfig[0.42]{ch03_cn_cloud}{Circularly symmetric complex Gaussian noise (navy) and a rotated copy
(red) are statistically identical: a round cloud has no preferred direction.}

\subsection{Circular symmetry}

Receivers work with complex baseband signals (Section~\ref{sec:complexenvelope}), so their noise is
complex: $n=n_I+jn_Q$, one Gaussian on the I rail and another on the Q rail. When the two parts are
independent, zero-mean Gaussian and of equal variance $\sigma^2$, the noise is \term{circularly
symmetric complex Gaussian}, written $n\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,2\sigma^2)$, with density
\begin{equation}
p(n)=\frac{1}{\pi N}\,e^{-|n|^2/N},\qquad N=\E|n|^2=2\sigma^2 .
\label{eq:ch03:cn}
\end{equation}
The density depends only on $|n|$---picture a round, fuzzy cloud centred on the origin
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_cn_cloud}). It does not change under rotation: $e^{j\theta}n$ has the same
distribution as $n$ for every $\theta$. Two consequences follow immediately. A receiver's carrier
phase offset changes only the signal, never the noise statistics; and the phase of the noise is
uniformly distributed on $[-\pi,\pi)$.

Formally, circular symmetry requires the \emph{pseudo-covariance}
$\E[n^2]=\E[n_I^2]-\E[n_Q^2]+2j\E[n_In_Q]$ to vanish. A receiver with IQ gain or phase imbalance
squashes the round cloud into a tilted ellipse, whose pseudo-covariance is not zero---which is one
way to detect and correct the imbalance (Chapter~\ref{ch:sdr}). If you ever plot the raw noise of a
software radio with no antenna attached and see an ellipse rather than a circle, you are looking at
its IQ imbalance. The same test works on any receiver whose noise you can capture: compute
$\E[n_I^2]$, $\E[n_Q^2]$ and $\E[n_In_Q]$ over a few thousand samples, and the gain and phase errors
of the two rails fall straight out. It is one of the cheapest calibrations in radio engineering,
because the noise that is always present becomes the test signal.

\subsection{Rayleigh, exponential and uniform phase}

Now take the magnitude of that round cloud. The magnitude $R=|n|$ has the \term{Rayleigh
distribution}
\begin{equation}
p_R(r)=\frac{r}{\sigma^2}\,e^{-r^2/2\sigma^2},\qquad r\ge0,
\label{eq:ch03:rayleigh}
\end{equation}
obtained by transforming the joint Gaussian density of $(n_I,n_Q)$ to polar coordinates: the
Jacobian $r$ creates the factor $r$---there is more ``room'' at larger radii, so very small
magnitudes are rare even though the density of the cloud peaks at the origin. The angle is uniform
and independent of $R$. The Rayleigh mean is $\sigma\sqrt{\pi/2}$, its mode is $\sigma$, and its mean
square is $2\sigma^2$. The power $P=|n|^2$ is \term{exponentially distributed},
\begin{equation}
p_P(x)=\frac{1}{\bar P}\,e^{-x/\bar P},\qquad P(P<x)=1-e^{-x/\bar P},\qquad \bar P=2\sigma^2 .
\label{eq:ch03:expo}
\end{equation}
The CDF answers the most important practical question about Rayleigh fading
(Chapter~\ref{ch:channels}): the probability of a fade $10$\,dB below the mean is
$1-e^{-0.1}\approx9.5\%$, $20$\,dB below is about $1\%$ and $30$\,dB below about $0.1\%$---one decade
of probability per 10\,dB of fade depth. Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_fade_time} shows the law at work on a
simulated mobile channel, where the ``noise'' is the sum of dozens of scattered echoes.

\mdcfig{ch03_fade_time}{A Rayleigh-fading signal in time: the sum of 64 scattered paths with a
40\,Hz maximum Doppler shift. The power spends about 10\% of the time more than 10\,dB below its
mean and about 1\% more than 20\,dB below, as the exponential law~\eqref{eq:ch03:expo} predicts.
The deep, brief nulls are the signature of Rayleigh fading.}

\subsection{Chi-square: sums of squared Gaussians}

The sum of squares of $k$ independent standard Gaussians is \term{chi-square} with $k$ degrees of
freedom,
\begin{equation}
p_Y(y)=\frac{y^{k/2-1}e^{-y/2}}{2^{k/2}\Gamma(k/2)},\qquad y\ge0,
\label{eq:ch03:chisq}
\end{equation}
with mean $k$ and variance $2k$. The square-law detector output is $k=1$; the power of one complex
Gaussian sample is $k=2$ (the exponential); the energy collected by an energy detector from $N$
complex samples, or the combined power of $L$ diversity branches, is $k=2N$ or $2L$. Adding nonzero
means gives the \emph{noncentral} chi-square, whose noncentrality parameter is the sum of the
squared means---the energy of a signal hidden in the noise. Its tail is the generalised Marcum Q
function, which we meet in noncoherent detection (Section~\ref{sec:ch03:detection}).

The right panel of Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_chisq} shows the most important application. The
probability that the normalised power of $L$ combined independent complex Gaussians falls below a
small value $x$ behaves like $x^L$: a slope of one decade per 10\,dB for $L=1$, one decade per
5\,dB for $L=2$, and so on. That slope is the \term{diversity order}, and it is the reason two
receive antennas improve a fading link far more than 3\,dB of extra power would
(Chapters~\ref{ch:channels} and~\ref{ch:mimo}). Two antennas rarely fade at the same moment, just as
two independent coin tosses rarely both come up tails ten times in a row.

\mdcfig{ch03_chisq}{Left: chi-square densities. Right: CDF of the normalised power of the sum of
$L$ independent complex Gaussians (chi-square with $2L$ degrees of freedom). Deep fades become rarer
by one decade per $10/L$\,dB---the diversity effect.}

\subsection{The Rice distribution}

If a constant component $s$ is added---a sinusoid plus noise, or a line-of-sight path plus
scattered paths---the cloud of Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_cn_cloud} is shifted away from the origin, and
its envelope follows the \term{Rice distribution},
\begin{equation}
p_R(r)=\frac{r}{\sigma^2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{r^2+s^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)I_0\!\left(\frac{rs}{\sigma^2}\right),
\label{eq:ch03:rice}
\end{equation}
where $I_0$ is the modified Bessel function of order zero. It is characterised by the
\emph{K-factor} $K=s^2/2\sigma^2$, the ratio of the steady power to the random power
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_envelopes}). For $K=0$ it is Rayleigh; for large $K$ it approaches a Gaussian
of mean $s$ and variance $\sigma^2$, because only the noise component in phase with the constant
changes the envelope to first order. Stephen Rice derived these distributions in his 1944--45
\emph{Bell System Technical Journal} papers to describe a sinusoid in noise; they returned as the
models of multipath fading in Chapter~\ref{ch:channels}. When the envelope is not well described by
either, the two-parameter \emph{Nakagami-$m$} distribution, whose power is gamma-distributed, is the
usual empirical fit.

\mdcfig{ch03_envelopes}{Left: envelope distributions of a constant plus circular Gaussian noise,
for three Rice K-factors (histograms from simulation, curves from theory). Right: the phase of
circularly symmetric Gaussian noise is uniformly distributed.}

\paragraph{The log-normal.} One more distribution completes the family. A signal that passes
through many independent attenuating obstacles---walls, trees, hills---has a power that is a
\emph{product} of random factors; its logarithm is a sum, and by the CLT is Gaussian. A power whose
value in decibels is Gaussian is \term{log-normal}. This is the standard model of
\emph{shadowing}, with a standard deviation of 4--12\,dB in mobile radio
(Chapter~\ref{ch:channels}). The same CLT that makes thermal noise Gaussian in volts makes
shadowing Gaussian in decibels.

\section{Random processes}
\label{sec:ch03:processes}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Ensembles and sample paths}

Switch on an oscilloscope connected to a receiver's output with no signal present. You see a
jagged, restless trace. Switch the receiver off and on again and the trace is different in every
detail, yet unmistakably ``the same kind'' of noise. A \term{random process} $x(t)$ captures exactly
this: it is a random variable at every instant---equivalently, a random choice of an entire waveform
from an \emph{ensemble} of possible waveforms. Each trace on the scope is one \emph{sample path}
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_ensemble}).

\mdcfig{ch03_ensemble}{An ensemble of sample paths. Left: an ergodic process---averaging one path
over time (red arrow) gives the same answer as averaging many paths at one instant (dashed column).
Right: a process that is stationary but not ergodic: every unit has its own random DC offset, and no
amount of averaging one unit reveals the spread across the production line.}

A complete description would require the joint distribution of $x(t_1),\dots,x(t_n)$ for every $n$
and every choice of times. Fortunately, for the Gaussian processes that model noise, the mean
$\mu_x(t)=\E[x(t)]$ and the \term{autocorrelation function}
\begin{equation}
R_x(t_1,t_2)=\E[x(t_1)\,x^*(t_2)]
\end{equation}
suffice: they fix every joint distribution. (The conjugate makes the definition work for complex
baseband processes; for real ones it does nothing.)

\subsection{Stationarity}

A process is \emph{strict-sense stationary} if its statistics do not change with the time origin:
the noise at 3\,p.m.\ is statistically the same as the noise at 4\,p.m. The weaker and far more
useful condition is \term{wide-sense stationarity} (WSS): the mean is constant and the
autocorrelation depends only on the time difference $\tau=t_1-t_2$, so that we can write
$R_x(\tau)=\E[x(t+\tau)x^*(t)]$. For Gaussian processes the two notions coincide. The
autocorrelation of a WSS process has three properties worth memorising: $R_x(0)=\E|x|^2$ is the
average power; $|R_x(\tau)|\le R_x(0)$, so nothing is more similar to the process than itself; and
$R_x(-\tau)=R_x^*(\tau)$.

The autocorrelation measures how quickly a process ``forgets'' its past. The time over which
$R_x(\tau)$ stays near $R_x(0)$---its \emph{correlation time}---is the time over which you can
predict the process from its present value. Thermal noise forgets in picoseconds; the fading gain of
a pedestrian's mobile channel remembers for milliseconds (the coherence time of
Chapter~\ref{ch:channels}); the phase noise of a crystal oscillator wanders over seconds.

\mdcfig{ch03_wk_pair}{The Wiener--Khinchin theorem in pictures. Three Gaussian processes with
correlation times of 1, 5 and 25 samples. Longer memory (a wider autocorrelation, centre) means a
smoother sample path (left) and a spectrum squeezed towards low frequencies (right).}

\subsection{Ergodicity: one record instead of an ensemble}

In the laboratory we never see an ensemble. We see one long record and compute time averages. A
process is \term{ergodic} (in the mean, or in the autocorrelation) if these time averages converge
to the ensemble averages:
\begin{equation}
\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac1T\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}x(t)\,dt=\mu_x,\qquad
\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac1T\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}x(t+\tau)x^*(t)\,dt=R_x(\tau).
\end{equation}
Ergodicity is what makes measurement possible: a spectrum analyzer, a power meter and the
noise-variance estimator in a modem all replace expectations by time averages and trust the answer.
A sufficient condition for ergodicity in the mean is that the autocovariance decays to zero, so that
distant parts of the record are effectively independent and the time average behaves like an average
of many independent samples.

The canonical counterexample is a process whose every sample path is a constant, chosen at random:
$x(t)=A$ with $A\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_ensemble}, right). It is perfectly
stationary, but the time average of any one path is just $A$, never the ensemble mean 0. Physically,
this is a DC offset that differs from unit to unit: averaging longer on one receiver will never
reveal the distribution across the production line. The random-phase sinusoid
$x(t)=\cos(2\pi f_0t+\Theta)$ is, by contrast, ergodic in mean and autocorrelation---each sample path
visits every phase.

\subsection{The power spectral density and the Wiener--Khinchin theorem}
\label{sec:ch03:psd}

Noise has a \emph{colour}. The hiss of a radio tuned between stations is ``white'', with power spread
evenly over frequency; the rumble of distant traffic is ``red'', with its power at low frequencies. The
\term{power spectral density} (PSD) $S_x(f)$ makes this precise: the power in a narrow band $df$ is
$S_x(f)\,df$. The natural definition is operational---filter the process through an ideal narrow
band-pass filter and measure the output power---and the equivalent mathematical definition uses the
truncated Fourier transform $X_T(f)=\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}x(t)e^{-j2\pi ft}\,dt$ of a record of length $T$:
\begin{equation}
S_x(f)=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac1T\,\E\big[|X_T(f)|^2\big].
\label{eq:ch03:psddef}
\end{equation}

Before writing the next equation, guess what it should say. A process that forgets its past quickly
must be able to change quickly, so it must contain high frequencies; a sluggish process with a long
memory must live at low frequencies. Memory in time and width in frequency should be a
Fourier pair. They are: the \term{Wiener--Khinchin theorem} says that for a WSS process the PSD is the
Fourier transform of the autocorrelation:
\begin{equation}
S_x(f)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}R_x(\tau)e^{-j2\pi f\tau}d\tau,\qquad \E|x(t)|^2=R_x(0)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} S_x(f)\,df.
\label{eq:ch03:wk}
\end{equation}
In other words: a narrow autocorrelation (short memory) means a wide spectrum, and the total area
under the spectrum is the total power. Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_wk_pair} shows the pair for three
processes with different memories.

\mdcfig[0.5]{ch03_periodogram}{A single periodogram of white noise (grey) is as ragged after 8192
samples as after 100; averaging segments (Welch, navy) is what makes it converge to the true flat
PSD.}

\begin{deeper}[Deriving Wiener--Khinchin]
Expanding the squared magnitude in~\eqref{eq:ch03:psddef},
\[
\frac1T\E|X_T(f)|^2=\frac1T\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}\!\!\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}R_x(t-s)\,e^{-j2\pi f(t-s)}\,dt\,ds
=\int_{-T}^{T}\Big(1-\frac{|\tau|}{T}\Big)R_x(\tau)e^{-j2\pi f\tau}\,d\tau ,
\]
where the second step changes variables to $\tau=t-s$ and counts how much of the square
$[-T/2,T/2]^2$ lies on each diagonal $t-s=\tau$: a length proportional to $T-|\tau|$. The triangular
weight $1-|\tau|/T$ tends to one as $T\to\infty$ (provided $R_x$ decays fast enough), and the Fourier
transform of $R_x$ remains.
\end{deeper}

Two lessons hide in the derivation. First, the PSD is a property of the \emph{ensemble}; for a single
record, $|X_T(f)|^2/T$ (the \term{periodogram}) does not converge as $T$ grows---its standard deviation
stays equal to its mean at every frequency (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_periodogram}). Spectral estimates
must average: Welch's method splits a record into overlapping windowed segments and averages their
periodograms, trading frequency resolution for variance, and a spectrum analyzer does the same with
its resolution and video bandwidths. Second, the finite record convolves the true spectrum with the
transform of the triangle (or of whatever window is used): resolution is limited to about $1/T$.

The PSD is real and non-negative, and for a real process it is even, $S_x(-f)=S_x(f)$. Engineers then
often quote the \emph{one-sided} PSD $2S_x(f)$ over $f>0$, so that the total power is the integral over
positive frequencies only. The PSD of a complex baseband process need not be even: a signal can have
more power above its centre frequency than below.

A process that changes quickly has a narrow autocorrelation and a wide spectrum, and vice versa---the
same time--bandwidth reciprocity as for deterministic signals (Chapter~\ref{ch:signals}). A useful
pair: a process with $R_x(\tau)=\sigma^2e^{-|\tau|/\tau_c}$ (the output of an RC filter driven by white
noise, or the Ornstein--Uhlenbeck model of a drifting oscillator frequency) has the Lorentzian
spectrum $S_x(f)=2\sigma^2\tau_c/(1+(2\pi f\tau_c)^2)$, whose 3\,dB bandwidth is $1/(2\pi\tau_c)$. The
middle row of Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_wk_pair} is exactly this pair in discrete time.

\begin{history}[Wiener, Khinchin and Kolmogorov]
Norbert Wiener, at MIT, developed his \emph{generalized harmonic analysis} in a long 1930 paper in
\emph{Acta Mathematica}: a way to give a meaning to the spectrum of an irregular signal that has no
Fourier transform, through the transform of its autocorrelation. Aleksandr Khinchin, in Moscow,
proved the corresponding theorem for stationary random processes in 1934, and Andrei Kolmogorov laid
the axiomatic foundations of probability itself (1933) and solved the problem of predicting a
stationary sequence (1941). During the Second World War Wiener applied the theory to anti-aircraft
fire control, deriving the optimal linear filter for separating a signal from noise; his classified
report, nicknamed ``the yellow peril'' for its yellow covers and its difficulty, was published in 1949
as \emph{Extrapolation, Interpolation, and Smoothing of Stationary Time Series}. The Wiener filter
(Section~\ref{sec:ch03:estimation} and Chapter~\ref{ch:equalization}) and the Kalman filter that
generalised it in 1960 descend from this work, and so does the habit of thinking about noise in terms
of spectra.
\end{history}

paths, power spectral densities and autocorrelation functions.}

\subsection{Filtering random processes}

When a WSS process passes through an LTI filter with impulse response $h(t)$ and frequency response
$H(f)$, the output is WSS with autocorrelation $R_y(\tau)=h(\tau)*h^*(-\tau)*R_x(\tau)$, and taking
Fourier transforms,
\begin{equation}
S_y(f)=|H(f)|^2S_x(f).
\label{eq:ch03:filtpsd}
\end{equation}
This single equation carries most of the noise analysis in this book. Read it as a stencil: the
filter's \emph{power} response is laid over the input spectrum, and only what shows through the
holes comes out. It says, for example, that noise power at a receiver output is the input noise PSD
integrated over the receiver's power response, which is why the noise-equivalent bandwidth of
Section~\ref{sec:bandwidth} is the right bandwidth to use. The phase response of the filter is
irrelevant to the output spectrum; it affects only the waveform. The cross-correlation between input
and output, $R_{yx}(\tau)=h(\tau)*R_x(\tau)$, is equally useful: drive an unknown system with white
noise, and the input--output cross-correlation \emph{is} the impulse response. Channel sounders and
the least-mean-squares equalisers of Chapter~\ref{ch:equalization} exploit exactly this.

\subsection{White noise}

Thermal noise has a flat PSD from DC to far beyond any radio frequency (up to about 6\,THz at room
temperature, where quantum effects set in; Section~\ref{sec:ch03:thermal}). We idealise it as
\term{white noise}---white because, like white light, it contains all frequencies in equal
measure---with two-sided PSD $N_0/2$\,W/Hz, whose autocorrelation is an impulse:
$R_n(\tau)=\frac{N_0}{2}\delta(\tau)$. Samples taken at any two distinct instants are
uncorrelated---and, being Gaussian, independent. White noise has infinite power and does not exist,
but once filtered it becomes perfectly well behaved: through a filter of noise bandwidth $B$, the
output power is $N_0B$ (counting both positive and negative frequencies of a real filter).
Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_filtered_noise} shows white noise and low-pass filtered noise: filtering smooths
the sample paths, narrows the spectrum and widens the autocorrelation.

\mdcfig{ch03_neb}{Noise-equivalent bandwidth. Left: power responses of Butterworth filters with the
same 3\,dB bandwidth and the rectangles that pass the same noise power. Right: the ratio
$B_N/f_{3\,\mathrm{dB}}$ approaches one as the filter becomes sharper.}

\paragraph{White noise in discrete time.} A simulation cannot generate continuous-time white noise,
and does not need to. Sampling at rate $f_s$ is preceded (implicitly, in a simulation; physically, in
a radio) by an anti-aliasing filter of bandwidth $f_s/2$. The filtered noise has power $N_0f_s/2$,
and its samples at spacing $1/f_s$ are uncorrelated, because the autocorrelation of ideally low-pass
filtered white noise is a sinc with zeros at multiples of $1/f_s$. So \emph{discrete-time white
noise} is a sequence of independent Gaussian samples of variance $\sigma^2=N_0f_s/2$ (real) or
$N_0f_s$ (complex baseband, in the convention of Section~\ref{sec:ch03:bandpass}). The variance per
sample grows with the sample rate while the density $N_0$ stays fixed---a fact that causes more
simulation bugs than any other, and to which we return in Section~\ref{sec:ch03:snr}.

\subsection{Noise-equivalent bandwidth}

Real filters do not have rectangular responses, so ``noise in bandwidth $B$'' needs a definition. The
\term{noise-equivalent bandwidth} $B_N$ of a filter is the width of the rectangular filter, with the
same peak power gain, that passes the same noise power:
\begin{equation}
B_N=\frac{1}{|H(f_0)|^2}\int_0^\infty|H(f)|^2\,df
\label{eq:ch03:neb}
\end{equation}
for a real low-pass filter ($f_0=0$) or band-pass filter (peak at $f_0$). Think of pouring the area
under $|H(f)|^2$ into a rectangular tank of the same height: $B_N$ is the width of the tank.
Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_neb} compares $B_N$ with the 3\,dB bandwidth. A single-pole RC filter has
$B_N=\frac{\pi}{2}f_{3\,\mathrm{dB}}$, 57\% wider than its 3\,dB bandwidth, because its skirts fall
only at 6\,dB per octave and keep admitting noise far from the passband. An $n$-pole Butterworth
filter has $B_N/f_{3\,\mathrm{dB}}=(\pi/2n)/\sin(\pi/2n)$: 1.11 for two poles and 1.03 for four.

A spectrum analyzer's Gaussian-shaped resolution filter has $B_N\approx1.06\times$ its 3\,dB
bandwidth, which is why noise-marker readings include a correction of about 0.27\,dB in addition to
the log-averaging correction mentioned earlier. The most important example is the matched filter: a
root-raised-cosine receive filter for symbol rate $R_s$ has a noise bandwidth of exactly $R_s$ at
complex baseband, \emph{whatever its roll-off}, because $|H(f)|^2$ is a raised-cosine spectrum, whose
area equals that of the ideal Nyquist rectangle.

\mdcfig[0.5]{ch03_ktc}{$kT/C$ noise against the quantisation noise of 2\,V converters. Each extra
bit needs four times the capacitance---or cooling.}

\begin{worked}[The noise of a capacitor: $kT/C$]
A resistor $R$ at temperature $T$ in parallel with a capacitor $C$ forms a single-pole low-pass
filter for the resistor's noise. Section~\ref{sec:ch03:thermal} shows that the resistor has a
one-sided open-circuit noise-voltage PSD $4kTR$\,V$^2$/Hz. The filter has
$f_{3\,\mathrm{dB}}=1/(2\pi RC)$ and so $B_N=\frac\pi2f_{3\,\mathrm{dB}}=1/(4RC)$. The mean-square
voltage on the capacitor is therefore
\[
\overline{v^2}=4kTR\cdot\frac{1}{4RC}=\frac{kT}{C},
\]
independent of $R$: a larger resistor makes more noise but in a narrower band. For $C=1$\,pF at
300\,K the RMS voltage is $\sqrt{kT/C}\approx64\,\mu$V. This \term{$kT/C$ noise} is frozen onto the
sampling capacitor of every switched-capacitor ADC each time its switch opens, and it sets the
capacitor sizes of high-resolution converters: to keep the noise of a 2\,V full-scale, 14-bit
converter below its quantisation noise ($\Delta/\sqrt{12}\approx35\,\mu$V), the sampling capacitance
must exceed roughly 3.4\,pF, and each extra bit quadruples it (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_ktc}).
\end{worked}

The worked example hides a beautiful piece of physics. Why should the answer not depend on $R$? Because
the capacitor stores one degree of freedom of energy, $\tfrac12C\overline{v^2}$, and in thermal
equilibrium the equipartition theorem gives every such degree of freedom $\tfrac12kT$---no more, no
less, whatever resistor is jostling it. Set the two equal and $\overline{v^2}=kT/C$ drops out with no
filter theory at all. The same reasoning, applied to the modes of a transmission line, is how Nyquist
derived thermal noise itself (Section~\ref{sec:ch03:thermal}). Designers of image sensors and
precision ADCs live with $kT/C$ every day, and the trick of correlated double sampling---measure the
capacitor just after reset, measure it again after the signal arrives, and subtract---exists largely
to cancel it.

\subsection{The I/Q representation}

Tune a radio to an empty channel and look at the IF output on a scope. It looks like a carrier---a
sinusoid at the IF frequency---but one whose amplitude and phase wander restlessly, as if someone were
turning the volume and nudging the tuning knob at random. That is narrowband noise, and the
description of it as ``a carrier with a randomly wandering envelope and phase'' is not just a picture;
it is exact.

At the output of a radio's IF or channel filter, noise occupies a band of width $B$ around a carrier
$f_c$. Like any bandpass signal (Section~\ref{sec:complexenvelope}), it can be written in terms of a
complex envelope $\tilde n(t)=n_I(t)+jn_Q(t)$:
\begin{equation}
n(t)=\re\{\tilde n(t)e^{j2\pi f_ct}\}=n_I(t)\cos2\pi f_ct-n_Q(t)\sin2\pi f_ct .
\label{eq:ch03:bpnoise}
\end{equation}
The in-phase and quadrature components $n_I(t)$ and $n_Q(t)$ are what a quadrature downconverter
produces (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_iqdown}): multiply by $2\cos2\pi f_ct$ and $-2\sin2\pi f_ct$ and
low-pass filter.

\mdcfig{ch03_bandpass}{Narrowband Gaussian noise. Top: the bandpass waveform $n(t)$ with its envelope
$|\tilde n(t)|$ and in-phase component $n_I(t)$; the noise looks like a carrier whose amplitude and
phase wander on a time scale of $1/B$. Bottom left: the passband PSD (two-sided) and the PSDs of $n_I$
and of the complex envelope, each 3\,dB above the previous one---the factors of two in action. Bottom
right: decorrelated samples of $(n_I,n_Q)$ form a circular cloud with negligible correlation.}

If $n(t)$ is a zero-mean WSS Gaussian process, then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $n_I$ and $n_Q$ are jointly Gaussian, zero-mean, low-pass processes with the same PSD,
\begin{equation}
S_{n_I}(f)=S_{n_Q}(f)=S_n(f-f_c)+S_n(f+f_c),\qquad |f|<B/2,
\end{equation}
obtained by sliding the positive- and negative-frequency halves of the bandpass spectrum to zero and
adding them.
\item Each has the same power as $n(t)$ itself: $\E[n_I^2]=\E[n_Q^2]=\E[n^2]$. (The cosine and sine
carriers each carry half of their component's power, and the two halves add up.)
\item Their cross-spectrum is proportional to the \emph{asymmetry} of $S_n(f)$ about $f_c$. If the
bandpass spectrum is symmetric about the carrier---the usual case for a filter centred on
$f_c$---$n_I$ and $n_Q$ are uncorrelated at every lag and hence, being Gaussian, independent.
\end{enumerate}
For white noise of two-sided PSD $N_0/2$ passed through an ideal band-pass filter of width $B$, $n_I$
and $n_Q$ therefore have PSD $N_0$ over $|f|<B/2$ and power $N_0B$ each, and the complex envelope
$\tilde n$ has PSD $2N_0$ and power $\E|\tilde n|^2=2N_0B$. Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_bandpass} shows all
of this in a simulation: the bandpass noise looks like a carrier whose amplitude and phase wander at a
rate set by $B$, its I and Q components are slowly varying, and their scatter plot is a circular cloud.

\mdcfig[0.75]{ch03_rice_phasor}{A carrier plus narrowband noise, seen as a phasor $A$ (red) plus a cloud of
complex Gaussian noise. Left: with a strong carrier the envelope wobbles only slightly about $A$ and
the phase hardly moves. Right: with a weak carrier the cloud swallows the phasor; envelope and phase
are now mostly noise---the threshold effect.}

\begin{pitfall}[Factors of two]
Noise PSDs are quoted one-sided ($N_0$\,W/Hz over $f>0$) or two-sided ($N_0/2$ over all $f$); with the
envelope convention~\eqref{eq:ch03:bpnoise}, which this book uses, the complex envelope has PSD $2N_0$
in the band---and the envelope of a signal of passband energy $E$ also has energy $2E$, so the
\emph{ratio} $E/N_0$ is unchanged. Many authors, and all the labs, rescale the envelope by $1/\sqrt2$
to make energies match; then the complex noise has PSD $N_0$, with $N_0/2$ in each of I and Q, and
$\E|n|^2=N_0$ per complex sample at unit sample rate. Both conventions give the same error rates if
used consistently. Most errors of exactly 3\,dB in simulations come from mixing them. The labs use one
convention throughout: unit-energy symbols and complex noise of variance $N_0$ per symbol-spaced
sample, so that $E_s/N_0$ is the ratio of symbol energy to noise variance at the matched-filter
output.
\end{pitfall}

\subsection{Envelope and phase}

Writing the complex envelope in polar form, $\tilde n=r(t)e^{j\psi(t)}$, gives the
\emph{envelope--phase} representation $n(t)=r(t)\cos(2\pi f_ct+\psi(t))$. At any instant
$(n_I,n_Q)$ is a circular Gaussian pair, so, from Section~\ref{sec:ch03:complex}, $r$ is Rayleigh,
$\psi$ is uniform, and the two are independent. An envelope detector (a diode and RC filter in an AM
radio, or $|\cdot|$ in software) driven by noise alone produces a Rayleigh-distributed output; a
squarer produces an exponential one.

Now add a carrier: $x(t)=A\cos2\pi f_ct+n(t)$. The complex envelope is $A+\tilde n$, a constant plus
circular Gaussian noise (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_rice_phasor}), so the envelope is Rice-distributed with $K=A^2/2\sigma^2$, where $\sigma^2$
is the variance of each noise component---exactly the left panel of Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_envelopes}.
At high SNR, the envelope is approximately Gaussian with mean $A$, and the phase error is
approximately Gaussian with variance $\sigma^2/A^2=1/(2K)$: only the quadrature noise component
rotates the phasor. At low SNR, the noise dominates and the envelope becomes Rayleigh; the signal is
``captured'' by the noise. This transition is the \emph{threshold effect} of envelope-detected AM and
of FM (Chapter~\ref{ch:analog}): above threshold, output SNR is proportional to input SNR; below it,
it collapses.

\subsection{Simulating bandpass noise at baseband}

Because bandpass noise is completely described by its complex envelope, simulations never need to
generate it at the carrier. The recipe used in every lab is:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Choose the complex sample rate $f_s$ (e.g.\ $f_s=N_{\text{sps}}R_s$ with $N_{\text{sps}}$
samples per symbol).
\item Generate independent complex samples $n_k=\sigma(u_k+jv_k)/\sqrt2$, with $u_k,v_k$ independent
$\mathcal N(0,1)$, so that $\E|n_k|^2=\sigma^2$.
\item Set $\sigma^2=N_0f_s$ in the energy-matched convention of the pitfall above, with $N_0$ obtained
from the required $E_s/N_0$ and the symbol energy.
\end{enumerate}
Section~\ref{sec:ch03:snr} turns this into a formula. Coloured or band-limited noise is obtained by
filtering this white sequence, and~\eqref{eq:ch03:filtpsd} predicts its spectrum.

\section{Cyclostationarity: the hidden periodicity of signals}
\label{sec:ch03:cyclo}
%======================================================================

\mdcfig[0.5]{ch03_cyclo_concept}{A signal's hidden heartbeat. The variance of a BPSK signal rises
and falls once per symbol; the variance of noise is flat. That periodic rhythm is cyclostationarity.}

\subsection{Why modulated signals are not stationary}

A heart monitor can find a heartbeat in a noisy trace not because the beat is loud but because it
\emph{repeats}. Noise has no rhythm; a heartbeat does. Communication signals have a heartbeat too, and
it can betray them even when they are far weaker than the noise.

A random process is \term{cyclostationary} (in the wide sense) if its mean and autocorrelation are
periodic in time: $R_x(t+T_0+\tau,\,t+T_0)=R_x(t+\tau,\,t)$ for some period $T_0$. Noise is
stationary. Man-made signals almost never are. A PAM signal $s(t)=\sum_ka_kp(t-kT)$ has statistics
that repeat every symbol period: its variance is largest at the instants where pulses peak and
smallest in between (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_cyclo_concept}). A signal on a carrier has statistics that
repeat at the carrier period. A TDMA frame repeats at the frame rate, an OFDM signal with a cyclic
prefix at the OFDM symbol rate, a spread-spectrum signal at the code period. Every one of these
periodicities is a fingerprint that noise does not share.

For spectral analysis the periodicity is usually averaged away. Averaging $R_x(t+\tau,t)$ over one
period gives a stationary autocorrelation whose Fourier transform is the familiar PSD; this is exactly
how the PSD of PAM, $S_s(f)=\frac{\sigma_a^2}{T}|P(f)|^2$ for uncorrelated zero-mean symbols, is derived
in Chapter~\ref{ch:baseband}. But averaging throws information away. A spectrum analyzer shows you how
much power a signal has at each frequency; it cannot show you the rhythm, and the rhythm is often the
more useful clue. A sensitive receiver that knows to listen for the beat can do far better than one
that merely listens for loudness.

\subsection{Cyclic autocorrelation and spectral correlation}

Expand the periodic autocorrelation in a Fourier series in $t$:
\begin{equation}
R_x(t+\tau/2,\,t-\tau/2)=\sum_{\alpha}R_x^{\alpha}(\tau)\,e^{j2\pi\alpha t},\qquad
R_x^{\alpha}(\tau)=\big\langle x(t+\tau/2)x^*(t-\tau/2)e^{-j2\pi\alpha t}\big\rangle_t .
\label{eq:ch03:cyclicac}
\end{equation}
The coefficients $R_x^\alpha(\tau)$ are the \term{cyclic autocorrelation} functions, and the
frequencies $\alpha$ at which they are nonzero are the \term{cycle frequencies}: multiples of $1/T_0$.
The coefficient at $\alpha=0$ is the ordinary (time-averaged) autocorrelation. The Fourier transform of
$R_x^\alpha(\tau)$ in $\tau$ is the \term{spectral correlation function} $S_x^\alpha(f)$, and it has a
beautifully concrete meaning: it is the correlation between the spectral components of $x$ at
frequencies $f+\alpha/2$ and $f-\alpha/2$. For a stationary process all distinct frequency components
are uncorrelated, so $S_x^\alpha(f)=0$ for every $\alpha\ne0$. For a cyclostationary process,
components separated by a cycle frequency are correlated: they carry the same information.

\mdcfig{ch03_cyclo}{Cyclostationarity reveals a signal buried in noise. A BPSK signal (RRC,
$\beta=0.35$, 8 samples per symbol, carrier offset $f_0$) at an overall SNR of $-10$\,dB barely raises
the PSD (left). The spectral coherence, maximised over $f$ and plotted against cycle frequency, shows
sharp features at $\alpha=\pm R_s$ (non-conjugate, centre) and at $\alpha=2f_0$ and $2f_0\pm R_s$
(conjugate, right). Noise alone (grey) shows only the estimation floor.}

For PAM with uncorrelated symbols of variance $\sigma_a^2$, the result is
\begin{equation}
S_s^{\alpha}(f)=\frac{\sigma_a^2}{T}\,P\!\left(f+\tfrac\alpha2\right)P^*\!\left(f-\tfrac\alpha2\right),
\qquad \alpha=\frac kT .
\label{eq:ch03:pamscf}
\end{equation}
A feature at $\alpha=1/T$ exists only where the pulse spectrum and its copy shifted by $1/T$
overlap---that is, in the \emph{excess bandwidth} of the pulse. A pulse with zero roll-off (an ideal
sinc) has no symbol-rate cyclostationarity at all, and a timing-recovery circuit that relies on it (a
squarer followed by a narrow filter at $1/T$, Chapter~\ref{ch:sync}) has nothing to lock to. The
roll-off chosen in Chapter~\ref{ch:baseband} is therefore also a choice about how easy the signal is to
synchronise.

There is a second, \emph{conjugate} spectral correlation, built from $\E[x(t+\tau/2)x(t-\tau/2)]$
without the conjugate. It is zero for circular processes---complex Gaussian noise, QPSK,
16-QAM---but nonzero for signals with real-valued constellations such as BPSK and PAM, whose conjugate
features sit at $\alpha=2f_0+k/T$, where $f_0$ is the residual carrier offset. Squaring a BPSK signal
produces a spectral line at twice the carrier; that is the Costas loop's secret (Chapter~\ref{ch:sync}),
and the presence or absence of conjugate features is the first test of an automatic modulation
classifier.

\subsection{Detecting and classifying signals}

Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_cyclo} shows why this matters. A BPSK signal 10\,dB below the noise in the
analysis band is invisible in a short PSD estimate and would be hard to detect with an energy detector,
especially if the noise level is not known precisely---an uncertainty of only 0.5\,dB in the noise floor
makes an energy detector useless below a certain SNR, the so-called \emph{SNR wall}. The spectral
correlation, by contrast, measures structure, not level: noise contributes nothing at $\alpha\ne0$
however strong it is, so the features emerge from the estimation floor as the observation grows. The
locations of the features identify the symbol rate, the carrier offset and whether the constellation is
real or complex.

\begin{inpractice}[Cyclostationary processing at work]
Cyclostationary feature detection was proposed in the 1980s for signal interception and has since been
used for signal intelligence, spectrum monitoring and, more recently, spectrum sensing in cognitive
radio, where a secondary user must detect a licensed transmitter far below the noise floor. Practical
receivers use the idea constantly without naming it: the square-law and Gardner timing detectors and
the Costas and squaring carrier loops of Chapter~\ref{ch:sync} are cycle-frequency estimators; the
cyclic-prefix correlator that finds the OFDM symbol boundary in Wi-Fi and LTE (Chapter~\ref{ch:ofdm})
detects the cyclostationarity the prefix creates; and blind modulation recognition in
spectrum-monitoring equipment begins by estimating the cycle frequencies. The FFT accumulation method
and the strip spectral correlation analyser make the computation affordable for wideband searches.
\end{inpractice}

\begin{keyidea}[Noise is stationary; signals are not]
A stationary process has uncorrelated spectral components. Every man-made signal has
periodicities---symbol rate, carrier, frame, code---that correlate its spectral components at specific
separations. Measuring those correlations detects, synchronises and classifies signals without knowing
their content and without knowing the noise level. Much of synchronisation is applied
cyclostationarity.
\end{keyidea}

\section{Physical noise sources}
\label{sec:ch03:physical}
%======================================================================

The probabilistic models above say nothing about how \emph{much} noise there is. For that we need
physics. This section is a field guide to the noise zoo, in rough order of importance to a radio
engineer: from the inescapable thermal floor, through the hail of discrete charges and the slow
murmur of flicker, to lightning, spark plugs and the hiss of the universe itself.
Table~\ref{tab:ch03:zoo} is the field guide in one glance.

\subsection{Thermal noise}
\label{sec:ch03:thermal}

Look closely at an ordinary resistor (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_resistor_xsec}). Inside the ceramic body
is a film or slug of carbon, metal or oxide, and inside that, an unimaginable crowd of free electrons
careering about at thermal speeds, colliding with the lattice billions of times a second. On average
their motion goes nowhere; at any instant, a few more happen to be moving left than right, and a tiny
voltage appears across the terminals. Every warm resistor whispers.

Quantitatively, any conductor at absolute temperature $T$ contains charge carriers in random thermal
motion. The net current their motion produces averages to zero but fluctuates, and across a resistor
$R$ it appears as an open-circuit noise voltage with one-sided PSD
\begin{equation}
\overline{v_n^2}=4kTR\ \ \mathrm{V^2/Hz},\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \overline{i_n^2}=\frac{4kT}{R}\ \ \mathrm{A^2/Hz},
\label{eq:ch03:johnson}
\end{equation}
the second form being the Norton equivalent. Connect the resistor to a matched load (another resistor
$R$) and it delivers the \term{available noise power}
\begin{equation}
P_n=kTB,\qquad k=1.380649\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J/K},
\label{eq:ch03:ktb}
\end{equation}
in a bandwidth $B$, independent of $R$ and of the material. Read it aloud, because you will use it
for the rest of your career: \emph{noise power is Boltzmann's constant times temperature times
bandwidth}. Double the bandwidth, double the noise; halve the absolute temperature, halve the noise.
At the reference temperature $T_0=290$\,K, $kT_0=4.00\times10^{-21}$\,W/Hz, or $-174$\,dBm/Hz. This
number deserves memorising, together with its common scalings (Table~\ref{tab:ch03:ktb}).

\begin{analogy}[Atoms jiggling: every warm resistor whispers $kTB$]
Picture the electrons in a resistor as a crowd milling in a hall. Nobody is going anywhere in
particular, yet at any moment the crowd's centre of mass drifts a little this way or that. Heat the
hall and the milling gets more energetic; listen through a wider doorway (more bandwidth) and you hear
more of it. What you never hear is the \emph{material}---carbon, metal or salt water, the crowd mills
the same way, because thermodynamics fixes how much energy each ``way of moving'' gets. The limit of
the analogy: the hall never empties. Only cooling towards absolute zero quietens the crowd, which is
exactly what radio astronomers do with their amplifiers.
\end{analogy}

\paragraph{Nyquist's derivation.} Nyquist's argument is a model of physical reasoning: no
microscopic model of conduction at all, only the second law of thermodynamics and a clever thought
experiment. Connect two identical resistors at the same temperature with a matched transmission line.
Each launches noise into the line and absorbs all of the other's. In equilibrium the exchange must
balance---otherwise one resistor would heat the other, a perpetual-motion machine of the second
kind---so the noise power cannot depend on the material. Then count the energy stored in the line.

\begin{deeper}[Nyquist's transmission-line argument, 1928]
Connect two identical resistors $R$, both at temperature $T$, by a lossless transmission line of
characteristic impedance $R$ and length $\ell$. Each resistor launches noise power $P$ per unit
bandwidth into the line, and since the line is matched, all of it is absorbed by the other resistor.
In thermal equilibrium the exchange must balance, so $P$ is the same for every resistor regardless of
its material. To find $P$, short both ends of the line suddenly, trapping the energy in flight. The
line now supports standing-wave modes at frequencies $f_m=mv/2\ell$, so there are $2\ell\,\Delta f/v$
modes in a band $\Delta f$. By the equipartition theorem each mode holds energy $kT$ (half electric,
half magnetic). The trapped energy $kT\cdot2\ell\Delta f/v$ was travelling equally in both directions
and took $\ell/v$ to traverse the line, so the power each resistor was launching was
\[
P=\frac{\tfrac12\,kT\cdot2\ell\Delta f/v}{\ell/v}=kT\Delta f .
\]
A source of open-circuit voltage $v$ and resistance $R$ delivers $v^2/4R$ to a matched load, so
$\overline{v^2}=4kTR\Delta f$. Nothing in the argument depends on the conduction mechanism: the result
is as general as thermodynamics.
\end{deeper}

\mdcfig[0.8]{ch03_planck}{The quantum correction to thermal noise: available noise power per hertz
relative to the classical $kT$, for five physical temperatures. Room-temperature thermal noise is white
to about 1\,THz; cryogenic noise is not.}

\paragraph{The quantum correction.} Equipartition fails when the energy quantum $hf$ of a mode becomes
comparable with $kT$. Nyquist's paper already gave the correction: replace $kT$ by the mean energy of a
quantum oscillator,
\begin{equation}
kT\ \longrightarrow\ \frac{hf}{e^{hf/kT}-1},
\label{eq:ch03:planck}
\end{equation}
which is Planck's law for a single mode. Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_planck} shows the ratio. At 290\,K,
$hf=kT$ at $f\approx6$\,THz, so the classical formula holds to better than 3\% up to 300\,GHz. At
2.7\,K, the temperature of the cosmic background, the available noise power is already halved near
90\,GHz. At optical frequencies ($hf/k\approx9000$\,K at 193\,THz) thermal noise vanishes entirely, and
what remains is the graininess of light itself: optical receivers are limited by shot noise and by the
quantum noise of amplifiers, not by $kT$. Quantum mechanics also sets a floor for amplifiers: a linear
amplifier that treats both quadratures equally must add noise equivalent to about half a photon per
mode at its input (Caves, 1982), a noise temperature of order $hf/k$---about 0.05\,K at 1\,GHz, 5\,K at
100\,GHz, and the dominant limitation in the superconducting parametric amplifiers now used for quantum
computing readout.

\mdcfig[0.5]{ch03_resistor_density}{Open-circuit noise density $\sqrt{4kTR}$ of a resistor at three
temperatures. At room temperature a 50\,$\Omega$ source has about 0.9\,nV$/\sqrt{\text{Hz}}$ and a
1\,k$\Omega$ resistor about 4\,nV$/\sqrt{\text{Hz}}$---already noisier than a good audio op-amp.}

\begin{history}[Johnson, Nyquist and the noise of a resistor, 1928]
John Bertrand Johnson, a Swedish-born physicist at Bell Telephone Laboratories, set out in 1926 to
understand the irreducible hiss of high-gain vacuum-tube amplifiers. He found that the noise at an
amplifier's output depended on the resistance of the input circuit and its temperature, and measured it
carefully for resistors of carbon, metal films and electrolytes: the mean-square voltage per unit
bandwidth was proportional to $RT$, with the same constant for every material. From his measurements
he could even estimate Boltzmann's constant. He described the result to the American Physical Society
in 1927 and published it in the \emph{Physical Review} in July 1928 as ``Thermal agitation of
electricity in conductors''. Harry Nyquist, his colleague, published the theory in the same issue:
``Thermal agitation of electric charge in conductors'', four pages that derived $4kTR\,\Delta f$ from the
second law of thermodynamics and the transmission-line argument given above, including the quantum
correction. Thermal noise is still called \emph{Johnson--Nyquist noise}, and Nyquist went on to the
sampling and stability results that also carry his name. Few pairs of papers have been cited by so many
engineers who have never read them.
\end{history}

\begin{worked}[How big is thermal noise?]
A 50\,$\Omega$ resistor at 290\,K in a 1\,MHz bandwidth has an open-circuit RMS noise voltage
$\sqrt{4kT_0RB}=\sqrt{4\times4.00\times10^{-21}\times50\times10^6}\approx0.89\,\mu$V, and delivers
$kT_0B=4.0\times10^{-15}$\,W $=-114$\,dBm to a matched load. A 1\,k$\Omega$ resistor has a noise density
$\sqrt{4kTR}\approx4$\,nV/$\sqrt{\text{Hz}}$, the figure audio designers know by heart; over the 20\,kHz
audio band it gives 0.57\,$\mu$V RMS. A microphone preamplifier with 1\,nV/$\sqrt{\text{Hz}}$ input
noise is therefore quieter than a 1\,k$\Omega$ source---and a 4\,nV/$\sqrt{\text{Hz}}$ op-amp is already
as noisy as one (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_resistor_density}). In a radio, the same physics appears as
$-174$\,dBm/Hz: a GPS signal arrives at about $-130$\,dBm spread over 2\,MHz, some 19\,dB \emph{below}
the $-111$\,dBm of thermal noise in that band---yet receivers track it routinely, by despreading
(Chapter~\ref{ch:spreadspectrum}).
\end{worked}

\subsection{Shot noise}

Rain on a tin roof does not make a smooth sound. It makes a patter, because rain is not a fluid but a
stream of drops arriving at random. Electric current is the same. Current is not a fluid; it is a
stream of discrete charges. When they cross a potential barrier independently and at random
times---electrons boiling off a hot cathode (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_tube}), carriers crossing a pn
junction, photoelectrons released in a photodiode---the current is a train of impulses
$i(t)=\sum_iq\,\delta(t-t_i)$ whose arrival times form a Poisson process of rate $\lambda=I/q$. By
Campbell's theorem, the fluctuation about the mean $I$ is white with two-sided PSD $q^2\lambda=qI$, or
one-sided
\begin{equation}
\overline{i_n^2}=2qI\ \ \mathrm{A^2/Hz},
\label{eq:ch03:shot}
\end{equation}
up to frequencies comparable with the inverse transit time of a carrier. This is \term{shot noise},
predicted by Walter Schottky in 1918. The fluctuation grows only as the square root of the current, so
its \emph{relative} size shrinks as the current grows: a trickle of charges is lumpy, a flood is smooth
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_shot}, left). A 1\,mA diode current carries $\sqrt{2qI}\approx17.9$\,pA/$\sqrt{\text{Hz}}$
of shot noise---the same as the thermal noise current of a 50\,$\Omega$ resistor. Unlike thermal noise,
shot noise needs a current and a barrier: a resistor carrying DC in thermal equilibrium shows only
thermal noise, because the carriers' motion is correlated by their frequent collisions.

Shot noise dominates in optical receivers (Chapter~\ref{ch:wireline}). The photocurrent of a photodiode
is a Poisson stream of photoelectrons, so even a perfect detector observing a perfectly steady laser
sees fluctuations. With direct detection of on--off keyed light, a ``one'' that produces no
photoelectrons at all is an error; with a Poisson mean of $N$ photons per ``one'' the probability of that
is $e^{-N}$, and a $10^{-9}$ error rate needs $N\approx20$, an average of about 10 photons per bit---the
\emph{quantum limit} of direct detection. Practical receivers, limited by the thermal noise of their
transimpedance amplifiers, need hundreds to thousands; avalanche photodiodes and optical preamplifiers
exist to close the gap.

\begin{history}[Schottky and the small-shot effect]
Walter Schottky, working at Siemens and the University of Berlin, predicted in a 1918 paper in the
\emph{Annalen der Physik} that the current of a vacuum tube must fluctuate because electrons are emitted
independently, like lead shot---hence \emph{Schroteffekt}, the shot effect. He suggested that the effect
could be used to measure the charge of the electron, and experimenters in the 1920s did exactly that.
Johnson, looking for the shot effect at audio frequencies, found far more low-frequency noise than
Schottky's formula predicted; in 1926 Schottky explained it as a slow fluctuation of the cathode's
emitting surface, the \emph{flicker effect}. Schottky also invented the screen-grid tube and,
independently of Armstrong, the superheterodyne principle, and his name lives on in the Schottky barrier
diode in every mixer and RF detector.
\end{history}

\subsection{Flicker (1/$f$) noise}

Nearly every active device, and many passive ones, shows excess noise whose PSD rises at low frequency
roughly as $1/f^\gamma$ with $\gamma\approx1$---\term{flicker noise} or \emph{pink noise}. ``Pink''
because it is white noise with extra red (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_noise_colors}): equal power in every
octave, so it sounds like a waterfall rather than a hiss. Its physical origins vary (trapping and release
of carriers by defects at the silicon--oxide interface of a MOSFET is the usual explanation in CMOS) and
are still debated in general; its effects are not. It is characterised by its \emph{corner frequency},
where it equals the white noise floor: typically below a few kilohertz for bipolar transistors and from
tens of kilohertz to megahertz for small modern MOSFETs.

Flicker noise matters in three places. In \emph{direct-conversion} (zero-IF) receivers, which move the
wanted channel to DC (Chapter~\ref{ch:sdr}), the baseband amplifiers' $1/f$ noise lands on top of the
signal; narrowband zero-IF receivers therefore use bipolar or large-area devices, or a low-IF
architecture that keeps the signal away from DC. In \emph{oscillators}, flicker noise is upconverted into
close-in phase noise (below). In \emph{precision instrumentation}, it limits how long averaging helps:
white noise averages down as $1/\sqrt{T}$, but $1/f$ noise has as much power in each decade of frequency
as in the next, so the variance of a long average stops falling. Chopper stabilisation and correlated
double sampling move the measurement away from DC to escape it.

\subsection{Phase noise: a preview}

An oscillator's output is not a perfect sinusoid but $A\cos(2\pi f_ct+\phi(t))$, where the phase
$\phi(t)$ wanders randomly. Its spectrum is a line broadened into skirts, specified by the single-sideband
phase-noise density $\mathcal L(\Delta f)$ in dBc/Hz at offset $\Delta f$ from the carrier. Leeson's model
(1966) explains the typical shape (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_leeson}): white noise in the oscillator's sustaining amplifier, filtered by the
resonator, gives a $1/\Delta f^2$ region; flicker noise upconverted by the amplifier's nonlinearity gives
a steeper $1/\Delta f^3$ region close in; far from the carrier the spectrum flattens to the amplifier's
white floor. Phase noise hurts in two ways: it rotates the constellation of the wanted signal, which
limits high-order QAM and OFDM (Chapters~\ref{ch:modulation} and~\ref{ch:ofdm}), and through
\emph{reciprocal mixing} it transfers a strong neighbour's power into the wanted channel, adding noise of
approximately $P_{\text{blocker}}+\mathcal L(\Delta f)+10\log_{10}B$ dBm. A $-30$\,dBm blocker 1\,MHz away
and an LO with $-130$\,dBc/Hz at that offset put about $-107$\,dBm into a 200\,kHz channel, comparable
with the sensitivity of a GSM receiver. Chapter~\ref{ch:sdr} treats oscillators, synthesisers and their
phase noise in detail.

\subsection{Quantisation noise: a preview}

Every digital receiver ends in an analog-to-digital converter, and every ADC rounds. A uniform quantiser
with step $\Delta$, driven by a busy signal, makes an error that is approximately uniform on
$[-\Delta/2,\Delta/2)$, uncorrelated with the signal and white, with power $\Delta^2/12$. For an $N$-bit
converter driven by a full-scale sinusoid, this gives the familiar signal-to-quantisation-noise ratio
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SQNR}=6.02N+1.76\ \mathrm{dB},
\label{eq:ch03:sqnr}
\end{equation}
spread uniformly from DC to $f_s/2$. Every extra bit buys 6\,dB. Oversampling spreads the same noise
power over a wider band, so a receiver that keeps only bandwidth $B$ gains $10\log_{10}(f_s/2B)$\,dB of
processing gain; noise shaping in sigma--delta converters pushes it further out of band.
Chapter~\ref{ch:pcm} develops quantisation, companding and sigma--delta conversion;
Section~\ref{sec:ch03:adcfloor} below shows how to treat an ADC as a noisy stage in the receiver's noise
budget.

\subsection{Impulsive and man-made noise}
\label{sec:ch03:impulsive}

Tune an AM radio to a quiet spot on the band and drive past a building site, or switch on a cheap LED
lamp: the hiss turns into a crackle. Below a few hundred megahertz the dominant noise in most places is
not thermal at all but man-made: ignition systems (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_sparkplug}), electric motors,
switch-mode power supplies, LED lighting drivers, plasma displays, power-line arcing and the clock
harmonics of every digital device. Nature adds its own crackle: lightning flashes dozens of times
a second somewhere on the planet, and each stroke is a broadband radio transmitter whose pulses the
ionosphere carries around the world.

Much of this noise is \emph{impulsive}: long quiet intervals punctuated by bursts. Its amplitude
distribution is far from Rayleigh (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_impulsive}). ITU-R P.372 characterises such
noise by its amplitude probability distribution and by the parameter $V_d$, the ratio in decibels of the
RMS to the average envelope: $V_d=1.05$\,dB for Gaussian noise ($20\log_{10}(2/\sqrt\pi)$), and values
of 5--15\,dB are common for man-made noise.

A widely used model is Middleton's \emph{class A} noise, a Poisson-weighted mixture of Gaussians: at any
instant the noise is Gaussian with variance $\sigma_m^2$ with probability $e^{-A}A^m/m!$, where $m$ is
the number of active impulse sources and the \emph{impulsive index} $A$ (typically 0.01--1) sets how
often impulses occur. Small $A$ means rare, strong impulses. A receiver designed for Gaussian noise is
badly mismatched to such noise: one impulse can wipe out a block of symbols, and the error-rate curve
develops a floor. Remedies include blanking or clipping before the matched filter (a crude approximation
of the optimal detector, which applies a nonlinearity to the samples), interleaving so that FEC sees
isolated errors, and OFDM, which spreads an impulse's energy across all subcarriers. Power-line
communication and DSL live with impulse noise; VDSL2 specifies an \emph{impulse noise protection}
parameter in symbols (Chapter~\ref{ch:wireline}).

\subsection{Antenna temperature: the noise of the sky}
\label{sec:ch03:antenna}

Point a radio antenna at the sky and it hears hiss, even with no transmitter anywhere. Some comes from
the warm ground in its sidelobes, some from the air, some from the stars and gas of the Milky Way, and a
little from the creation of the universe. Radio engineers have a wonderfully practical way to book-keep
all of it: express everything as a temperature.

An antenna is a receiver of thermal radiation as well as of signals. A body at physical temperature $T$
radiates; in the Rayleigh--Jeans limit ($hf\ll kT$) its brightness is proportional to $T$, and radio
engineers describe any radiation by its \term{brightness temperature} $T_B(\theta,\phi)$, the
temperature a black body would need to produce it. An antenna with power pattern $G(\theta,\phi)$
looking at a scene of brightness $T_B$ delivers to a matched receiver the noise power $kT_AB$, where the
\term{antenna temperature} is the pattern-weighted average
\begin{equation}
T_A=\frac{1}{4\pi}\oint G(\theta,\phi)\,T_B(\theta,\phi)\,d\Omega .
\label{eq:ch03:tant}
\end{equation}
\mdcfig[0.82]{ch03_skynoise}{External noise temperatures versus frequency. Straight lines: median
man-made and galactic noise from the ITU-R P.372 formulas (dashed: galactic trend extended above the
formula's range). Purple: an approximate clear-sky model at microwave frequencies at the zenith and at
10$^\circ$ elevation, showing the 22\,GHz water-vapour line and the 60\,GHz oxygen band. Between roughly
1 and 10\,GHz the sky is colder than any receiver.}

An antenna inside a closed box at uniform temperature $T$ therefore has $T_A=T$, whatever its
pattern---the transmission-line argument again. An antenna pointed at the sky sees the sum of several
contributions, plotted in Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_skynoise}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Man-made noise} (above), dominant below about 30\,MHz in cities and important up to a few
hundred megahertz. ITU-R P.372 gives median values of the external noise figure
$F_a=10\log_{10}(T_A/T_0)$ as $F_a=c-d\log_{10}f_{\text{MHz}}$ with, for example, $(c,d)=(76.8,27.7)$ for
business (city) areas, $(72.5,27.7)$ residential, $(67.2,27.7)$ rural and $(53.6,28.6)$ quiet rural,
valid from 0.3 to 250\,MHz.
\item \textbf{Atmospheric noise} in the ITU's sense: radiation from lightning, propagated worldwide by the
ionosphere. It dominates at LF to HF, especially at night and in the tropics.
\item \textbf{Galactic noise}: synchrotron radiation from relativistic electrons spiralling in the Milky
Way's magnetic field, with $F_a\approx52-23\log_{10}f_{\text{MHz}}$ (P.372) and strongest towards the
galactic centre in Sagittarius. It dominates the quiet sky from about 20\,MHz to 1\,GHz: at 100\,MHz the
cold sky is near 1000\,K.
\item \textbf{The cosmic microwave background}: a 2.725\,K black body filling the sky, the afterglow of the
hot early universe (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_cmb}).
\item \textbf{The atmosphere}: oxygen and water vapour absorb and therefore emit. In clear air the zenith
contribution is only a few kelvin between 1 and 10\,GHz, but rises near the 22\,GHz water line, becomes
total (the full 270--290\,K of the air) in the 60\,GHz oxygen band, and grows at low elevation angles,
where the path through the air is longer. A lossy medium with loss $L$ at physical temperature $T_m$
contributes $T_m(1-1/L)$; rain is such a medium, so a rain fade both weakens the signal \emph{and} raises
the noise (Chapter~\ref{ch:satellite}).
\item \textbf{The ground and the Sun}: sidelobes that see the 290\,K ground add tens of kelvin to a dish's
temperature; the Sun's microwave brightness temperature is around $10^4$\,K at 10\,GHz and higher at lower
frequencies, enough to blind a satellite receiver when the Sun passes behind the satellite---the \emph{sun
outage} that interrupts geostationary TV reception for a few minutes a day around the equinoxes.
\end{itemize}

The figure explains three facts of radio engineering. At HF (3--30\,MHz), external noise is so strong
that receiver noise figure hardly matters: a 10\,dB noise-figure receiver is as good as a perfect one, and
the art lies in antennas that reject local man-made noise. From VHF to the low gigahertz, man-made and
galactic noise still often exceed a good receiver's noise, which is why a TV or FM receiver's noise figure
is rarely critical. In the ``microwave window'' from about 1 to 10\,GHz, the sky is quiet and receiver
noise dominates, which is why satellite, deep-space and radio-astronomy systems live there and use
cryogenically cooled amplifiers.

\mdcphoto[0.8]{ch03_cmb}{The cosmic microwave background as mapped by NASA's WMAP satellite (nine-year
data). The whole sky glows at 2.725\,K; the colours show fluctuations of only about $\pm200\,\mu$K around
that value. To a radio engineer this is the coldest possible antenna temperature---the last few kelvin of
every satellite and deep-space noise budget.}

\begin{history}[Jansky's star noise and Penzias and Wilson's excess temperature]
In 1930 Bell Labs asked a young engineer, Karl Jansky, to find the sources of the static that plagued
the new transatlantic radiotelephone service. At Holmdel, New Jersey, he built a rotating directional
antenna for 20.5\,MHz---the ``merry-go-round'' (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_jansky_antenna})---and over more than
a year catalogued the static from nearby and distant thunderstorms. A third component remained: a steady
hiss whose direction drifted through the day. It returned to the same direction not every 24 hours but
every 23 hours and 56 minutes, a sidereal day, so its source was fixed among the stars; it was strongest
towards Sagittarius, the centre of the Milky Way. Jansky published ``Electrical disturbances apparently of
extraterrestrial origin'' in the \emph{Proceedings of the IRE} in 1933, founding radio astronomy; the unit
of radio flux density, the jansky, honours him.

Three decades later, at the same Holmdel site, Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson were using a 20-foot horn
antenna, built for the Echo and Telstar satellite experiments, and a maser receiver calibrated against a
liquid-helium cold load---the most carefully understood low-noise system of its day. After accounting for
the atmosphere, the antenna's losses and the ground, they were left with an excess antenna temperature of
about 3.5\,K at 4.08\,GHz, the same in every direction and at every time of year. They cleaned the horn,
famously evicting a pair of pigeons and their ``white dielectric material'', and the excess remained. A
group at Princeton led by Robert Dicke was at that moment building an instrument to search for the
predicted relic radiation of a hot early universe; the two papers appeared together in the
\emph{Astrophysical Journal} in 1965. Penzias and Wilson shared the 1978 Nobel Prize in Physics. The
cosmic microwave background was discovered as an entry in a noise-temperature budget---a reminder that
careful noise accounting is a scientific instrument.
\end{history}

\section{Noise figure and noise temperature}

\subsection{Definitions}

Walk into a noisy restaurant with a friend. The room is already loud; that is the antenna noise, and
nothing you do will make it quieter. Now your friend starts tapping a spoon on the table. The
conversation was hard before; now it is harder. The spoon is your receiver's own noise, and the
\emph{noise figure} measures how much worse your receiver makes an already noisy room.

Every real amplifier, mixer and filter adds noise of its own. Following D.~O.~North (RCA, 1942) and
H.~T.~Friis (Bell Labs, 1944), we characterise a two-port by how much it degrades the signal-to-noise
ratio. Its \term{noise factor} is
\begin{equation}
F=\frac{\mathrm{SNR}_{\text{in}}}{\mathrm{SNR}_{\text{out}}}\Bigg|_{\,T_{\text{source}}=T_0=290\,\mathrm K},
\label{eq:ch03:nfdef}
\end{equation}
and its \term{noise figure} is $\mathrm{NF}=10\log_{10}F$\,dB. In words: the number of decibels of SNR
the device throws away. The condition matters: the noise factor is defined with the input noise being
the thermal noise $kT_0B$ of a matched source at the standard temperature $T_0=290$\,K. (The value was
chosen as a typical room temperature; it also makes $kT_0$ a round $4.00\times10^{-21}$\,W/Hz.) An
equivalent form follows from writing the output noise as amplified input noise plus the device's own
contribution $N_a$: $F=(GkT_0B+N_a)/(GkT_0B)=1+N_a/(GkT_0B)$. A noiseless device has $F=1$ (0\,dB).

\begin{analogy}[How much worse your receiver makes a noisy room]
Noise figure compares the noise \emph{you} add with the noise that was already there in a standard,
room-temperature room. A 3\,dB noise figure means your receiver adds exactly as much noise as the room
supplied, doubling it. A 0.5\,dB noise figure means you add about an eighth as much. The analogy also
shows the trap: if the ``room'' is much quieter than standard---an antenna looking at the cold sky---the
same receiver noise is a much bigger fraction of the total, and the noise figure, defined for a
290\,K room, understates the damage. That is why space engineers think in temperatures instead.
\end{analogy}

The same device can be modelled as \emph{noiseless}, with an extra noise source at its input of
\term{equivalent noise temperature} $T_e$, chosen so that $N_a=GkT_eB$ (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_noisemodel}).
Comparing the two descriptions,
\begin{equation}
T_e=(F-1)\,T_0,\qquad F=1+\frac{T_e}{T_0}.
\label{eq:ch03:te}
\end{equation}
A 0.5\,dB noise figure corresponds to $T_e=35$\,K; 1\,dB to 75\,K; 2\,dB to 170\,K; 3\,dB to 290\,K; 6\,dB
to 865\,K; 10\,dB to 2610\,K (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_nf_te}). Noise temperature is the natural unit when the
input noise is not at 290\,K---for example, an antenna looking at the cold sky---and it is the unit
satellite and radio-astronomy engineers prefer, because it adds: a source at $T_s$ feeding a device with
noise temperature $T_e$ produces output noise $Gk(T_s+T_e)B$, and the output SNR is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SNR}_{\text{out}}=\frac{P_s}{k(T_s+T_e)B}.
\label{eq:ch03:snrtsys}
\end{equation}
Noise figure is convenient for terrestrial systems whose antennas see the 290\,K environment; for
anything else, convert to temperatures, add, and convert back.

\mdcside[0.44]{ch03_nf_te}{Converting noise figure to noise temperature. Below about 1\,dB the
temperature scale is far more sensitive: 0.5\,dB and 1\,dB differ by 40\,K, enough to matter when the sky
contributes only 20\,K.}

The asymmetry of the conversion is worth a moment's thought. Between 0 and 1\,dB of noise figure the
noise temperature sweeps from 0 to 75\,K; between 9 and 10\,dB it moves by more than 500\,K, yet the
SNR consequences for a terrestrial link are tiny in the first case and only a decibel in the second.
Which unit tells the truth depends on what else is in the room. When the antenna sees 290\,K, a
decibel of noise figure is a decibel of SNR to first order and decibels are the honest unit. When the
antenna sees 20\,K, every kelvin of receiver noise is a sizeable fraction of the total and kelvins are
the honest unit. Good engineers keep both columns in their spreadsheets.

\subsection{Passive lossy components}

Here is a result that surprises almost everyone the first time: a plain length of cable is a noise
source. A passive component with power loss $L\ge1$ (a cable, filter, attenuator, switch or the radome
in front of an antenna) at physical temperature $T_p$ is not noiseless: anything that absorbs also
emits. The proof is another equilibrium argument. Terminate its input in a matched load at the same
temperature $T_p$. Everything is in thermal equilibrium, so the output noise must be $kT_pB$. Of that,
the load contributes $kT_pB/L$ through the attenuation; the component itself must therefore contribute
$kT_pB(1-1/L)$, which referred to its input (multiply by $L$) is $kT_p(L-1)B$. Hence
\begin{equation}
T_e=(L-1)\,T_p,\qquad F=1+(L-1)\frac{T_p}{T_0},
\label{eq:ch03:lossy}
\end{equation}
and at $T_p=T_0$ simply $F=L$: a 2\,dB cable in front of a receiver adds exactly 2\,dB to the noise
figure. Cooling the loss helps: the same 2\,dB at 20\,K contributes only 12\,K instead of 169\,K, which is
why radio telescopes cool not only their amplifiers but their feed horns, polarisers and couplers.

\subsection{Cascades: the Friis formula}

\mdcpairany{ch03_friis}{Contribution of each stage to the cascade noise factor for two orderings of the
same components. Losses in front of the LNA add directly; everything after a high-gain LNA barely
matters.}{ch03_whisper_chain}{The same two line-ups as a whisper chain: cumulative SNR lost after each
stage. With the LNA first (green), the later stages hardly move the total; with it third (red), the
cable and filter have already done their damage.}

\begin{analogy}[The first person in a whisper chain matters most]
Play the party game ``telephone'': a message is whispered down a line of people, and whatever the
first listener mishears is passed faithfully to everyone after. A receiver is the same. Whatever noise
the first stage adds is amplified by every stage after it, along with the signal; noise added later is
small compared with a signal that has already been made loud. So put your best listener---the
low-noise amplifier---first, and make sure nobody mumbles (a lossy cable) before they hear it. The
analogy's limit: in the party game later players can also garble the message, whereas in a receiver a
late stage's noise really is divided by all the gain in front of it, which is why a noisy mixer behind
a good LNA hardly matters.
\end{analogy}

Consider two stages with gains $G_1,G_2$ and noise temperatures $T_{e1},T_{e2}$, driven by a source at
$T_s$. The output noise is the source noise and first-stage noise amplified by both stages, plus the
second stage's noise amplified by the second stage alone:
\[
N_{\text{out}}=G_1G_2k(T_s+T_{e1})B+G_2kT_{e2}B=G_1G_2k\Big(T_s+T_{e1}+\frac{T_{e2}}{G_1}\Big)B .
\]
The bracket identifies the cascade's noise temperature as $T_{e1}+T_{e2}/G_1$. Repeating the argument
for more stages, and converting with~\eqref{eq:ch03:te}, gives the formula Harald Friis published in 1944:
\begin{equation}
F=F_1+\frac{F_2-1}{G_1}+\frac{F_3-1}{G_1G_2}+\cdots,\qquad
T_e=T_{e1}+\frac{T_{e2}}{G_1}+\frac{T_{e3}}{G_1G_2}+\cdots
\label{eq:ch03:friis}
\end{equation}
Read it as the whisper chain in algebra: each stage's noise is divided by the total gain \emph{before}
it. The gains are \emph{available} power gains, and the formula assumes each stage's noise figure was
measured with the source impedance it actually sees in the cascade. The first stage dominates, provided
it has enough gain; this is the reason every sensitive receiver starts with a low-noise amplifier (LNA),
and why the LNA should be as close to the antenna as possible. A lossy stage has $G=1/L<1$, so it
\emph{multiplies} the contribution of everything after it. Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_friis} makes the point
numerically: moving a 1\,dB LNA from after a 2\,dB cable and a 1.5\,dB filter to directly at the antenna
lowers the system noise figure from about 5.1\,dB to 2.2\,dB, the equivalent of doubling the transmitter
power.

\begin{worked}[The noise budget of a handset receiver]
A cellular receive chain has, in order: an antenna switch (0.5\,dB loss), a SAW duplexer filter
(1.5\,dB), an LNA (NF 1.0\,dB, gain 18\,dB), a mixer (NF 10\,dB, conversion gain 8\,dB), a baseband
amplifier and filter (NF 12\,dB, gain 30\,dB) and an ADC with an effective noise figure of 27\,dB
(Section~\ref{sec:ch03:adcfloor}). Accumulating with~\eqref{eq:ch03:friis}, in linear units:

The 2\,dB of passive loss ahead of the LNA costs more than the LNA itself, and the mixer, despite its

the receiver's dynamic range are about (Chapter~\ref{ch:sdr}). The cascade noise figure of 3.6\,dB is
typical of a phone; the industry's reference sensitivity specifications assume about 9\,dB for the UE,
leaving room for worst-case bands, temperature and implementation.
\end{worked}

\begin{inpractice}[Mast-head amplifiers and satellite LNBs]
Cellular base stations often place a tower-mounted amplifier at the top of the mast, before tens of
metres of lossy coaxial cable. A satellite TV dish goes further: its low-noise block downconverter (LNB)
at the focus amplifies the 10.7--12.75\,GHz signal with a noise temperature under 100\,K and immediately
converts it to 950--2150\,MHz, where cable losses are tolerable. The Ettus B200, whose only LNA is the one
inside its AD9364 RFIC, is specified with a receive noise figure under 8\,dB---fine for bench work, but an
external LNA with 1\,dB NF will improve weak-signal reception by several decibels if placed at the antenna.
\end{inpractice}

\subsection{Antenna, line and system noise temperature}
\label{sec:ch03:tsys}

The \term{system noise temperature} combines the antenna's temperature and the receiver's, referred to a
single point. For an antenna at $T_{\text{ant}}$ connected through a feed of loss $L$ at physical
temperature $T_p$ to an LNA of noise temperature $T_{\text{LNA}}$ and gain $G$, followed by the rest of the
receiver at $T_{\text{rest}}$, the system temperature referred to the LNA input is
\begin{equation}
T_{\text{sys}}=\frac{T_{\text{ant}}}{L}+T_p\Big(1-\frac1L\Big)+T_{\text{LNA}}+\frac{T_{\text{rest}}}{G},
\label{eq:ch03:tsys}
\end{equation}
and the receiver's noise power is $kT_{\text{sys}}B$. The first term is the antenna noise attenuated by
the feed; the second is the feed's own emission. (Referred to the antenna terminals instead, every term is
multiplied by $L$; the signal is too, so the SNR is the same.) Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_lossyline} shows why
satellite and deep-space engineers obsess over a tenth of a decibel. With a cold sky ($T_{\text{ant}}=20$\,K)
and a good LNA, each 0.1\,dB of loss at 290\,K adds about 6.6\,K to $T_{\text{sys}}$, on top of losing
0.1\,dB of signal: half a decibel of feed loss degrades the SNR by nearly 3\,dB. With a warm antenna looking
at 290\,K (a terrestrial link), the same loss simply costs its own value.

\mdcfig{ch03_lossyline}{The cost of loss ahead of the LNA. Left: system noise temperature at the LNA input
from~\eqref{eq:ch03:tsys}. Right: total SNR degradation (signal loss plus noise increase) relative to a
lossless feed. For a cold-sky antenna a few tenths of a decibel of warm loss cost several decibels; for an
antenna seeing 290\,K the penalty equals the loss.}

For a satellite ground station the figure of merit is $G/T_{\text{sys}}$ in dB/K, the antenna gain divided
by the system temperature, both referred to the same point. Since the received carrier power is
proportional to $G$ and the noise density is $kT_{\text{sys}}$, $C/N_0$ is proportional to $G/T$ and the link
budget separates into transmitter, path and receiver terms (Section~\ref{sec:ch03:linkbudget} and
Chapter~\ref{ch:satellite}). Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_tsys_budget} shows an illustrative budget for a large
cryogenic receiver: every kelvin has a name.

\mdcfig[0.85]{ch03_tsys_budget}{An illustrative system-temperature budget for a large cryogenically cooled
microwave receiver at high elevation (values rounded and typical, not those of any particular antenna).
The universe, the air and the ground contribute roughly half; the rest is the price of the hardware.}

\mdcpairany{ch03_gbt}{The 100-metre Robert C.~Byrd Green Bank Telescope in West Virginia, the largest
fully steerable radio telescope. Its receivers are cryogenically cooled so that their own noise is

\begin{inpractice}[Cryogenic receivers: radio astronomy and the Deep Space Network]
When the sky contributes only a few kelvin, the receiver's noise is everything, and the answer is to cool
it. Radio telescopes and the antennas of NASA's Deep Space Network use HEMT (high-electron-mobility
transistor) amplifiers cooled by closed-cycle helium refrigerators to around 4--20\,K, at which temperatures
their noise temperatures are only a few kelvin at microwave frequencies; earlier generations used ruby masers
cooled in liquid helium (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_maser}). The resulting system temperatures, including the
atmosphere, the CMB and the antenna's own losses and spillover, are of order 20\,K at X-band (8.4\,GHz) for a
large DSN antenna at high elevation. A room-temperature LNA with a 0.5\,dB noise figure (35\,K) would roughly
double that, halving the data rate from a distant spacecraft. Cryogenic receivers pay for themselves many
times over when the alternative is a larger antenna.
\end{inpractice}

The maser in Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_maser} shows how far engineers were prepared to go. A ruby crystal,
pumped by microwaves at a higher frequency, amplified the weak signal by stimulated emission; the whole
assembly sat in liquid helium at about 4\,K, inside a jacket of liquid nitrogen, inside a vacuum flask,
and had to be refilled regularly. In return it offered noise temperatures of a few kelvin at a time when
the best room-temperature amplifiers were measured in hundreds. Masers of this kind listened to the early
space probes and, in the receiver of the Holmdel horn, made possible the measurement that found the cosmic
background. Today's cooled HEMT amplifiers are smaller, wider in bandwidth and far easier to live with,
and closed-cycle refrigerators have replaced the weekly helium delivery, but the principle is unchanged:
the colder the first stage, the quieter the whole receiver, and the smaller the antenna needed for a given
data rate. A kelvin saved at the front end is worth a square metre of dish.

\subsection{Mixers, image noise and the source impedance}

Two refinements matter in practice. First, \emph{mixers} convert two input bands to the same output: the
wanted signal band and the \emph{image} band on the other side of the LO. A single-sideband (SSB) noise
figure counts noise from both bands but signal from only one; a double-sideband (DSB) noise figure,
appropriate for radiometers and zero-IF receivers whose signal occupies both sidebands, counts signal in
both. For a mixer with equal response in both bands, the SSB figure is 3\,dB higher than the DSB figure.
Comparing a datasheet DSB number with an SSB requirement is a classic 3\,dB error. An image-reject filter
or image-reject mixer architecture removes the image noise (Chapter~\ref{ch:sdr}).

Second, the noise figure of an active device depends on the impedance of the source driving it:
\begin{equation}
F=F_{\min}+\frac{R_n}{G_s}\,\big|Y_s-Y_{\text{opt}}\big|^2,
\label{eq:ch03:fmin}
\end{equation}
where $Y_s=G_s+jB_s$ is the source admittance, $Y_{\text{opt}}$ the optimum, $F_{\min}$ the minimum noise
factor and $R_n$ the noise resistance. The optimum source for noise is generally not the conjugate match
that maximises gain, so LNA design is a compromise between noise figure, gain, input return loss and
stability, made on the Smith chart with circles of constant noise figure and constant gain (Pozar's
\emph{Microwave Engineering} is the standard treatment). Inductive source degeneration in CMOS LNAs is a
popular trick that brings the noise and power matches close together. Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_fmin_circles}
shows what~\eqref{eq:ch03:fmin} looks like to an LNA designer: circles of constant noise figure on the
Smith chart, centred on the optimum source reflection coefficient.

\mdcfig[0.48]{ch03_fmin_circles}{Constant-noise-figure circles from~\eqref{eq:ch03:fmin} for an
illustrative transistor with $\mathrm{NF}_{\min}=0.5$\,dB. A plain 50\,$\Omega$ source (centre) sits on the
0.75\,dB circle; matching the input for noise rather than for power recovers the difference.}

\subsection{Measuring noise figure: the Y-factor method}
\label{sec:ch03:yfactor}

How do you measure a quantity defined by a ratio of SNRs when you cannot see the signal and the noise
separately? The trick is to stop looking for a signal at all and feed the device \emph{noise}---two
different, precisely known amounts of it. The standard answer is the \term{Y-factor method}. Connect to
the device under test (DUT) a noise source that can be switched between two known temperatures, $T_h$
(hot) and $T_c$ (cold), and measure the output noise power in each state. Because the output power is a
straight line in the source temperature, $N=GkB(T_s+T_e)$, two points determine the line, and its
intercept on the temperature axis is $-T_e$ (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_yfactor}). With the measured ratio
$Y=N_h/N_c$,
\begin{equation}
Y=\frac{T_h+T_e}{T_c+T_e}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad T_e=\frac{T_h-YT_c}{Y-1}.
\label{eq:ch03:yfactor}
\end{equation}
Neither the gain nor the bandwidth needs to be known: they cancel in the ratio. Commercial noise sources
are avalanche diodes specified by their \term{excess noise ratio}, $\mathrm{ENR}=(T_h-T_0)/T_0$ (in dB),
with $T_c=T_0$ when the diode is off. Then~\eqref{eq:ch03:yfactor} simplifies to
\begin{equation}
F=\frac{\mathrm{ENR}}{Y-1}\qquad\text{(linear units)}.
\end{equation}

\mdcpairany{ch03_gr_noisegen}{A General Radio 1390-A random-noise generator, a classic bench instrument
for producing broadband noise of known level---the ``hot'' input of early noise-figure
measurements.}{ch03_noisetube}{A Rohde \& Schwarz noise-generator head (type SKTU) of the late 1960s.
Before solid-state avalanche-diode sources took over, hot sources were typically gas-discharge tubes or
temperature-limited diodes.}

\mdcfig{ch03_yfactor}{The Y-factor method. Left: output noise is linear in source temperature, so
measurements with a hot and a cold load fix the line and its intercept, $-T_e$. Right: error in the
measured noise figure caused by a 0.05\,dB error in measuring $Y$, for two noise-source ENRs. When the
DUT's noise is comparable with the hot source's excess noise, $Y$ approaches one and errors explode.}

\begin{worked}[Measuring an LNA]
A noise source with ENR 15.0\,dB (a factor of 31.6) drives an LNA of 20\,dB gain, followed by a
noise-figure analyzer whose own noise figure is 8\,dB. Switching the source on raises the measured power by
$Y=13.5$\,dB (22.4). The system noise factor is $31.6/(22.4-1)=1.478$, or 1.70\,dB. That includes the
analyzer, the second stage of the cascade; removing it with the Friis formula,
$F_1=1.478-(10^{0.8}-1)/100=1.425$, so the LNA's noise figure is 1.54\,dB, its noise temperature 123\,K.
For a cryogenic amplifier, by contrast, the hot--cold method with physical loads is more accurate: with an
ambient load at 290\,K and a liquid-nitrogen load at 77\,K (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_dewar}), a measured $Y$ of
5.05\,dB (3.20) gives $T_e=(290-3.20\times77)/(3.20-1)\approx20$\,K. Note how sensitive this is: an error of
0.1\,dB in $Y$ changes the answer by about 3\,K, 15\%.
\end{worked}

The liquid-nitrogen cold load is one of the most satisfying instruments in RF engineering, because its
calibration comes from physics rather than from a certificate. Nitrogen boils at 77\,K at sea-level
pressure, so a well-matched absorber immersed in it radiates as a 77\,K black body without anyone having to
trust a diode. Radio astronomers use the same idea on a grand scale: they point the telescope alternately
at a patch of empty sky and at an absorber at ambient temperature, or switch a calibrated noise diode into
the feed, and every few seconds they re-measure the line of Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_yfactor}. The Holmdel
horn's receiver was calibrated against a liquid-helium load in exactly this way, which is why Penzias and
Wilson could trust a residual of 3.5\,K. A measurement of noise is only as good as the temperatures it is
compared with.

\mdcphoto[0.32]{ch03_dewar}{A small laboratory Dewar. Filled with liquid nitrogen at 77\,K and holding a
matched load, it becomes the ``cold'' source of a hot--cold noise measurement.}

\begin{inpractice}[Y-factor measurements in the lab]
Noise-figure analyzers and spectrum analyzers with a noise-figure option automate the procedure: they pulse
a 28\,V supply to a calibrated noise source (ENR of about 5--6\,dB or about 15\,dB are common models),
measure the hot and cold powers across a list of frequencies, look up the source's ENR calibration table,
and apply the second-stage correction automatically after a calibration step without the DUT. Good
practice, much of it in Keysight's application note 57-2: use a low-ENR source for low-noise-figure DUTs
(it changes the source's impedance less between states and keeps the analyzer linear); make sure $Y$ is
well above 1 (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_yfactor}, right); keep the DUT's output well below compression, since the
measured power is broadband noise; add an isolator when the DUT's noise figure is sensitive to the source
match; and remember that the noise source's ENR assumes it is at 290\,K---on a hot bench, correct for it.
For frequency-converting DUTs, decide whether you want the SSB or the DSB figure before you start. An
alternative, the \emph{cold-source} method, measures only the DUT's output noise with a cold (ambient)
termination and relies on an accurately known gain; vector network analyzers use it because they already
measure gain precisely.
\end{inpractice}

\begin{tryit}[Measure a noise figure]
Lab 33's \emph{The Y-factor measurement} simulates 400 repeated measurements. With \emph{Excess noise ratio}
at 15\,dB and a 1.5\,dB DUT, the histogram is a tight spike around the truth. Raise the \emph{True noise
figure} towards 10\,dB and watch the spread widen as $Y$ approaches one; switch the ENR to 5\,dB and it
widens faster. Turn off \emph{Subtract the analyser's noise} with a low DUT gain to see the second-stage
error the Friis correction removes.
\end{tryit}

\begin{inpractice}[Noise figures of real receivers]
Representative, approximate values: a discrete GaAs pHEMT LNA at 2\,GHz, 0.4--0.8\,dB; a GPS active-antenna
LNA, about 1\,dB; an LNA integrated in a CMOS transceiver, 1.5--3\,dB; the complete receive chain of a
smartphone, including the antenna switch and duplexer, roughly 3--7\,dB depending on the band; a Wi-Fi
access point, about 4--6\,dB; a software-defined radio such as the Ettus B200, 5--8\,dB depending on frequency
and gain setting; a Ku-band satellite LNB, 0.5--1\,dB; a millimetre-wave 5G phased-array front end at
28\,GHz, 5--8\,dB; a cryogenic radio-astronomy receiver, a few kelvin. The specifications are more
conservative: 3GPP's reference sensitivity derivations assume a UE noise figure of about 9\,dB and the IEEE
802.11 minimum sensitivities are consistent with about 10\,dB (Section~\ref{sec:ch03:sensitivity}). The gap
is margin for production spread, temperature, the worst band and the loss of the antenna itself.
\end{inpractice}

\section{Receiver sensitivity}
\label{sec:ch03:sensitivity}
%======================================================================

\subsection{The sensitivity equation}

How faint a signal can your receiver hear? Every radio datasheet answers with one number, its
sensitivity, and that number can be rebuilt with nothing but a pencil and the ideas of this chapter.

The \term{sensitivity} of a receiver is the smallest input power at which it meets a specified
quality---a bit-error rate, packet-error rate or throughput. Three numbers determine it: the thermal noise
in the receiver's noise bandwidth, the receiver's noise figure, and the SNR the demodulator needs:
\begin{equation}
P_{\text{sens}}\,[\mathrm{dBm}]=-174+10\log_{10}B+\mathrm{NF}+\mathrm{SNR}_{\text{req}}\,(+\,\text{implementation margin}).
\label{eq:ch03:sens}
\end{equation}
Each term has a home in this book. The $-174$ is physics (Section~\ref{sec:ch03:thermal}). The bandwidth
is the \emph{noise} bandwidth, which for a matched filter equals the symbol rate
(Section~\ref{sec:ch03:processes}). The noise figure is the cascade of Section~\ref{sec:ch03:nf}. The
required SNR comes from the modulation and coding (Chapters~\ref{ch:modulation}, \ref{ch:classiccodes}
and~\ref{ch:moderncodes}) and from the channel---static, or faded and with diversity.
Chapter~\ref{ch:channels} turns this equation into the maximum allowable path loss of a cellular link
budget. Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_sens_stack} builds it like a staircase.

\subsection{Sensitivity in the standards}

Every air-interface standard specifies a minimum sensitivity, and the numbers can be reconstructed
from~\eqref{eq:ch03:sens} with the assumptions the standards' authors used. Two examples show how.

\mdcfig[0.82]{ch03_sensitivity}{Thermal noise floor $kT_0B$ against the sensitivities and received levels
of real systems, plotted at their noise bandwidths. Cellular and Wi-Fi sensitivities (specification
values) lie above the floor by the noise figure plus the required SNR; spread-spectrum systems (LoRa, GPS)
operate below it.}

\paragraph{IEEE 802.11a/g.} The standard (IEEE 802.11, OFDM PHY clause) requires a packet-error rate below
10\% for 1000-byte packets at $-82$\,dBm for the 6\,Mb/s mode and $-65$\,dBm for 54\,Mb/s, in a 20\,MHz
channel. The thermal noise in 20\,MHz is $-101$\,dBm; the usual reconstruction adds a 10\,dB noise figure
and a 5\,dB implementation margin, leaving a required SNR of about 4\,dB for BPSK rate-1/2 and about 21\,dB
for 64-QAM rate-3/4. A real access point beats these figures by 5--10\,dB, which is why vendors quote their
own sensitivities.

\paragraph{LTE and NR.} For an LTE UE in a 10\,MHz carrier (band 1), 3GPP TS 36.101 specifies a
\emph{reference sensitivity} (REFSENS) of about $-97$\,dBm for 95\% of maximum throughput with QPSK and a
low code rate, received on two antennas. A commonly quoted reconstruction: thermal noise in the 9\,MHz
occupied by 50 resource blocks, $-104.5$\,dBm; UE noise figure 9\,dB; required SNR about $-1$\,dB; a
two-antenna diversity gain of 3\,dB; and an implementation margin of 2.5\,dB:
$-104.5+9-1-3+2.5=-97$\,dBm. TS 38.101-1 specifies NR's REFSENS per band and bandwidth in the same way.
These numbers are minimums that every phone must meet in every band; they are also the starting point of
operators' link budgets.

Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_sensitivity} places these and other systems against the thermal floor. Most receivers
sit 5--20\,dB above $kT_0B$. Two sit far below it: LoRa, whose chirp spread spectrum with spreading factor
12 is demodulated about 20\,dB below the noise in its 125\,kHz channel (datasheet sensitivities near
$-137$\,dBm), and GPS, whose signal is received about 19\,dB below the noise in its 2\,MHz bandwidth.
Neither violates physics: both trade bandwidth for SNR by spreading, and their \emph{post-despreading}
SNR, in the much smaller bandwidth of the information, is positive (Chapter~\ref{ch:spreadspectrum}).

\mdcfig[0.5]{ch03_adc_floor}{Total noise figure of a 3\,dB analog chain followed by an ADC, against the
analog gain between them. Below about 30\,dB of gain the ADC's floor dominates; far above it, strong
signals start to clip.}

\subsection{The ADC as a noisy stage}
\label{sec:ch03:adcfloor}

An ADC adds quantisation and thermal noise, and it is convenient to describe it, like any other stage, by
a noise figure. If its full-scale input power is $P_{\text{FS}}$ (dBm, into the specified impedance) and
its measured SNR for a full-scale signal is $\mathrm{SNR}_{\text{ADC}}$, spread uniformly over the Nyquist
band $f_s/2$, its noise density is $P_{\text{FS}}-\mathrm{SNR}_{\text{ADC}}-10\log_{10}(f_s/2)$\,dBm/Hz, and
its effective noise figure is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{NF}_{\text{ADC}}=P_{\text{FS}}-\mathrm{SNR}_{\text{ADC}}-10\log_{10}(f_s/2)+174\ \ \mathrm{dB}.
\label{eq:ch03:nfadc}
\end{equation}
An ideal 12-bit converter (SNR 74\,dB) at 100\,MS/s with a full scale of $+4$\,dBm has a noise density of
$-147$\,dBm/Hz and an effective noise figure of 27\,dB; a 14-bit converter at 250\,MS/s, about 11\,dB in the
ideal case and more in practice. These are poor noise figures, which is why the analog chain must provide
enough gain to lift the antenna's thermal noise comfortably above the ADC's floor---typically by 10\,dB or
more, so that the ADC adds a few tenths of a decibel to the cascade (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_adc_floor}). Too
little gain and the ADC dominates the noise figure; too much and strong signals clip. The usable range
between is the receiver's \emph{dynamic range}, the central trade-off of software-defined radio design
(Chapter~\ref{ch:sdr}). Equation~\eqref{eq:ch03:nfadc} also shows the reward of a high sample rate:
doubling $f_s$ improves $\mathrm{NF}_{\text{ADC}}$ by 3\,dB, because the same noise is spread more thinly
and the digital filters discard most of it.

\section{SNR, \texorpdfstring{$E_b/N_0$}{Eb/N0}, \texorpdfstring{$E_s/N_0$}{Es/N0} and \texorpdfstring{$C/N_0$}{C/N0}}
\label{sec:ch03:snr}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Four ratios}

Ask four engineers how strong a signal is and you may get four different numbers, all correct: an SNR, an
$E_b/N_0$, an $E_s/N_0$ and a $C/N_0$. They are four rulers for the same quantity, each graduated for a
different job, and translating between them is daily work (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_ratios}).
\begin{itemize}
\item The \textbf{signal-to-noise ratio} $\mathrm{SNR}=P/(N_0B)$ depends on the bandwidth $B$ in which
the noise is measured. Always ask: which bandwidth?
\item The \textbf{carrier-to-noise-density ratio} $C/N_0=P/N_0$, in dB-Hz, is independent of bandwidth.
It is the natural figure of merit for a link budget, because it can be computed before the modulation is
chosen. GPS receivers report it for each satellite; 45\,dB-Hz is a strong GPS signal and 30\,dB-Hz a weak
one.
\item The \textbf{energy per symbol to noise density} $E_s/N_0=P/(R_sN_0)$ is what the demodulator sees:
with a matched filter, the SNR at the sampler.
\item The \textbf{energy per bit to noise density} $E_b/N_0=P/(R_bN_0)$ normalises by the information bit
rate $R_b$. It is the fair basis for comparing modulation and coding schemes, because it charges each
scheme for the energy it spends per bit of information.
\end{itemize}

Since $E_b=P/R_b$ and $E_s=P/R_s$, and an $M$-ary scheme with code rate $R_c$ carries
$R_b=R_s\log_2M\cdot R_c$ information bits per second, they are related by
\begin{equation}
\frac{E_b}{N_0}=\frac{C}{N_0}\cdot\frac{1}{R_b}=\mathrm{SNR}\cdot\frac{B}{R_b},
\qquad
\frac{E_s}{N_0}=\frac{E_b}{N_0}\cdot\log_2M\cdot R_c ,
\label{eq:ch03:ebno}
\end{equation}
where $E_s$ is the energy per symbol. The energy per \emph{coded} bit is $E_c=R_cE_b$; a rate-1/2 code
spends half of each information bit's energy on each of two coded bits, which is why a coded system's raw
(pre-decoder) bit-error rate is worse than an uncoded one's at the same $E_b/N_0$---and its decoded error
rate far better. In decibels everything is addition: $E_b/N_0=C/N_0-10\log_{10}R_b$, and
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SNR}=\frac{E_b}{N_0}+10\log_{10}\frac{R_b}{B}=\frac{E_s}{N_0}+10\log_{10}\frac{R_s}{B}\quad\text{(dB)}.
\label{eq:ch03:snrdb}
\end{equation}
The ratio $R_b/B$ is the \emph{spectral efficiency} $\eta$ in b/s/Hz, so $\mathrm{SNR}=\eta\,E_b/N_0$.
Substituting into Shannon's formula gives the minimum $E_b/N_0$ for reliable communication at efficiency
$\eta$, $(2^\eta-1)/\eta$, which tends to $\ln2$, or $-1.59$\,dB, as $\eta\to0$---the ultimate limit on
energy per bit (Chapter~\ref{ch:infotheory}).

\begin{worked}[From $C/N_0$ to bit rate]
A receiver measures $C/N_0=60$\,dB-Hz. A QPSK link with a rate-1/2 code needs $E_b/N_0=2$\,dB at the target
error rate. The maximum bit rate is $R_b=(C/N_0)/(E_b/N_0)=10^{(60-2)/10}\approx630$\,kb/s. With 3\,dB of
implementation margin, plan for 315\,kb/s. The symbol rate is then $R_s=R_b/(2\times\tfrac12)=315$\,kBd, and
with RRC filtering of roll-off 0.25 the occupied bandwidth is about 394\,kHz. The SNR in that occupied
bandwidth is $E_b/N_0+3+10\log_{10}(315/394)\approx4$\,dB, a number that would look alarmingly low on a
spectrum analyzer and is perfectly adequate for the modem.
\end{worked}

\subsection{Getting the noise right in simulation}

A simulation at $N_{\text{sps}}$ samples per symbol, with symbols of energy $E_s$ (in units of
sample-power $\times$ sample-period), must add complex noise of variance
\begin{equation}
\sigma^2=\frac{N_{\text{sps}}\,P_{\text{sample}}}{E_s/N_0}=\frac{N_{\text{sps}}\,P_{\text{sample}}}{(E_b/N_0)\,R_c\log_2M}
\label{eq:ch03:simnoise}
\end{equation}
per sample, where $P_{\text{sample}}$ is the average signal power per sample (usually normalised to one).
The factor $N_{\text{sps}}$ is the one most often forgotten: oversampling spreads the same noise density
over a wider simulated bandwidth, so each sample carries more noise, and the matched filter, which
integrates over $N_{\text{sps}}$ samples, collects it back. Measuring the SNR per sample of an oversampled
waveform and calling it $E_s/N_0$ is a 9\,dB error at 8 samples per symbol.

\mdcfig{ch03_sps_noise}{The same simulation seen two ways. Left: at 8 samples per symbol the SNR per
sample is about $-1$\,dB and the waveform looks hopeless. Right: after the matched filter has gathered the
energy of each symbol, $E_s/N_0=8$\,dB and the two levels are cleanly separated. Mixing up the two ratios is
a 9\,dB error.}

\begin{pitfall}[What counts as a bit?]
$E_b$ is the energy per \emph{information} bit, and every overhead must be charged to it: the cyclic
prefix of OFDM (about 7\% in LTE), pilot and reference symbols, preambles, guard bands that carry no data,
CRC and header bits, and the energy in pulse-shaping excess bandwidth if the noise bandwidth is wider than
the symbol rate. Published curves of BER versus $E_b/N_0$ for coded systems are comparable only if they
agree on this accounting. When comparing a datasheet's ``SNR'' with a textbook's $E_b/N_0$, convert
explicitly with~\eqref{eq:ch03:snrdb} and state the bandwidth.
\end{pitfall}

\section{Detecting a signal in noise}
\label{sec:ch03:detection}
%======================================================================

\subsection{The binary hypothesis test}

Every kitchen has a detection engineer bolted to the ceiling. A smoke alarm measures something noisy---the
particles drifting into its chamber---and must decide, many times a minute, between ``nothing is wrong'' and
``fire''. Set it too sensitive and it shrieks every time you make toast; set it too dull and it sleeps through
the fire. No setting avoids both mistakes; you can only choose which to suffer more often.

\mdcpairany{ch03_smoke}{A smoke detector on a ceiling: a binary detector running continuously, with a
threshold someone had to choose.}{ch03_ppi}{A wartime radar plan-position indicator (Bell Telephone
Magazine, 1945): a coastline painted by echoes above a speckle of receiver noise. Every bright spot is a
decision that something is there.}

The simplest question a receiver can ask is the smoke alarm's: is there a signal at all? A radar asks it in
every range cell (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_ppi}), a Wi-Fi receiver at every sample while waiting for a preamble,
a GPS receiver for every code phase and Doppler bin, a 5G base station for every random-access opportunity.
Formally, the receiver observes $\vect r$ and chooses between two hypotheses,
\begin{equation}
H_0:\ \vect r=\vect n,\qquad H_1:\ \vect r=\vect s+\vect n .
\end{equation}
Two errors are possible: a \term{false alarm} (deciding $H_1$ when $H_0$ is true), with probability
$P_{FA}$, and a \emph{miss} (deciding $H_0$ under $H_1$), with probability $1-P_D$, where $P_D$ is the
\term{probability of detection}. Any detector can trade one for the other by moving its threshold. The
curve of $P_D$ against $P_{FA}$ traced out as the threshold moves is the \term{receiver operating
characteristic} (ROC), a name that comes from the radar receivers of the Second World War.

\begin{analogy}[A smoke alarm and its threshold]
Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_smoke_roc} puts numbers on the kitchen. The sensor reading under ``toast'' and under
``fire'' are two overlapping bell curves. A hair-trigger threshold catches almost every fire but goes off
for toast more than a third of the time; a dull one almost never false-alarms but misses most fires. Every
threshold is one point on a single curve, the ROC; a better \emph{sensor}---more separation between the
bells---pushes the whole curve towards the top-left corner, and nothing else does. The analogy's limit: in
radio, unlike in the kitchen, we can often make the sensor better simply by listening longer, because the
separation grows with the signal energy collected.
\end{analogy}

\mdcfig{ch03_smoke_roc}{The smoke-alarm trade-off. Left: sensor readings without and with a fire, and three
thresholds. Right: the receiver operating characteristic; each threshold is one point on it, with its miss
and false-alarm probabilities.}

\subsection{The Neyman--Pearson lemma}

If the priors and the costs of the two errors are known, the Bayes decision rule of
Section~\ref{sec:ch03:prob} applies: compare the likelihood ratio with a threshold set by the priors and
costs. In detection problems the priors are usually unknown (how likely is an enemy aircraft?), and the
natural criterion is that of Neyman and Pearson (1933): \emph{maximise $P_D$ subject to $P_{FA}\le\alpha$}.
The \term{Neyman--Pearson lemma} says the optimal detector is again a likelihood-ratio test,
\begin{equation}
\Lambda(\vect r)=\frac{p(\vect r\mid H_1)}{p(\vect r\mid H_0)}\ \underset{H_0}{\overset{H_1}{\gtrless}}\ \gamma,
\label{eq:ch03:lrt}
\end{equation}
with the threshold $\gamma$ chosen to make $P_{FA}=\alpha$. In words: for every possible observation, ask
``how much more likely is this if the signal is present than if it is not?'', and say ``signal'' whenever the
answer exceeds a number fixed by the false-alarm rate you can tolerate.

The proof is a short exchange argument, like filling a knapsack: you have a fixed budget of false-alarm
probability to spend, and each region of observation space costs some $P_{FA}$ and buys some $P_D$. The
best buys are the regions with the highest ratio of $P_D$ to $P_{FA}$---the highest likelihood ratio---so you
buy those first until the budget is gone. Any other decision region with the same $P_{FA}$ must give up
some points with high likelihood ratio in exchange for points with lower likelihood ratio, and so loses
detection probability. Every optimal detector, MAP or Neyman--Pearson, computes the same statistic; they
differ only in the threshold. The ROC of a likelihood-ratio test is concave, lies above the diagonal
$P_D=P_{FA}$ (the performance of a coin toss), and has slope $\gamma$ at the operating point.

\subsection{Known signal in white Gaussian noise}

For a known signal $s(t)$ of energy $E$ in white noise of density $N_0/2$, the log-likelihood ratio reduces
(Chapter~\ref{ch:modulation}) to the correlation $y=\int r(t)s(t)\,dt$: a matched filter sampled at the
right instant. Under $H_0$, $y$ is Gaussian with mean 0 and variance $EN_0/2$; under $H_1$ the mean is $E$.
Setting the threshold for a given $P_{FA}$ and computing $P_D$,
\begin{equation}
P_{FA}=\Q\!\left(\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{EN_0/2}}\right),\qquad
P_D=\Q\!\big(\Q^{-1}(P_{FA})-d\big),\qquad d=\sqrt{\frac{2E}{N_0}} .
\label{eq:ch03:pd}
\end{equation}
The \emph{deflection} $d$ is the separation of the two Gaussian hypotheses in standard deviations, and it
depends only on the signal energy, not its shape (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_roc}). To reach $P_D=0.9$ at
$P_{FA}=10^{-6}$ requires $d=4.75+1.28=6.03$, an $E/N_0$ of 12.6\,dB.

\mdcfig{ch03_roc}{Detection in Gaussian noise. Left: the test statistic under the two hypotheses; the
threshold $\gamma$ sets $P_{FA}$ (dark area) and $P_D$ (light area). Centre: ROC curves for a known signal at
deflections $d=\sqrt{2E/N_0}$ from 1 to 4. Right: $P_D$ versus $E/N_0$ at $P_{FA}=10^{-6}$ for coherent
detection, envelope detection of a signal of unknown phase, and an energy detector integrating 16 complex
samples of an unknown signal.}

\subsection{Unknown phase, unknown signal}

A radar echo or a burst preamble arrives with an unknown carrier phase. The optimal detector then
correlates with both the in-phase and quadrature versions of the expected waveform and compares the
\emph{envelope} $|y|$ with a threshold---a noncoherent detector. Under $H_0$ the envelope is Rayleigh, so
the threshold for a given false-alarm rate follows from $P_{FA}=e^{-\gamma^2/2\sigma^2}$; under $H_1$ it is
Rice, and
\begin{equation}
P_D=Q_1\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{2E}{N_0}},\ \sqrt{-2\ln P_{FA}}\right),
\label{eq:ch03:marcum}
\end{equation}
where $Q_1(a,b)$ is the \term{Marcum Q function}, the tail of the Rice distribution, introduced by
J.~I.~Marcum in his 1948 RAND report on radar detection. The penalty for not knowing the phase is
surprisingly small at high $P_D$: $P_D=0.9$, $P_{FA}=10^{-6}$ needs 13.2\,dB instead of 12.6\,dB. Radar
engineers estimate it with Albersheim's empirical equation, which for a single pulse reads
$\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{dB}}\approx\big(6.2+4.54/\sqrt{1.44}\big)\log_{10}(A+0.12AB+1.7B)$, with
$A=\ln(0.62/P_{FA})$ and $B=\ln(P_D/(1-P_D))$; for $P_D=0.9$, $P_{FA}=10^{-6}$ it gives 13.1\,dB.

When nothing is known about the signal except its band, the best one can do is measure energy: the
\emph{energy detector} (radiometer) sums $|r_k|^2$ over $N$ samples and compares with a threshold. Under
$H_0$ the sum is chi-square with $2N$ degrees of freedom; under $H_1$ it is noncentral chi-square. It needs
several decibels more energy than a coherent detector (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_roc}, right) and, worse, its
threshold depends on knowing the noise level precisely---the weakness that cyclostationary detectors avoid
(Section~\ref{sec:ch03:cyclo}).

\mdcfig{ch03_cfar}{Why radars use CFAR. Received power in 400 range cells; the right half lies in rain
clutter six times stronger than the receiver noise. A fixed threshold set for $P_{FA}=10^{-4}$ in quiet
conditions (orange) is crossed dozens of times by clutter alone. A cell-averaging CFAR threshold (green),
computed from 32 neighbouring cells, rises with the clutter and still catches both targets.}

\subsection{False alarms in many cells}

A detector is rarely run once. A surveillance radar tests perhaps $10^6$ range--Doppler cells a second; at
$P_{FA}=10^{-6}$ per cell that is one false target per second, which is why radar designers quote
false-alarm \emph{rates} and set per-cell probabilities of $10^{-6}$ to $10^{-10}$. Because the noise and
clutter level is never known exactly, practical radars use \emph{constant false-alarm rate} (CFAR)
detectors that estimate the local noise level from neighbouring cells and scale the threshold accordingly
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_cfar}).

\begin{inpractice}[Acquisition: GPS, Wi-Fi and 5G random access]
A GPS receiver searching for a satellite without assistance tests 1023 code phases (in half-chip steps,
2046) times a few tens of Doppler bins, of order $10^5$ cells. To keep the probability of a false
acquisition over the whole search to a few per cent, the per-cell $P_{FA}$ must be near $10^{-7}$, and with
the signal at $C/N_0=40$\,dB-Hz a 1\,ms coherent integration gives only about $10$\,dB of $E/N_0$---not
enough. Receivers therefore integrate coherently for several milliseconds and then noncoherently over many
blocks, and verify each detection before declaring lock (Chapter~\ref{ch:spreadspectrum}). A Wi-Fi
receiver runs a short-preamble autocorrelation detector continuously and must not fire on noise more than
occasionally, because each false trigger occupies the receiver; it confirms with the long preamble. A 5G gNB
searches for random-access preambles in every PRACH occasion with a threshold set for a specified
false-alarm probability (the performance requirements in TS 38.104 specify a false-alarm probability of
$10^{-3}$ or less and a detection probability of at least 99\% at a stated SNR). Each is a Neyman--Pearson
design.
\end{inpractice}

\section{Estimating parameters in noise}
\label{sec:ch03:estimation}
%======================================================================

Detection asks \emph{whether}; estimation asks \emph{how much}. Before it can demodulate a single bit, a
receiver must estimate the carrier frequency and phase, the symbol timing, the channel gain and the noise
level, and Chapters~\ref{ch:sync} and~\ref{ch:equalization} are largely about how. A carpenter measuring a
board twice and averaging is doing estimation theory; so is a GPS receiver measuring its distance to four
satellites a thousand times a second. Here are the principles.

\mdcpairany{ch03_wiener}{The Wiener filter: unity gain where the signal spectrum towers over the noise,
gentle roll-off where they are comparable, and strong suppression where noise
dominates.}{ch03_crb_curvature}{Fisher information as curvature. The log-likelihood of a level in noise
from 4 and from 40 samples. More data make the peak ten times sharper in curvature, and the estimate
correspondingly more precise.}

\subsection{Maximum likelihood and MMSE}

An \emph{estimator} $\hat\theta(\vect r)$ is any function of the observations. Its quality is measured by
its bias $\E[\hat\theta]-\theta$ and its mean-square error
$\mathrm{MSE}=\E[(\hat\theta-\theta)^2]=\text{variance}+\text{bias}^2$. Two philosophies dominate.

The \term{maximum-likelihood} (ML) estimator treats $\theta$ as an unknown constant and chooses the value
that makes the observations most probable, $\hat\theta_{\text{ML}}=\arg\max_\theta p(\vect r;\theta)$. For a
constant level $A$ in white Gaussian noise, $r_k=A+n_k$, the likelihood is maximised by the sample mean
$\bar r=\frac1N\sum r_k$, with variance $\sigma^2/N$. For a complex tone of unknown frequency in white noise,
the ML estimate is the location of the peak of the periodogram---which is why an FFT followed by
peak-picking and interpolation is the standard coarse frequency estimator.

The \term{minimum mean-square error} (MMSE) estimator treats $\theta$ as random with a known prior and
minimises the average MSE; the answer is the posterior mean, $\hat\theta=\E[\theta\mid\vect r]$. With a
Gaussian prior of variance $\sigma_A^2$ on the level $A$ above, it is
$\hat A=\frac{\sigma_A^2}{\sigma_A^2+\sigma^2/N}\,\bar r$: the sample mean shrunk towards the prior mean,
strongly when data are few and hardly at all when they are many (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_crb}, right). It is
common sense made precise: trust your prior when the evidence is thin, trust the data when it is plentiful.
Restricting the MMSE estimator to be linear gives the \emph{linear MMSE} estimator, which needs only
second-order statistics; applied to a stationary signal in stationary noise, it is the \term{Wiener filter},
$H(f)=S_s(f)/(S_s(f)+S_n(f))$, which passes frequencies where the signal dominates and suppresses those where
the noise does (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_wiener}). The MMSE equaliser of Chapter~\ref{ch:equalization} and the
LMMSE channel estimator of OFDM receivers are Wiener filters.

\mdcfig{ch03_crb}{Estimation in noise. Left: mean-square error of the ML frequency estimate (peak of a
finely interpolated periodogram) of a complex tone, $N=64$ samples, against the Cram\'er--Rao bound. Above
about 0\,dB SNR per sample the estimator is efficient; below, outliers create a threshold. Right: estimating
a level in noise. The sample mean meets the CRB $\sigma^2/N$; with a Gaussian prior (variance 0.25) the MMSE
estimator does better when $N$ is small, by shrinking towards the prior.}

\subsection{The Cram\'er--Rao bound}

How good can an unbiased estimator be? Picture the log-likelihood as a hill whose summit is the estimate. If
the hill is a sharp peak, small noise barely moves the summit; if it is a gentle dome, a little noise can
slide the summit a long way (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_crb_curvature}). The \term{Cram\'er--Rao bound} (CRB) turns
this picture into a number:
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{var}(\hat\theta)\ \ge\ \frac{1}{I(\theta)},\qquad
I(\theta)=-\E\!\left[\frac{\partial^2\ln p(\vect r;\theta)}{\partial\theta^2}\right],
\label{eq:ch03:crb}
\end{equation}
where $I(\theta)$ is the \emph{Fisher information}: the curvature of the log-likelihood, a measure of how
sharply the data pin down the parameter. For the level in Gaussian noise, $I=N/\sigma^2$, so the sample mean,
with variance $\sigma^2/N$, is \emph{efficient}: it meets the bound. For the frequency $f$ (in cycles per
sample) of a complex tone with SNR $A^2/\sigma^2$ observed for $N$ samples, Rife and Boorstyn (1974) showed
that
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{var}(\hat f)\ \ge\ \frac{6}{(2\pi)^2\,\mathrm{SNR}\,N(N^2-1)} .
\label{eq:ch03:crbfreq}
\end{equation}
The variance falls as $N^{-3}$, not $N^{-1}$: a longer observation both collects more energy and measures a
phase that has rotated further. Doubling the length of a frequency-estimation preamble reduces the RMS error
by a factor of $2^{3/2}\approx2.8$.

The CRB is a small-error bound and ML estimators achieve it asymptotically---at high SNR or long
observation. At low SNR something else happens: the periodogram's peak is occasionally captured by a noise
peak far from the true frequency, the error variance jumps by orders of magnitude, and the estimator departs
from the bound (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_crb}, left). This \term{threshold effect} is universal in nonlinear
estimation---the same phenomenon as the FM threshold of Chapter~\ref{ch:analog} and the cycle slips of a
phase-locked loop in Chapter~\ref{ch:sync}---and it is why synchronisation algorithms are designed to work
well above their threshold.

\begin{keyidea}[Energy decides; the bound tells you how close you are]
Both detection and estimation performance are governed by the total signal energy relative to
$N_0$---$2E/N_0$ for detection, $N\cdot\mathrm{SNR}$ for estimating a level---not by the SNR per sample. A
weak signal observed for a long time is as detectable and as measurable as a strong one observed briefly,
provided the receiver can integrate coherently. The Cram\'er--Rao bound gives the target for any estimator
before it is designed; a synchronisation algorithm within a decibel of the bound is finished.
\end{keyidea}

\section{The link budget}
\label{sec:ch03:linkbudget}
%======================================================================

\subsection{The Friis transmission equation}

\mdcfig[0.5]{ch03_fspl}{Is a higher frequency lossier? With fixed-gain antennas, received power falls
20\,dB per decade of frequency; with fixed-size dishes, it \emph{rises} 20\,dB per decade.}

Every piece of this chapter now comes together in one calculation, the one an engineer does on the back of
an envelope before anything else: will the link close? A transmitter of power $P_t$ feeding an antenna of
gain $G_t$ produces, at distance $d$ in free space, a power flux density $P_tG_t/4\pi d^2$\,W/m$^2$---the
power spread over a sphere that grows as $d^2$, like paint thinning as a balloon inflates. A receiving
antenna of gain $G_r$ has an effective area $A_e=G_r\lambda^2/4\pi$, so it collects
\begin{equation}
P_r=P_tG_tG_r\left(\frac{\lambda}{4\pi d}\right)^2,
\qquad
L_{\text{fs}}=\left(\frac{4\pi d}{\lambda}\right)^2 .
\label{eq:ch03:friistx}
\end{equation}
This is the Friis transmission equation (Friis, 1946). The free-space path loss $L_{\text{fs}}$ appears to
grow with frequency, but that is an artefact of using antennas of fixed gain: an antenna of fixed
\emph{area} $A$ has gain $4\pi A/\lambda^2$, so between two dishes of fixed size the received power actually
\emph{rises} with frequency (Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_fspl}). This is why point-to-point microwave links and
satellites use high frequencies and large dishes, and why a small phone antenna, whose gain is fixed at
about 0\,dBi whatever the frequency, collects less power at millimetre wave. In decibels, the link budget
is a sum:
\begin{equation}
P_r\,[\mathrm{dBm}]=P_t+G_t+G_r-L_{\text{fs}}-L_{\text{other}},\qquad
L_{\text{fs}}\,[\mathrm{dB}]=32.45+20\log_{10}f_{\mathrm{MHz}}+20\log_{10}d_{\mathrm{km}}.
\label{eq:ch03:fspl}
\end{equation}

The resolution of the apparent paradox is worth spelling out, because it trips up experienced engineers.
Free space does not absorb more at high frequency; nothing in a vacuum absorbs at all. What changes is the
size of the antenna that a ``0\,dBi'' gain implies. An isotropic antenna's effective area is
$\lambda^2/4\pi$, so at ten times the frequency it catches a hundredth of the power from the same flux
density. Keep the \emph{physical} size of the antenna fixed instead and its gain grows as $1/\lambda^2$ at
both ends of the link, more than compensating. The practical lesson is to ask, whenever someone says that
millimetre waves ``don't go as far'', which antenna they are holding constant. Real millimetre-wave links do
suffer extra losses---oxygen absorption, rain, foliage, walls---but those belong in $L_{\text{other}}$, not in
the free-space term.

\subsection{From received power to \texorpdfstring{$C/N_0$}{C/N0}}

\mdcfig[0.52]{ch03_sat_budget}{The satellite downlink of the worked example as a waterfall in decibels:
EIRP, path loss and atmospheric loss, the receiver's $G/T$ and Boltzmann's constant add up to
$C/N_0\approx89$\,dB-Hz.}

Dividing the received power by the noise density $N_0=kT_{\text{sys}}$ gives the form used by satellite
engineers, with the transmitter, path and receiver separated:
\begin{equation}
\frac{C}{N_0}=\mathrm{EIRP}-L_{\text{fs}}-L_{\text{other}}+\frac{G_r}{T_{\text{sys}}}-k\quad\text{[dB-Hz]},
\qquad k=-228.6\ \mathrm{dBW/K/Hz},
\label{eq:ch03:cn0}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{EIRP}=P_tG_t$ is the effective isotropic radiated power (in dBW) and $G_r/T_{\text{sys}}$ is in
dB/K. Chapter~\ref{ch:satellite} develops this equation with atmospheric losses, rain and interference;
Chapter~\ref{ch:channels} replaces free space by the empirical path-loss models of terrestrial radio.

\begin{worked}[A satellite TV downlink]
A geostationary satellite transmits a 27.5\,MBd DVB-S2 carrier at 12\,GHz with an EIRP of 52\,dBW towards a
home 38\,000\,km away (the slant range is longer than the 35\,786\,km altitude for any receiver not directly
beneath the satellite). The wavelength is 2.5\,cm and the free-space loss is
$20\log_{10}(4\pi\times3.8\times10^7/0.025)=205.6$\,dB. A 60\,cm dish with 65\% efficiency has gain
$10\log_{10}[0.65(\pi\times0.6/0.025)^2]=35.7$\,dBi; with an LNB and a clear sky, $T_{\text{sys}}\approx120$\,K
(20.8\,dBK), so $G/T=14.9$\,dB/K. Allowing 0.5\,dB of atmospheric loss,
\[
C/N_0=52-205.6-0.5+14.9+228.6=89.4\ \text{dB-Hz},
\]
\[
E_s/N_0=89.4-10\log_{10}(27.5\times10^6)=15.0\ \text{dB}.
\]
DVB-S2 QPSK rate 3/4 needs an $E_s/N_0$ of about 4\,dB (ETSI EN 302 307), so this carrier could use a
higher-order mode---8PSK or 16APSK---and still keep several decibels of margin for rain, which at Ku-band both
attenuates the signal and, by~\eqref{eq:ch03:lossy}, raises $T_{\text{sys}}$.
\end{worked}

\subsection{Margins}

A real link budget adds losses for cables, polarisation mismatch, rain, walls, body blockage and, above all,
\emph{fading}. Multipath fading can drop the received power 20--30\,dB below its average for a small
fraction of the time (Section~\ref{sec:ch03:complex} and Chapter~\ref{ch:channels}), so the budget must
include a \emph{fade margin} chosen for the required availability. Interference adds to noise; when it is
noise-like, the budget uses the signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio (SINR) and an \emph{interference
margin} (the noise rise). The final comparison is between the received $E_b/N_0$ or SNR and the value the
modem needs, plus margin. Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_linkbudget} draws an indoor Wi-Fi budget as a waterfall:
20\,dBm from the access point, antenna gains of 3\,dBi, about 98\,dB of path loss at 50\,m with a path-loss
exponent of 3 at 5.5\,GHz (free space to 1\,m, then 30\,dB per decade), and an 8\,dB wall, leaves about
$-80$\,dBm---14\,dB above the noise floor of a 20\,MHz receiver with 7\,dB noise figure. That supports 16-QAM
with moderate coding (Chapter~\ref{ch:wifi}).

\mdcfig[0.82]{ch03_linkbudget}{A link budget drawn as a waterfall. Gains lift the level, losses lower it; the
gap between the received power and the noise floor is the SNR available to the modem.}

\begin{pitfall}[Margins are statistics, not safety factors]
Every margin in a link budget is a statement about a probability: a 10\,dB Rayleigh fade margin gives about
90\% availability at a point (Section~\ref{sec:ch03:complex}), an 8.7\,dB shadowing margin a stated coverage
probability (Chapter~\ref{ch:channels}), a rain margin an annual availability (Chapter~\ref{ch:satellite}).
Adding margins chosen independently for the worst case of each impairment over-designs the link; omitting the
statistics behind them makes the budget impossible to defend. Write down, for each margin, the event it
protects against and the probability it buys.
\end{pitfall}

\begin{history}[Talking to Voyager]
Voyager~1, launched in 1977, is now more than 160 times as far from the Sun as the Earth is. It transmits
roughly 20\,W at 8.4\,GHz from a 3.7\,m dish, and its signal is received by the 70\,m antennas of NASA's Deep
Space Network, whose cryogenic receivers have system noise temperatures of a few tens of kelvin. At more than
$2.4\times10^{10}$\,km, the free-space path loss exceeds 300\,dB, and the received power is of order
$10^{-19}$\,W---about a million times weaker than the weakest signal a phone can decode. The link still
carries 160\,b/s of science data, thanks to high antenna gains, very low noise temperatures, a narrow
bandwidth and powerful concatenated error-correcting codes---a convolutional code and a Reed--Solomon code,
the same pair of codes that protected digital television for a generation (Chapter~\ref{ch:classiccodes}).
Every term of Equation~\eqref{eq:ch03:friistx} and every kelvin of Equation~\eqref{eq:ch03:tsys} has been
pushed to its limit.
\end{history}

\begin{itemize}
\item Noise is described by probability. Moments have electrical meanings (mean = DC, variance = AC power);
characteristic functions turn sums into products; transformations and Bayes' rule underlie every detector
and soft-decision decoder. Uncorrelated is not independent, except for jointly Gaussian variables.
\item The Gaussian is everywhere because of the central limit theorem---the Galton board---and is the
worst-case noise because it maximises entropy. The CLT converges slowly in the tails, so few-term
impairments are not Gaussian where error rates live.
\item $\Q(x)$ is the Gaussian tail: how far the noise must reach to fool you. Remember
$\Q^{-1}(10^{-3})=3.09$, $\Q^{-1}(10^{-6})=4.75$, $\Q^{-1}(10^{-9})=6.0$; bound it with
$\tfrac12e^{-x^2/2}$ or $\phi(x)/x$; use Craig's form to average over fading.
\item Circular complex Gaussian noise has a uniform phase, a Rayleigh envelope and an exponential power; a
constant plus such noise has a Rice envelope; sums of $L$ powers are chi-square with $2L$ degrees of
freedom, the root of diversity.
\item A WSS process is described by its autocorrelation and PSD, a Fourier pair (Wiener--Khinchin). Filters
shape the PSD by $|H(f)|^2$; the noise-equivalent bandwidth is the right bandwidth for noise. White noise
sampled at $f_s$ has variance proportional to $f_s$.
\item Bandpass noise has independent, identically distributed I and Q components when its spectrum is
symmetric; watch the factors of two between passband, I/Q and complex-envelope PSDs.
\item Modulated signals are cyclostationary; their spectral correlation reveals symbol rate and carrier
offset even far below the noise, and underlies synchronisation and spectrum sensing.
\item Thermal noise is $kTB$, $-174$\,dBm/Hz at 290\,K, derived by Nyquist from thermodynamics and corrected
by Planck's law at high frequency and low temperature. Shot noise is $2qI$; flicker noise rises as $1/f$;
man-made and galactic noise dominate below about 1\,GHz; the sky is cold between 1 and 10\,GHz.
\item Noise figure and noise temperature describe added noise: $T_e=(F-1)T_0$; a passive loss $L$ at $T_0$
has $F=L$; cascades follow Friis, so the first stage and any loss ahead of it dominate. Measure with the
Y-factor method.
\item Sensitivity $=-174+10\log_{10}B+\mathrm{NF}+\mathrm{SNR}_{\text{req}}$. Keep $C/N_0$, $E_s/N_0$,
$E_b/N_0$ and SNR straight with the bandwidths and the code rate; in simulation, remember the samples per
symbol.
\item Detection is a likelihood-ratio test; Neyman--Pearson sets the threshold by the false-alarm rate; the
ROC summarises the trade-off; the deflection $\sqrt{2E/N_0}$ decides performance. Estimation is bounded by
the Cram\'er--Rao bound, with a threshold at low SNR.
\item The link budget combines EIRP, path loss, $G/T$ and Boltzmann's constant into $C/N_0$, and margins turn
it into a probability of success.
\end{itemize}

\problems
\begin{enumerate}[label=\thechapter.\arabic*]
\item Show that if $X$ and $Y$ are independent $\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$, then $R=\sqrt{X^2+Y^2}$ is Rayleigh
distributed and $R^2$ is exponential. What fraction of the time is a Rayleigh-fading signal more than 10\,dB
below its mean power? 20\,dB? What are the answers for the combined power of two independent Rayleigh
branches?
\item Let $\Theta$ be uniform on $[0,2\pi)$. Derive the pdf of $Y=A\cos\Theta$ using~\eqref{eq:ch03:transform}.
Show that $\cos\Theta$ and $\sin\Theta$ are uncorrelated but not independent.
\item A link has a bit-error rate of $10^{-5}$. Using the binomial distribution, find the probability that a
1500-byte packet contains (a) no errors, (b) more than two errors. When is the Poisson approximation adequate?
\item Derive the Chernoff bound $\Q(x)\le e^{-x^2/2}$ from~\eqref{eq:ch03:chernoff}, and the bound
$\Q(x)\le\tfrac12e^{-x^2/2}$ from Craig's formula~\eqref{eq:ch03:craig}. By how many decibels of SNR is each
pessimistic at $\Q=10^{-6}$?
\item Prove the Wiener--Khinchin theorem for a discrete-time WSS sequence, following the continuous-time
derivation in Section~\ref{sec:ch03:psd}. Then compute the PSD of the sequence $y_k=x_k+0.9\,y_{k-1}$ driven by
white noise of variance $\sigma^2$, and its noise-equivalent bandwidth relative to the sample rate.
\item Compute the thermal noise power in dBm for bandwidths of 12.5\,kHz (narrowband FM), 200\,kHz (GSM),
20\,MHz (Wi-Fi) and 400\,MHz (5G mmWave), all at 290\,K. What is the RMS open-circuit noise voltage of a
75\,$\Omega$ resistor in a 6\,MHz TV channel?
\item White noise of two-sided PSD $N_0/2$ passes through an ideal bandpass filter of width $B$ centred on
$f_c$. Show that $n_I$ and $n_Q$ each have power $N_0B$. Repeat for a filter whose passband is \emph{not}
symmetric about $f_c$ (it extends from $f_c-B/4$ to $f_c+3B/4$), and find the cross-correlation of $n_I$ and
$n_Q$.
\item A receiver has an antenna with $T_{\text{ant}}=50$\,K, a feed line with 0.5\,dB loss at 290\,K, an LNA
with NF 0.8\,dB and 30\,dB gain, and a second stage with NF 10\,dB. Compute the system noise temperature. How
much does moving the LNA before the feed line improve the SNR?
\item Derive the Friis formula~\eqref{eq:ch03:friis} for three stages from the definition of noise
temperature, and the noise temperature~\eqref{eq:ch03:lossy} of a matched attenuator from the
thermal-equilibrium argument.
\item A Y-factor measurement uses a noise source with ENR 5.2\,dB. The measured $Y$ is 3.1\,dB. Find the noise
figure. If the power measurement has an uncertainty of $\pm0.05$\,dB, what is the uncertainty in the noise
figure? Would a 15\,dB ENR source be better or worse for this DUT?
\item A 14-bit ADC samples at 122.88\,MS/s with a full-scale input of $+2$\,dBm and a measured SNR of 70\,dB.
Compute its effective noise figure. The LTE receiver in front of it has a cascade noise figure of 3\,dB. How
much gain must precede the ADC so that it degrades the total noise figure by no more than 0.2\,dB?
\item A geostationary satellite 36\,000\,km away transmits 50\,dBW EIRP at 12\,GHz. A 60\,cm dish with 65\%
efficiency and a 60\,K system noise temperature receives it. Compute $C/N_0$. What bit rate can a DVB-S2 QPSK
rate-3/4 carrier (needing about 4\,dB $E_s/N_0$) support?
\item A Bluetooth Low Energy link transmits 0\,dBm at 2.4\,GHz with 0\,dBi antennas. The receiver sensitivity
is $-95$\,dBm. What is the free-space range? Repeat with a path-loss exponent of 3 beyond 1\,m.
\item A radar must detect a target with $P_D=0.9$ at $P_{FA}=10^{-8}$ using a single pulse of unknown phase.
Find the required $E/N_0$ with~\eqref{eq:ch03:marcum} (numerically) and compare with the coherent
result~\eqref{eq:ch03:pd}. How many false alarms per second occur if $5\times10^6$ cells are tested per second?
\item \emph{(Simulation)} Generate complex Gaussian noise in Python and verify numerically that (a) its phase
is uniform, (b) its magnitude is Rayleigh, and (c) after a filter $H(f)$ its PSD is $|H(f)|^2N_0$. Then measure
how many samples are needed to estimate a $10^{-6}$ tail probability to within 10\%, and explain the answer
with the binomial distribution.
\item \emph{(Simulation)} Simulate BPSK with RRC pulses at 8 samples per symbol and add noise
with~\eqref{eq:ch03:simnoise}. Verify that the measured BER after the matched filter matches
$\Q(\sqrt{2E_b/N_0})$. Then deliberately omit the factor $N_{\text{sps}}$ and identify the resulting error in
decibels.
\item \emph{(Simulation)} Implement the frequency estimator of Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_crb} for $N=32$, 64 and
128. Locate the threshold SNR in each case and verify that above threshold the MSE follows the $N^{-3}$ law
of~\eqref{eq:ch03:crbfreq}.
\end{enumerate}

\furtherreading
\begin{itemize}
\item A.~Papoulis and S.~U.~Pillai, \emph{Probability, Random Variables and Stochastic Processes}, 4th ed.,
McGraw-Hill, 2002. The classic engineering reference for everything in
Sections~\ref{sec:ch03:prob}--\ref{sec:ch03:bandpass}, including cyclostationary processes.
\item A.~Leon-Garcia, \emph{Probability, Statistics, and Random Processes for Electrical Engineering}, 3rd ed.,
Pearson, 2008. A gentler, example-rich introduction, well suited to filling gaps in the refresher of
Section~\ref{sec:ch03:prob}.
\item S.~O.~Rice, ``Mathematical analysis of random noise,'' \emph{Bell System Technical Journal}, vol.~23,
pp.~282--332, 1944, and vol.~24, pp.~46--156, 1945. The classic: shot noise, the statistics of narrowband
noise and of a sinusoid plus noise, still worth reading.
\item W.~A.~Gardner, ``Exploitation of spectral redundancy in cyclostationary signals,'' \emph{IEEE Signal
Processing Magazine}, vol.~8, no.~2, pp.~14--36, 1991. The accessible tutorial on spectral correlation by the
researcher who did most to develop it; his book \emph{Statistical Spectral Analysis: A Nonprobabilistic Theory}
(Prentice Hall, 1988) gives the full theory.
\item J.~B.~Johnson, ``Thermal agitation of electricity in conductors,'' and H.~Nyquist, ``Thermal agitation of
electric charge in conductors,'' \emph{Physical Review}, vol.~32, pp.~97--109 and 110--113, 1928. The two
founding papers of noise theory, short enough to read in an evening.
\item H.~T.~Friis, ``Noise figures of radio receivers,'' \emph{Proceedings of the IRE}, vol.~32, pp.~419--422,
1944; and ``A note on a simple transmission formula,'' \emph{Proceedings of the IRE}, vol.~34, pp.~254--256,
1946. The noise cascade and the transmission equation, in their original form.
\item D.~M.~Pozar, \emph{Microwave Engineering}, 4th ed., Wiley, 2012. Noise in microwave circuits, noise figure,
noise circles and LNA design, with clear derivations of the results of Section~\ref{sec:ch03:nf}.
\item Keysight Technologies (formerly Agilent), Application Notes 57-1, \emph{Fundamentals of RF and Microwave
Noise Figure Measurements}, and 57-2, \emph{Noise Figure Measurement Accuracy: The Y-Factor Method}. Free and
practical; the source of most bench practice in Section~\ref{sec:ch03:yfactor}.
\item Recommendation ITU-R P.372, \emph{Radio noise} (current edition). Measured and modelled external noise
levels---atmospheric, galactic, man-made, gaseous and the Sun---from 0.1\,Hz to 100\,GHz, with the formulas
behind Figure~\ref{fig:ch03_skynoise}.
\item S.~M.~Kay, \emph{Fundamentals of Statistical Signal Processing}, vol.~I, \emph{Estimation Theory} (1993),
and vol.~II, \emph{Detection Theory} (1998), Prentice Hall. The clearest modern treatment of ML, MMSE, the
Cram\'er--Rao bound, Neyman--Pearson detection and ROCs, with communications and radar examples throughout.
\item H.~L.~Van Trees, \emph{Detection, Estimation, and Modulation Theory, Part I}, Wiley, 1968 (reissued
2001). The monumental original synthesis of detection and estimation for engineers.
\item J.~H.~Yuen (ed.), \emph{Deep Space Telecommunications Systems Engineering}, JPL, 1982 (free online), and
the JPL DESCANSO monograph series. The engineering of links like Voyager's, including cryogenic receivers and
system temperature budgets.
\end{itemize}
