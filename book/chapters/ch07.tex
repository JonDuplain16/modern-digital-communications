\chapter{The Radio Transceiver and the Software-Defined Radio}
\label{ch:sdr}

\begin{chapterintro}
The mathematics of this book assumes ideal mixers, perfect oscillators and linear amplifiers.
Real radios have none of these, and the engineer who understands their imperfections designs
systems that work on the first try. This chapter walks through a modern transceiver from the
antenna to the samples: receiver architectures, the sensitivity--linearity trade-off and its
figures of merit, oscillators and phase noise, power amplifiers and the linearisation techniques
that make OFDM affordable, and the digital correction of analog impairments. It ends with the
software-defined radio---its history, its architecture as embodied in the USRP B200, and the
practical art of streaming samples between a radio and a computer.
\end{chapterintro}

\section{Anatomy of a transceiver}

Every radio, from a Bluetooth earbud to a 64-antenna base station, has the same skeleton. On the
receive side:
\begin{enumerate}
\item An antenna and a \emph{band filter} (a SAW, BAW or cavity filter) that passes only the band
of interest and protects the receiver from strong out-of-band signals.
\item A \term{low-noise amplifier} (LNA) that sets the noise figure of the whole chain
(Chapter~\ref{ch:noise}).
\item One or more \emph{mixers} driven by a \emph{local oscillator} (LO) that translate the signal to
an intermediate frequency or directly to baseband.
\item Analog filtering and gain (the \emph{baseband} or \emph{IF} section) with automatic gain control.
\item An ADC, followed by digital down-conversion, decimation and channel filtering
(Chapter~\ref{ch:dsp}).
\end{enumerate}
The transmit side mirrors it: DAC, reconstruction filter, I/Q up-converter, driver, power
amplifier (PA), harmonic filter, and antenna, with a switch or duplexer sharing the antenna with the
receiver. Each block contributes noise, distortion, spurious signals and frequency errors, and the
radio designer's job is to allocate a budget for each.

\section{Receiver architectures}

\subsection{Three families}

Figure~\ref{fig:ch07_architectures} sketches the three architectures in use today. The
\term{superheterodyne} of Chapter~\ref{ch:analog} converts to a fixed IF where high-quality filters
select the channel. It remains the choice where selectivity and dynamic range are paramount:
spectrum analysers, military receivers, many base stations, and satellite ground terminals.

\mdcfig{ch07_architectures}{Receiver architectures in the frequency domain. Top: the superheterodyne
must reject the image before mixing. Middle: the zero-IF receiver's image is the signal's own
mirror, rejected only by I/Q balance, and its baseband sits on the DC offset. Bottom: a direct
RF-sampling receiver digitises the whole band and performs the mixing digitally.}

The \term{direct-conversion} or \term{zero-IF} receiver sets the LO at the carrier and produces I and
Q baseband outputs directly. It needs no image filter and no IF filter---channel selection is a
low-pass filter that can be integrated on chip---so it dominates mobile phones and integrated
transceivers like the AD9364 in the B200. Its weaknesses are the ones Chapter~1 of the companion labs
lets you see: \emph{DC offset} from LO leakage that mixes with itself, \emph{flicker (1/f) noise}
in the baseband amplifiers, \emph{second-order intermodulation} that converts strong amplitude-varying
blockers directly to baseband, and \emph{I/Q imbalance} that creates a mirror image of the signal onto
itself. A \emph{low-IF} receiver places the signal a few hundred kilohertz or a megahertz from DC,
avoiding the DC and flicker problems at the cost of needing better image rejection; it is common in
Bluetooth, GNSS and FM radio chips.

\term{Direct RF sampling} puts the ADC at the antenna, or nearly so. Converters sampling at 3 to 10\,GS/s
with 12 to 14 bits, integrated with FPGA logic in devices such as AMD's RFSoC, can digitise
everything up to several gigahertz. The mixers, IF filters and channel filters all become DSP. This is
the logical end of Mitola's software radio vision, limited today by power consumption and by the
dynamic range needed when strong and weak signals share the band.

\subsection{The sensitivity--linearity trade-off}

A receiver must hear signals near the thermal floor while tolerating others 80 or 100\,dB stronger
on nearby frequencies. Gain early in the chain improves noise figure (Friis) but pushes later stages
toward overload. The two figures of merit are noise figure, for the weak signal, and linearity, for
the strong ones.

Linearity is usually characterised by expanding the amplifier's transfer function as a power series,
$y=a_1x+a_2x^2+a_3x^3+\cdots$. Driving it with two tones at $f_1$ and $f_2$ produces, among many
products, \term{third-order intermodulation} (IM3) at $2f_1-f_2$ and $2f_2-f_1$. These are the
dangerous ones because they fall close to the tones---in the band, where no filter can remove them
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch07_two_tone}). As the input rises by 1\,dB the IM3 products rise by 3\,dB; the
extrapolated input level at which they would equal the fundamental is the \term{third-order intercept
point} (IIP3). A second-order intercept (IIP2) describes the $a_2$ products that trouble zero-IF
receivers. The 1\,dB \term{compression point} (P1dB), where gain has fallen by 1\,dB, sits roughly
10\,dB below IIP3 for a simple amplifier.

\mdcfig{ch07_two_tone}{Left: a two-tone test through a cubic nonlinearity produces
third-order products at $2f_1-f_2$ and $2f_2-f_1$. Right: fundamental and IM3 output against input
power; their extrapolated intersection is the third-order intercept.}

The \term{spurious-free dynamic range} combines both ends: the range of input powers over which a signal
is above the noise floor while two equal interferers' IM3 products are still below it,
\begin{equation}
\text{SFDR}=\tfrac23\big(\text{IIP3}-P_{\text{noise}}\big)\ \text{dB},
\qquad P_{\text{noise}}=-174+10\log_{10}B+\text{NF}\ \text{dBm}.
\label{eq:ch07:sfdr}
\end{equation}
A receiver with NF 6\,dB, IIP3 $-10$\,dBm and a 200\,kHz channel has a noise floor of $-115$\,dBm and
an SFDR of 70\,dB. In cascades, IIP3 degrades with gain before a stage:
$1/\text{IIP3}_{\text{tot}}\approx\sum_k G_{k-1}/\text{IIP3}_k$ (linear units, where $G_{k-1}$ is the gain
ahead of stage $k$). Noise wants gain early; linearity wants it late. Every receiver line-up is a
negotiation between the two.

\subsection{Blocking and desensitisation}

A strong \emph{blocker} near the wanted channel does harm in three ways even if no intermodulation
product lands on the signal: it compresses the LNA or mixer, reducing gain for the weak signal; its
presence forces the AGC to reduce gain, raising the effective noise figure; and it mixes with the LO's
phase noise to spread noise onto the wanted channel (reciprocal mixing, below). Cellular standards
therefore specify blocking tests in which the receiver must maintain its sensitivity with a
specified interferer present, and those tests, rather than the plain sensitivity test, usually set
the design.

\section{Oscillators and frequency synthesis}

\subsection{References}

Every frequency in a radio derives from a reference oscillator, almost always a quartz crystal.
A plain crystal oscillator (XO) is accurate to about $\pm20$\,ppm over temperature; a
temperature-compensated crystal oscillator (TCXO), like the B200's, to about $\pm2$\,ppm; an
oven-controlled oscillator (OCXO) to 10\,ppb; and a GPS-disciplined oscillator (GPSDO) to parts in
$10^{12}$ over a day. A frequency error of $\epsilon$ at carrier $f_c$ is a carrier frequency offset of
$\epsilon f_c$: 2\,ppm at 3.5\,GHz is 7\,kHz, a quarter of a 30\,kHz NR subcarrier. Cellular base
stations hold $\pm50$\,ppb, and phones lock their oscillators to the base station, which is why
Chapter~\ref{ch:sync} matters so much.

\subsection{PLL synthesisers}

A \term{phase-locked loop} (PLL) synthesiser multiplies the reference to the desired LO frequency.
A voltage-controlled oscillator's output is divided by $N$ and compared in phase with the reference;
the loop filter steers the VCO until the divided output tracks the reference, so
$f_{\text{VCO}}=Nf_{\text{ref}}$. An \emph{integer-N} synthesiser can only step in multiples of
$f_{\text{ref}}$; a \emph{fractional-N} synthesiser alternates the divide ratio with a sigma-delta
modulator (Chapter~\ref{ch:pcm}) to achieve fine resolution with a high reference frequency. Inside
the loop bandwidth, the output phase noise follows the reference multiplied by $20\log_{10}N$\,dB;
outside it, the VCO's own noise dominates. The AD9364 contains two such fractional-N synthesisers,
one for receive and one for transmit, both locked to the B200's 40\,MHz TCXO.

\subsection{Phase noise}

A real oscillator's phase wanders randomly. Its spectrum, instead of a line, has skirts described by
the \term{single-sideband phase noise} $\mathcal{L}(f)$ in dBc/Hz at offset $f$ from the carrier
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch07_phase_noise}). Leeson's model gives the characteristic shape: $1/f^3$ close in
(flicker noise upconverted), $1/f^2$ in the middle (white noise integrated by the resonator), and a
flat floor far out. Integrating $\mathcal{L}(f)$ over offsets gives the RMS phase error,
\begin{equation}
\sigma_\phi^2=2\int_{f_1}^{f_2}10^{\mathcal{L}(f)/10}\,df\quad(\text{rad}^2).
\end{equation}
Phase noise hurts in three ways.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Constellation rotation.} Residual phase jitter after carrier tracking rotates dense
constellations: 64-QAM tolerates about $1.5^\circ$ RMS, 1024-QAM less than $0.5^\circ$.
\item \textbf{OFDM impairment.} Slow phase noise produces a common phase error on all subcarriers,
which pilots track; fast phase noise produces inter-carrier interference, which they cannot. This
is why 5G NR at millimetre-wave uses wider subcarriers and adds phase-tracking reference signals
(Chapter~\ref{ch:ofdm}).
\item \textbf{Reciprocal mixing.} A strong blocker at offset $\Delta f$ mixes with the LO's phase noise
at that offset and lands on the wanted channel with power $P_{\text{blocker}}+\mathcal{L}(\Delta f)+10\log_{10}B$.
A $-30$\,dBm blocker 1\,MHz away, an LO at $-130$\,dBc/Hz and a 1\,MHz channel give $-100$\,dBm of
noise---enough to swamp a weak signal.
\end{itemize}

\mdcfig{ch07_phase_noise}{Left: typical oscillator phase-noise profile with its $1/f^3$, $1/f^2$ and
floor regions. Centre and right: 64-QAM with $0.5^\circ$ and $3^\circ$ RMS residual phase jitter;
the outer points smear along arcs.}

\section{The transmitter}

\subsection{The I/Q modulator}

A modern transmitter synthesises the complex envelope digitally, converts I and Q with two DACs and
up-converts them with a quadrature modulator:
$s(t)=I(t)\cos2\pi f_ct-Q(t)\sin2\pi f_ct$. Its impairments mirror the receiver's: \emph{LO leakage}
appears as a carrier spike at the centre of the signal, and I/Q gain and phase mismatch produce a
mirror image with the suppression given by Figure~\ref{fig:ch04_ssb_phase}. Both are measured and
corrected digitally, and both are part of the transmit EVM budget that standards specify.

\subsection{Power amplifiers}

The PA is the most power-hungry component of most radios and the most nonlinear. Its classes trade
linearity for efficiency. \emph{Class A} conducts all the time and is linear but at best 50\%
efficient; \emph{class AB} and \emph{B} conduct for about half the cycle and reach 60 to 78\% at peak
power; \emph{class C, D, E} and \emph{F} operate as switches or with harmonic shaping and exceed 80\%,
but only for constant-envelope signals such as FM, GMSK and FSK. That is why 2G GSM, Bluetooth and
most IoT links use constant-envelope modulations: they let a battery-powered device use a
saturated, efficient PA.

Linear modulations such as QAM and OFDM have a large peak-to-average power ratio (PAPR), about 10\,dB
for OFDM at $10^{-3}$ probability. A PA driven into saturation clips the peaks, and the clipping
distortion spreads into adjacent channels as \term{spectral regrowth} (Figure~\ref{fig:ch07_pa_regrowth}).
Standards limit it through the \term{adjacent channel leakage ratio} (ACLR), typically 45\,dB for
LTE and NR terminals, and limit in-band distortion through EVM. The simplest fix is \term{back-off}:
operating the PA well below saturation. But efficiency falls rapidly with back-off; a class-AB PA
backed off by 8 to 10\,dB may be only 10 to 15\% efficient.

\mdcfig{ch07_pa_regrowth}{Left: AM/AM curve of a solid-state PA (Rapp model). Right: spectrum of an
OFDM signal after the PA at 12, 6 and 3\,dB of back-off. Each 3\,dB less back-off raises the
out-of-band emission substantially.}

\subsection{Efficiency and linearisation}

Four techniques recover efficiency without giving up linearity:
\begin{description}
\item[Crest-factor reduction (CFR)] clips and filters the digital signal to reduce PAPR by 2 to 4\,dB
before the PA, trading a small, controlled EVM for a large efficiency gain.
\item[Digital predistortion (DPD)] applies the inverse of the PA's nonlinearity in the digital domain,
so that the cascade is linear. A small observation receiver samples the PA output; an adaptive
algorithm (usually the \emph{indirect learning architecture} with a \emph{memory polynomial} or
generalised memory polynomial model) learns the inverse. Figure~\ref{fig:ch07_dpd} shows a 20\,dB
reduction in spectral regrowth at 10\,dB of back-off. Every macro base station and most small cells
use DPD.
\item[Doherty amplifiers] combine a carrier amplifier biased in class AB with a peaking amplifier
that turns on only for signal peaks. Load modulation keeps efficiency high over about 6\,dB of
back-off. Doherty PAs with DPD are the standard in cellular base stations.
\item[Envelope tracking (ET)] varies the PA's supply voltage to follow the signal envelope, so the
transistor is always near saturation. It is used in nearly every 4G and 5G smartphone.
\end{description}

\mdcfig[0.72]{ch07_dpd}{Digital predistortion inverts the PA's AM/AM curve. At 10\,dB back-off the
out-of-band emission drops by about 20\,dB, approaching the ideal linear amplifier.}

\section{Correcting analog impairments digitally}

The philosophy of the modern transceiver is: build analog circuits that are good enough, then
measure and correct the rest in DSP. The standard digital corrections are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{DC offset}: a high-pass filter or an estimator that subtracts the running mean, often in
hardware (the B200's FPGA does this).
\item \textbf{I/Q imbalance}: estimate the gain and phase mismatch from the second-order statistics of
the received signal (for a proper complex signal, $E[I^2]=E[Q^2]$ and $E[IQ]=0$), then apply a
$2\times2$ correction. Frequency-dependent imbalance requires a short FIR per branch.
\item \textbf{Carrier and sampling frequency offset}: estimated and corrected by the synchronisation
algorithms of Chapter~\ref{ch:sync}.
\item \textbf{Transmit LO leakage and image}: calibrated with a loopback path at start-up and tracked in
the background.
\item \textbf{PA nonlinearity}: DPD, as above.
\end{itemize}

\section{The software-defined radio}

\subsection{From SPEAKeasy to the USRP}

The term \emph{software radio} was coined by Joseph Mitola III in 1991 and popularised in his 1995
paper ``The software radio architecture''. The US military's SPEAKeasy programme (1991--1995)
demonstrated a radio that could emulate more than a dozen military waveforms by loading different
software, motivated by the fact that US forces in the 1983 Grenada invasion could not talk to each
other across services. The Joint Tactical Radio System and the Software Communications
Architecture followed. In civil communications, the key enabler was the combination of fast ADCs,
FPGAs and general-purpose processors. Eric Blossom started GNU Radio in 2001; Matt Ettus built the
first Universal Software Radio Peripheral (USRP) in 2004 to give GNU Radio affordable hardware. The
RTL-SDR discovery of 2012---that a \$20 DVB-T dongle could stream raw samples---put an SDR receiver in
every student's hands. Today, software-defined base stations run on commodity servers
(OpenAirInterface, srsRAN) and the Open RAN movement is built on the same idea.

\subsection{The USRP B200 as a case study}

The B200 is a compact example of the architecture:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RF}: an Analog Devices AD9364 RFIC covering 70\,MHz to 6\,GHz, with one transmit and one
receive chain, zero-IF architecture, integrated LNAs, mixers, synthesisers, 12-bit ADCs and DACs
running up to 61.44\,MS/s, half-band and programmable FIR decimators, and hardware DC-offset and
quadrature correction. Instantaneous bandwidth is up to 56\,MHz.
\item \textbf{FPGA}: a Xilinx Spartan-6 performs further DSP (CIC and half-band decimation or
interpolation, DC-offset correction, timestamping) and packs samples for the host.
\item \textbf{Host interface}: USB 3.0, carrying 16-bit I/Q samples (or 8-bit for higher rates).
\item \textbf{Clocking}: a 40\,MHz TCXO ($\pm2$\,ppm) with inputs for an external 10\,MHz reference and
a pulse-per-second (PPS) signal; a GPSDO can be fitted internally.
\end{itemize}
The \emph{master clock rate} (MCR) sets the AD9364's converter rate; the requested host sample rate
is MCR divided by an integer decimation in the FPGA. Choosing an MCR that makes the decimation even,
or a power of two, gives the best filter performance, and choosing an MCR equal to a multiple of the
signal's natural rate (for example 30.72\,MHz for LTE and NR) avoids resampling altogether.

\subsection{Streaming, latency and time}

The link between radio and host is a stream of packets. If the host does not consume samples fast
enough, the radio's buffer \emph{overflows} and samples are lost (UHD prints \texttt{O}); if it does
not supply transmit samples fast enough, the DAC \emph{underflows} (\texttt{U}). A USB 3.0 link and a
modern CPU sustain a few tens of MS/s; beyond that, processing must move into the FPGA. Every packet
carries a timestamp from the radio's sample clock, which allows timed transmissions (transmit this
burst at time $t$), coherent operation of several radios sharing a reference and PPS, and
measurement of exact delays. Latency through the USB stack and operating system is typically a
few milliseconds---fine for most experiments, but far too much for protocols such as Wi-Fi's
16\,$\mu$s acknowledgement timing, which is why real-time MAC layers run in the FPGA.

\subsection{GNU Radio}

GNU Radio models a radio as a \emph{flowgraph} of blocks connected by streams of items, each block
running in its own thread with ring buffers between them. Blocks can also exchange asynchronous
\emph{messages} (for packets and control) and attach \emph{tags} to stream items (for burst lengths,
timestamps and frequency changes). GNU Radio Companion (GRC) draws the flowgraph and generates Python.
The hardware scripts in this book's companion code are written directly in Python in the same style,
so that every block and parameter is visible. GNU Radio 4.0, at release-candidate stage in 2026,
rebuilds the runtime around a new scheduler and C++ block model, but the flowgraph concept is
unchanged.

\begin{pitfall}[title=Safe loopback testing]
The B200 can transmit more than $+10$\,dBm. Its receiver is damaged by roughly $-15$\,dBm at the
input. Never connect TX to RX without at least 30\,dB of attenuation (60\,dB is safer), and start with
the lowest TX gain. Over the air, transmit only in bands you are licensed for or in ISM bands at low
power.
\end{pitfall}
