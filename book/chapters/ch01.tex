\begin{chapterintro}
Communication engineering is a young discipline built on very old problems. This chapter follows
one of them---how do you get a message to someone far away, quickly and correctly?---from hilltop
fires to 5G, and at every step it pulls out the engineering idea that is still alive inside today's
equipment. You will meet the codebook and the error-control protocol of the optical telegraph, the
variable-length code that compresses text, the time-division multiplexer and its synchronised
distributors, the cable that smears pulses and the inductance that straightens them, the carbon
microphone that was the first amplifier, the vacuum-tube repeater and the negative-feedback
amplifier that made long distance possible, the carriers that let thousands of conversations share
one coaxial tube, the superheterodyne receiver inside every radio, the wartime microwave engineering
that became satellite and radio relay, and finally the theorems of Nyquist, Hartley and Shannon that
draw the boundary of the possible.

Along the way there are worked examples with real numbers from real systems---the efficiency of
Morse code, the speed of the 1866 Atlantic cable, the gain budget of the 1915 transcontinental
line, the capacity of a telephone channel---and pointers to the chapters of this book that develop
each technology properly. Read it as a story, and keep it as a map of everything that follows.
\end{chapterintro}

\section{Why an engineer should know the history}
\label{sec:ch01:why}
%======================================================================

Pick up any piece of communication equipment and you are holding a museum. The 3.4\,kHz bandwidth
of a telephone channel, the 8\,kHz sampling rate of digital voice, the 1.544\,Mb/s of a T1 line, the
455\,kHz and 10.7\,MHz intermediate frequencies of AM and FM radios, the 29.97 frames per second of
American television, the 15\,kHz subcarrier spacing of LTE and 5G, the 30\,kHz channels of the first
cellular systems, the $-48$\,V on a telephone line: every one of these numbers is a fossil
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_fossils}). Each was
a decision made by an engineer under a particular constraint, and each propagated forward because
new systems had to live alongside old ones.

\begin{figure}[h!]\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/ch01_fossils}
\caption{Museum labels for numbers you will meet in this book: each is a fossil of an engineering
decision made decades ago.}\label{fig:ch01_fossils}\end{figure}

It is perfectly possible to design a modem without knowing who Harry Nyquist was, just as it is
possible to write software without knowing why a byte has eight bits. But when you understand
\emph{why} a number was chosen, you also know when you may safely change it. That is the first
reason to read this chapter.

The second reason is humility. The transatlantic cable of 1858 failed because its engineers did not
understand the physics of a long line, and its chief electrician tried to fix a dispersion problem
with more voltage. The early theorists of frequency modulation proved that it had no advantage; they
were right about the question they asked and wrong about the one that mattered. Code-division
multiple access was declared impractical by experts shortly before it became the basis of
third-generation cellular. Low-density parity-check codes were invented in 1960, forgotten for
thirty-five years, and are now in every 5G phone. Engineering is a conversation across generations,
and the earlier voices are worth hearing.

\mdcfig[0.98]{ch01_adversaries}{The four adversaries. A Napoleonic semaphore operator, a Victorian
cable engineer, a 1920s radio designer and a 5G modem architect all fought the same enemies: a
limited set of distinguishable symbols, noise, dispersion that smears one symbol into the next, and
the need to share an expensive medium among many users. Watch for them in every section of this
chapter---and of this book.}

The third reason is the best one: history shows the \emph{unity} of the subject. Look at
Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_adversaries}. A semaphore operator squinting through a telescope, a cable
engineer staring at a flickering spot of light, a radio designer fighting static and a 5G modem
architect all faced the same four adversaries. Their solutions differ in technology, but they rhyme
in principle, and once you see the rhymes the whole field becomes one story.
Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_timeline} lays out the two and a half centuries covered in this chapter on
seven parallel tracks; Table~\ref{tab:ch01:timeline} at the end of the chapter gives a more detailed
chronology. Keep an eye on how the tracks feed each other---the telephone's long-distance problem
built the amplifier that radio needed, and radar's microwave engineering built the satellite link.

\mdcfig[0.98]{ch01_timeline}{Two and a half centuries of telecommunication on seven parallel
tracks. Note how the tracks feed each other: the telephone's long-distance problem produced the
vacuum-tube amplifier that radio needed; radar's microwave engineering produced radio relay and
satellite links; the telephone network's need for reliable digital transmission produced
Shannon's theory and the transistor.}

\label{sec:ch01:preelectric}

\subsection{Fires, torches, drums and flags}

Aeschylus's play \emph{Agamemnon} (458\,BC) opens with a bored watchman on a palace roof. He has
been waiting a year for a fire. When it finally blazes on the horizon he knows, instantly, that Troy
has fallen hundreds of kilometres away across the Aegean: a chain of beacon fires has carried the
news to Argos hilltop by hilltop. The idea was used for real for the next two thousand years. The
Roman army used signal towers; the beacon towers of the Great Wall of China could relay a warning
hundreds of kilometres in hours; and in 1588 a chain of beacons on English hilltops announced the
sighting of the Spanish Armada.

\mdcfig[0.98]{ch01_beacons}{A beacon chain, as in Aeschylus's \emph{Agamemnon}. The message crosses
the whole chain at the speed of light plus a few reaction times---but the only thing it can say is
the one sentence agreed in advance. Enormous speed, almost no capacity.}

These systems were astonishingly fast. The message moved at the speed of light between towers,
delayed only by the reaction time of the watchers. But they had almost no \emph{capacity}. A fire is
either lit or not (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_beacons}), so the message had to be agreed in advance: ``the
enemy is coming.'' In modern language, the alphabet had two symbols, the system sent one bit per
event, and all the meaning lived in a codebook with exactly one entry. You could not use a beacon
chain to ask for more arrows.

The Greek historian Polybius, writing in the second century BC, saw the limitation and proposed a
cure. Arrange the alphabet in a $5\times5$ grid. Signal a letter by raising a number of torches on
the left to indicate its row and a number on the right to indicate its column
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_chappe}, right). This is a genuinely modern idea: an \emph{alphabetic} code that
can spell \emph{any} message, using a two-dimensional index into a table. Each letter needs two small
integers from 1 to 5, so the system distinguishes $25$ symbols with $5+5$ torch positions rather than
25 separate signals.

Hold on to that trick, because you will meet it again in Chapter~\ref{ch:modulation}. The 16-QAM
constellation in your Wi-Fi router is a $4\times4$ grid indexed by an in-phase level and a quadrature
level---Polybius with radio waves instead of torches.

Sound carried messages too. The talking drums of West and Central Africa reproduced the tonal
contours of spoken language. Because a drum loses the consonants and vowels, many words sound alike;
drummers resolved the ambiguity by adding redundancy, replacing single words with long stock phrases.
James Gleick's account of them (see Further Reading) is a beautiful illustration of
\emph{error-correcting redundancy} centuries before Hamming.

At sea, flag signalling grew into elaborate codebooks. Home Popham's telegraphic code, used by the
Royal Navy in the Napoleonic wars, assigned numbers to some three thousand common words. At
Trafalgar in 1805 Nelson's signal ``England expects that every man will do his duty'' was hoisted as
codebook numbers---except for the word ``duty'', which was not in the vocabulary and had to be spelled
out letter by letter. A frequent word gets a short code; a rare word is expensive. We will meet that
principle again in Morse code and in Huffman coding.

\subsection{Chappe's optical telegraph}
\label{sec:ch01:chappe}

Imagine France in 1794: at war on several fronts, its government in Paris, its armies on the
frontiers, and the fastest messenger a man on a horse. Into this crisis stepped Claude Chappe and his
brothers with the first system that could send \emph{arbitrary} messages quickly over long
distances: the \term{optical telegraph}. Beginning with the Paris--Lille line in 1794, the
revolutionary government built a network of towers, each topped by an articulated mechanism whose
configuration could be read by telescope from the next tower, typically 10 to 15\,km away
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_chappe_tower}).

\chOnePair{ch01_chappe_tower}{A restored Chappe telegraph tower near Saverne in Alsace, on the
Paris--Strasbourg line. The signalling mast with its regulator and indicators stands on the roof
above the operators' room.}{ch01_chappe_map}{The main Chappe lines of the 1840s radiating from Paris
(schematic). Each red dot stands for a tower, roughly one every 12\,km.}

Chappe had first called his machine the \emph{tachygraphe}, ``fast writer''. The name
\emph{t\'el\'egraphe}, ``far writer'', suggested by the diplomat Andr\'e-Fran\c{c}ois Miot de
M\'elito, was adopted in 1792 because distance, not speed, was the point. According to the standard
accounts, the first important message on the Lille line, in August 1794, announced the recapture of
the town of Le Quesnoy from the Austrians. It reached Paris in about an hour, when a mounted courier
would have needed a day or more. The age of telecommunication had begun.

The mechanism (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_chappe}, left) consisted of a long beam, the \emph{regulator},
pivoted at the top of a mast, with a shorter arm, the \emph{indicator}, at each end. The regulator
could be horizontal or vertical (oblique positions were used for control signals), and each
indicator could take seven distinguishable angles in steps of $45^\circ$---the eighth would hide it
behind the regulator. With two regulator positions and $7\times7$ indicator positions the device had
\begin{equation}
2\times7\times7 = 98
\end{equation}
legal configurations: a 98-letter alphabet, designed so that every letter could be told apart
through a telescope in haze by a tired operator.

\mdcfig[0.98]{ch01_chappe}{Left: eight examples of Chappe semaphore signs, each a configuration
of the regulator (navy) and its two indicators (red); the full repertoire had 98 configurations,
of which 92 indexed the pages and lines of a codebook. Right: the much older Polybius square,
which signalled a letter as a (row, column) pair of torch counts---the first known
two-dimensional signal alphabet.}

The Chappes reserved a few configurations for control---start, stop, error, ``repeat'',
``wait''---and used the remaining 92 to index a codebook. From around 1800 the code used pairs of
signs: the first selected one of 92 pages of a vocabulary and the second one of 92 lines on that
page, giving $92^2=8464$ words, phrases and place names. Supplementary books extended the vocabulary
further. In the language of Chapter~\ref{ch:sourcecoding}, the Chappe vocabulary was a
\term{dictionary code}: common messages were mapped to short symbol sequences, and the dictionary was
shared knowledge at the two ends.

Three further features make the Chappe telegraph feel strikingly modern.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A protocol with error control.} Each station repeated the sign it saw so that
the previous station could check it; there were control signals for errors and for
retransmission; and the line had a priority scheme for official traffic. This is a link-layer
protocol with acknowledgement and automatic repeat request, implemented by people with
telescopes.
\item \textbf{End-to-end encryption by accident of design.} The intermediate operators knew
only the signs, not the vocabulary; only the directors at the terminal stations held the
codebooks. Intermediate nodes relayed symbols they could not read, exactly as an Internet
router forwards encrypted packets.
\item \textbf{Pipelining.} A station could start relaying the first sign of a message while the
originating station was forming the second. The time for a message to cross the network
(\emph{latency}) therefore depended on the number of hops, while the rate at which signs could be
pushed through (\emph{throughput}) depended only on how fast the slowest station could form and
read a sign.
\end{itemize}

\begin{analogy}[A bucket brigade]
Picture a line of people passing buckets of water from a well to a fire. The first bucket takes a
while to arrive---it has to pass through every pair of hands. That is \emph{latency}, and it grows
with the length of the line. But once the line is full, a bucket arrives every few seconds no matter
how long the line is. That is \emph{throughput}, and it is set by the slowest pair of hands. A
semaphore line, a chain of Internet routers and the pipeline stages inside a DSP chip all behave
like this. The analogy's limit: buckets do not get corrupted on the way, while a sign misread at one
station is faithfully passed on by every station after it---which is why Chappe needed his error
signals.
\end{analogy}

\mdcside[0.40]{ch01_pipeline}{Space--time diagram of a semaphore line: each coloured line is one
sign marching up the chain. The first sign takes hops $\times$ delay to arrive; after that, a new sign
arrives every period $T$.}
At its height in the 1840s the French network had more than 500 stations and several thousand
kilometres of lines radiating from Paris to Lille, Strasbourg, Lyon, Toulon, Bordeaux, Brest and
beyond (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_chappe_map}). Other countries copied the idea with their own twists. Sweden built a network using Abraham
Edelcrantz's design of ten hinged shutters, each open or closed---a ten-bit \emph{parallel binary
code} with $2^{10}=1024$ symbols. The British Admiralty built a six-shutter line ($2^6=64$ symbols)
from London to the naval base at Deal in 1796. Operation was limited to daylight and good weather,
and the lines were expensive to staff: every tower needed operators awake and watching.
Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_pipeline} shows how signs flowed through such a chain: each one climbs the
line hop by hop, and a new one can enter before the first has arrived. Half a century before
electricity, the optical telegraph had established that information could be moved as a stream of
discrete symbols through a network of relays, and that its speed was limited by two things only:
the symbol rate and the size of the alphabet. Every modem engineer since has fought to raise
exactly those two numbers.

\begin{worked}[Throughput and latency of a semaphore line]
Consider the Paris--Lille line: about 15 stations over some 230\,km. Take as illustrative
assumptions, consistent with the usual accounts, that each operator needs about 30\,s to see a new
sign, set it on his own mechanism and have it confirmed, and that the originating station can form
a new sign every 30\,s (two signs a minute). Each codebook entry needs two signs.

\emph{Latency.} The first sign arrives after about $15\times30\,\mathrm{s}=450$\,s, between seven and
eight minutes; contemporary accounts give about nine. A courier on relays of fast horses might have
needed most of a day.

\emph{Throughput.} Signs leave every 30\,s, so a codebook entry leaves every minute. Each sign
carries $\log_2 92\approx6.52$ bits in Hartley's sense (Section~\ref{sec:ch01:theory}), so the
raw rate is $6.52/30\approx0.22$ bit/s. A 30-entry dispatch takes about 60 signs, or
$60\times30\,\mathrm{s}+450\,\mathrm{s}\approx38$ minutes end to end.

\emph{The power of the codebook.} If each entry stands for a whole word, 0.22 bit/s moves about
one word per minute. Spelling the same words letter by letter at one sign per letter would need
about five signs per word and would be five times slower. The codebook is a compressor that
exploits the shared context of military and government messages.

\emph{The lesson.} Adding stations increases the latency but not the throughput; speeding up
the slowest station increases throughput for everyone. Modern network engineers make exactly
this distinction between the bandwidth--delay product of a path and its bottleneck rate.
\end{worked}

\begin{history}[The first network fraud]
In 1834--36 two bankers in Bordeaux, the brothers Fran\c{c}ois and Joseph Blanc, found a way to
learn the movements of the Paris bond market before their competitors. The optical telegraph was
reserved for government business, so they bribed an operator at a station near Tours to
introduce deliberate ``errors'' into official messages: an agreed sign followed by the
error-correction signal. The error was cancelled before the message reached its destination, so
the government's traffic was never corrupted, but an accomplice watching the line with a
telescope downstream of the bribed station could read it. The scheme ran for about two years
before it was discovered. No law covered it; the episode led to a French law in 1837 that made
unauthorised use of the telegraph a crime. The Blancs had invented, in one stroke, the side
channel, steganography and the insider attack on a protocol's error-control mechanism.
\end{history}

\subsection{Heliographs and signal lamps}

\mdcsidephoto[0.42]{ch01_heliograph}{Ottoman signallers with heliographs on their tripods at Huj,
Palestine, during the First World War. The mirror flashes sunlight toward a distant station in Morse
code.}
Optical signalling did not end with the semaphore; it simply got faster. The \term{heliograph}, a
pivoted mirror that flashed sunlight in Morse code (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_heliograph}), was used by
the British army in India and Afghanistan from the 1870s and by the US Army in the campaigns in
Arizona in the 1880s. A good heliograph link spanned tens of kilometres in desert air, and record
links approaching 300\,km were reported. Its virtues were those of any narrow-beam optical link: it
needed no wire that an enemy could cut, and its beam was hard to intercept from outside the line of
sight. At night and at sea the signal lamp with a shutter took over; ships still carry them. The
idea is far from dead. Free-space optical links between buildings, and the laser terminals that
connect satellites in today's low-Earth-orbit constellations (Chapter~\ref{ch:satellite}), are the
heliograph's direct descendants---a mirror and the Sun replaced by a laser diode and a
photodetector, but the same narrow beam, the same need for a clear line of sight, and the same
security that comes from a beam nobody else can see.

\begin{keyidea}[What the optical era bequeathed: alphabet, codebook, protocol]
Before electricity, engineers had already discovered the three layers of any digital
communication system. A \emph{signal alphabet} of states chosen to be distinguishable through
haze and fatigue (the physical layer, Chapters~\ref{ch:baseband}--\ref{ch:modulation}). A
\emph{codebook} that maps frequent messages to short sequences (source coding,
Chapter~\ref{ch:sourcecoding}). And a \emph{protocol} of control signals, acknowledgements and
retransmissions that makes an unreliable relay chain reliable (the link layer). What they lacked
was a fast symbol rate, and electricity supplied it.
\end{keyidea}

\section{The electric telegraph}
\label{sec:ch01:telegraph}
%======================================================================

\subsection{From Volta's pile to the needle telegraph}

\mdcsidephoto[0.36]{ch01_cooke_wheatstone}{The Cooke and Wheatstone five-needle telegraph of 1837
(Science Museum, London). Two needles at a time swing to point at a letter on the diamond-shaped
grid---a code anyone could read.}
The optical telegraph had two fatal weaknesses: fog and night. Electricity had neither. It offered a
signal that moved along a wire faster than any horse and did not care about the weather, and the
ingredients arrived in quick succession. Alessandro Volta's battery (1800) gave a steady current.
Hans Christian \O rsted (1820) found that a current deflects a compass needle. William Sturgeon
(1825) and Joseph Henry (around 1830) built powerful electromagnets, and Henry showed that a magnet
wound with many turns of fine wire could be operated through a long circuit---the key to signalling
over distance.

In 1833 Carl Friedrich Gauss and Wilhelm Weber strung a wire about a kilometre long across the
rooftops of G\"ottingen between the observatory and the physics laboratory. They signalled with left
and right deflections of a galvanometer needle, encoding letters as short binary sequences---two of
the greatest scientists of the century chatting in binary across a university town. In 1838 Carl
August von Steinheil discovered that the earth itself could serve as the return conductor, halving
the amount of wire a telegraph needed; the ``earth return'' was standard on telegraph lines for a
century.

William Fothergill Cooke and Charles Wheatstone patented a needle telegraph in Britain in 1837
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_cooke_wheatstone}). Their first commercial instrument had five needles on a
diamond-shaped board engraved with twenty letters. Current in two of the five wires deflected two
needles so that they pointed together at the intended letter. C, J, Q, U, X and Z were missing, and
the operators simply spelled around them. The beauty of the design was that an untrained clerk could
read it. It went into service along the Great Western Railway out of Paddington in 1839, and in
January 1845 it made headlines. A description of the murderer John Tawell, who had boarded a train
at Slough, was telegraphed ahead to London, and police were waiting for him on the platform. For the
first time in history, news had outrun a train, and the public suddenly understood what the strange
wires beside the railway were for.

The five-needle instrument needed five (later six) wires, and wire was the dominant cost of a
telegraph. Cooke and Wheatstone soon moved to two-needle and then single-needle instruments, in
which letters were sent as sequences of left and right deflections. Fewer wires meant more symbols
per letter and a trained operator. This is the first appearance of a trade-off that runs through the
whole of this book: \emph{parallel} transmission uses more channels and simple coding; \emph{serial}
transmission uses one channel, a richer code and more time per character. Your laptop faced the same
choice when the wide parallel printer port gave way to the single-lane USB, and every high-speed
link in Chapter~\ref{ch:wireline} faces it still.

\subsection{Morse, Vail and the code}
\label{sec:ch01:morse}

\mdcsidephoto[0.38]{ch01_morse_recorder}{Samuel Morse photographed by Mathew Brady around 1857 with
his telegraph register, the instrument that embossed dots and dashes on a moving paper tape.}
In the United States, Samuel F.~B.~Morse---a successful portrait painter turned inventor---and his
collaborator Alfred Vail chose the serial path from the start
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_morse_recorder}): a single circuit, switched on and off by a key, whose pattern
of short and long pulses encoded the message. Morse's earliest scheme sent numbers that referred to
words in a numbered dictionary, as Chappe had; the letter code that bears his name took shape in
1838--44, and Vail's role in it was large. Congress funded an experimental line from Washington to
Baltimore, and on 24 May 1844 Morse sent the biblical phrase ``What hath God wrought''. Within a
decade telegraph wires followed the railways across the eastern United States. The transcontinental
line was completed in October 1861, and the Pony Express, which had carried mail across the West for
eighteen months, closed within days---perhaps the first business in history to be wiped out by a
communication network, and certainly not the last.

The original receiver embossed the dots and dashes on a moving paper tape. Operators soon found
that they could read the clicking of the receiving magnet by ear faster than they could read the
tape, and the \emph{sounder} replaced the register. The human ear and brain became the demodulator
and decoder, and a skilled operator could copy 30--40 words per minute.

The code itself evolved. The original ``American Morse'' used internal spaces and dashes of several
lengths, which were awkward on long lines and especially on submarine cables. Friedrich Gerke's 1848
revision for the German telegraphs used only dots and dashes of fixed lengths; with small changes it
was adopted at the International Telegraph Conference of 1865 as the International (or Continental)
Morse code, and it is the version still heard on the amateur bands.

\chOnePair{ch01_morse_key}{A Morse key, from Frank Pope's nineteenth-century handbook of
telegraph practice: the operator's hand is the modulator.}{ch01_morse_tree}{Morse code drawn as a binary tree:
go left for a dot, right for a dash. Frequent letters (drawn large) sit near the top.}

The Morse code is a remarkable piece of engineering. Its designers wanted to minimise the time needed
to send typical English text, and they realised that the answer was to give short codes to frequent
letters. According to the traditional account, Vail estimated letter frequencies by counting the type
in a printer's type case, which held thousands of E's but only a few hundred Z's. The code gives E a
single dot and T a single dash, while Q, Y, J and Z receive four symbols.
Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_morse_tree} makes the design visible: drawn as a tree, the common letters crowd
the top branches. This is a \term{variable-length source code}, a century before David Huffman proved
(in 1952) how to construct the optimal one. Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_morse_huffman} compares the two.

\mdcfig{ch01_morse_huffman}{Top: the duration of each International Morse letter in dot units
(including the 3-unit gap that ends it), and the length of a binary Huffman code designed for the
same letter frequencies, with letters sorted from most to least frequent. Both codes give short
codewords to frequent letters. Bottom: average cost per letter of English (letters only, no
spaces). The Morse figure is in time units of its constrained on-off channel; the others are in
binary digits.}

The timing rules of International Morse code are strict, almost musical: a dot lasts one unit, a dash
three, the gap between the elements of a letter one unit, between letters three and between words
seven. The duration of a letter whose code has $n_\cdot$ dots and $n_-$ dashes, including the gap
that follows it, is therefore
\begin{equation}
D = n_\cdot + 3n_- + (n_\cdot+n_- - 1) + 3 = 2n_\cdot + 4n_- + 2 \quad\text{units}.
\label{eq:ch01:morsedur}
\end{equation}
E costs 4 units, T costs 6, and Q, Y and J cost 16. Operators measure speed in words per minute
using the standard word ``PARIS'', which with its trailing word gap occupies exactly 50 units; at
$w$ words per minute the unit therefore lasts $T_u = 60/(50w) = 1.2/w$ seconds, or 60\,ms at
20\,wpm. Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_paris_timing} counts the units out.

\mdcfig{ch01_paris_timing}{The standard word PARIS keyed in International Morse code. Dots last one
unit, dashes three; gaps are one unit inside a letter, three between letters and seven after the word.
The whole word takes exactly 50 units, which is how ``words per minute'' is defined.}

\mdcfig{ch01_morse}{Top: the on-off keyed envelope of ``SOS''. Bottom: the power spectrum of
repeated Morse traffic with instantaneous (hard) keying and with raised-cosine shaped edges.
Shaping the pulses reduces the out-of-band spectrum by more than 40\,dB, which is why operators
added ``click filters'' to their keys.}

\begin{worked}[How good is Morse code?]
Using the standard English letter frequencies plotted in Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_morse_huffman} and
Equation~\eqref{eq:ch01:morsedur}, the average Morse letter costs
\[
\bar D = \sum_\ell p_\ell D_\ell \approx 9.07\ \text{units}.
\]
Is that good? The entropy of the letter distribution (Chapter~\ref{ch:infotheory}) is
\[
H = -\sum_\ell p_\ell\log_2 p_\ell \approx 4.17\ \text{bits per letter},
\]
and a binary Huffman code for the same frequencies averages 4.20 binary digits per letter,
within 1\% of the entropy. On the face of it Morse, at 9.07 units, looks twice as wasteful.

The comparison is unfair, because a Morse unit is not a free binary digit. The line must be on
for exactly one or three units, then off for at least one; it is a \emph{constrained} channel. In
his 1948 paper Shannon computed the capacity of exactly this telegraph channel (dot 2 units
including its trailing space, dash 4, letter space 3, word space 6) and found
$C\approx0.539$ bits per unit time. A Morse letter therefore uses $9.07\times0.539\approx4.89$ bits
of channel capacity to carry 4.17 bits of information: an efficiency of about 85\%. Vail's type
case got him within about 15\% of the best that any code could do on his channel.

Baudot's fixed five-unit code (Section~\ref{sec:ch01:baudot}), sent synchronously so that every unit
is a free binary digit, needs only 5 units per letter: $4.17/5\approx83$\% efficient, but on a
channel with nearly twice the capacity per unit. With start and stop elements (7.5 units per
character, the ITA2 teleprinter standard) the figure falls to 56\%. The lesson is that a code
must be judged against the channel it drives: Morse code is excellent \emph{source} coding
married to a mediocre \emph{channel} alphabet.
\end{worked}

Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_morse} shows the keyed envelope of ``SOS'' and something the early radio
operators discovered the hard way: the \emph{spectrum} of the keyed signal. Switching a carrier on and
off instantaneously produces sharp edges, and sharp edges have wide spectra. A radio telegraph with
hard keying spattered interference---``key clicks''---across neighbouring channels. The cure was to
round off the edges of each pulse, and the lower panel shows that raised-cosine edges lower the
spectral sidelobes by tens of decibels. Pulse shaping to control bandwidth is exactly the subject of
Chapter~\ref{ch:baseband}, where it becomes the root-raised-cosine filter inside every modern modem.

\begin{inpractice}[Baud, bits and words per minute]
Three units of speed coexist in the telegraph literature and one survives today. \emph{Words per
minute} (wpm) is the operator's unit, defined by the 50-unit word PARIS for Morse and by
six characters (five letters and a space) per word for teleprinters. The \emph{baud}, named after
\'Emile Baudot, is the number of signalling elements (symbols) per second: a 20\,wpm Morse signal
runs at $50\times20/60\approx17$\,baud. The \emph{bit per second} counts information. The
classic 60\,wpm teleprinter ran at 45.45\,baud: each 22\,ms unit carried one binary element, each
character took 7.5 units (one start, five data, one and a half stop), giving about 6.06 characters
per second, or 60.6 words per minute. Amateur radio teletype still uses 45.45\,baud. When a modern
modem sends 4 bits per symbol, its bit rate is four times its baud rate; confusing the two is a
classic error (Chapter~\ref{ch:baseband}).
\end{inpractice}

\subsection{Relays, the earth return and the first regenerators}

\mdcsidephoto[0.42]{ch01_relay}{A telegraph relay of the 1850s: a sensitive electromagnet (right)
pulls an armature that closes a local circuit, re-sending the signal at full strength.}
A telegraph line loses current through the series resistance of the wire and through leakage across
wet insulators. Beyond a few hundred kilometres the received current was too weak to operate a
sounder. The solution was the \term{relay} (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_relay}), an electromagnet whose
armature closes a contact in a
second, locally powered circuit. Joseph Henry had demonstrated the principle in the 1830s, and
Morse's lines used relays at intermediate stations to retransmit the signal into a fresh section of
line.

The relay deserves a moment's thought, because it was the first \term{regenerator}. It does not
amplify the weak, rounded, noisy current it receives; it \emph{decides} whether the current is above
or below its threshold and then sends a fresh, full-strength, square pulse onward
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_relay_regen}). Small amounts of noise and distortion are thrown away at every
relay instead of accumulating. That is precisely the property that, a century later, made digital
transmission win over analog (Section~\ref{sec:ch01:digital}): a binary signal can be cleaned up at
every hop. The price, as every relay station operator knew, was that a wrong decision is passed on
with full confidence.


\begin{analogy}[The copyist and the photocopier]
Make a photocopy of a photocopy of a photocopy, and by the tenth generation the page is grey mush:
every copy adds its own specks and blur, and nothing removes them. That is an analog amplifier
chain. Now give the smudged page to a careful copyist who \emph{reads} each word and writes it out
afresh in clean ink. As long as each word is still legible, the hundredth copy is as crisp as the
first. That is a regenerator. The analogy's limit is the honest one: if the copyist misreads a word,
the mistake is written out in beautiful handwriting and nobody downstream can tell.
\end{analogy}

\mdcfig{ch01_relay_regen}{A relay as a regenerator. Top: the weak, smeared, noisy current arriving
from a long line, with the relay's threshold. Bottom: the fresh pulses the relay sends on. The noise
is gone---but where a burst of interference pushed one element over the threshold (shaded), the
relay sends a wrong pulse just as cleanly as the right ones.}


\subsection{Making one wire do more: duplex and quadruplex}

Telegraph wires were expensive, and a single operator could send only 30 or 40 words per
minute. The first attack on this bottleneck was \term{duplex} operation---sending in both directions
at once on one wire. In the duplex circuits made practical by Joseph Stearns in 1872, each station's
receiving relay was wound differentially, or connected in a balanced bridge with an artificial line,
so that it ignored the station's own outgoing current and responded only to current from the far
end. That balanced bridge is the ancestor of the telephone \emph{hybrid} and of the echo cancellers
in every modem, DSL line and Ethernet transceiver (Chapter~\ref{ch:wireline}): to hear the far end
while you are talking, subtract an accurate model of yourself.

\mdcside[0.40]{ch01_quadruplex}{Edison's quadruplex alphabet. Four current levels carry two bits per
element: the sign (read by a polarised relay) and the size (read by a neutral relay).}
Thomas Edison's \term{quadruplex} of 1874 went further. It sent two messages in each direction at
once by using both the \emph{polarity} and the \emph{magnitude} of the line current as independent
signals. One receiving relay was polarised and responded only to the direction of the current; the
other was neutral and responded only to its strength. It was, in effect, a four-level amplitude
alphabet $\{-2,-1,+1,+2\}$ carrying two independent bits per signalling element
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_quadruplex}), plus full-duplex operation---multilevel signalling eighty years
before the term existed. Western Union reckoned that it doubled the value of its wire plant without
stringing a single new line. The same idea now runs your data centre: the PAM-4 signalling of 400\,Gb/s
Ethernet in Chapter~\ref{ch:baseband} uses exactly four levels to carry two bits per symbol, and its
designers worry, as Edison's linemen did, about whether the levels can still be told apart at the far
end of a lossy line. (The quadruplex also produced one of the great patent fights of the age: Edison
sold his rights to the financier Jay Gould, and Western Union contested them in court for years.)

\subsection{Baudot's multiplex and the five-unit code}
\label{sec:ch01:baudot}

\mdcsidephoto[0.30]{ch01_baudot_code}{Baudot's code table from his 1888 US patent: each letter is a
pattern of five $+$ and $-$ elements, one per key.}
The second attack on the bottleneck was \term{time-division multiplexing} (TDM): let several
operators take turns on one wire, so fast that each thinks he has it to himself. \'Emile Baudot, an
engineer of the French telegraph administration, patented his printing telegraph in 1874, and it
entered service in France in the late 1870s. At each end of the line a \emph{distributor}---a disc of
segmented contacts swept by a rotating brush---connected the line in turn to each of several
operators, typically four and sometimes six (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_baudot}).

During its time slot each operator's keyboard of five piano-like keys put a five-unit character on
the line; the receiving
distributor, rotating in synchronism, routed the five units to the corresponding printer, which
decoded and printed the character. Operators worked to a rhythm set by an audible ``cadence''
signal, pressing a chord of keys once per revolution---more like a pianist than a clerk.


Every character was therefore a fixed-length five-unit word, the \term{Baudot code}
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_baudot_code}). With $2^5=32$
combinations it could not cover letters, digits and punctuation directly, so two of the combinations
were used as \emph{shift} characters that switched between a letters page and a figures page---a
stateful code that every teleprinter user learned to curse when a lost shift turned a message into
numerals. Donald Murray adapted the code in 1901 for keyboard-perforated paper tape, choosing the
assignments so that the most frequent letters punched the fewest holes to reduce mechanical wear.
Murray's code became International Telegraph Alphabet No.~2 (ITA2), the code of teleprinters and of
the telex network for most of the twentieth century, and the ancestor of ASCII.

Baudot's system faced a problem that every TDM system since has faced: the two distributors must
rotate at the same speed and phase, or characters land in the wrong printer. Baudot's distributors
were governed mechanically and nudged back into step by correction pulses sent over the line---a
feedback loop for clock synchronisation, the subject of Chapter~\ref{ch:sync}. The unit of signalling
rate, the \emph{baud}, honours him: one baud is one symbol per second. Note the distinction, which we
will use constantly: the baud rate counts symbols, while the bit rate counts bits, and the two differ
whenever a symbol carries more than one bit.

\begin{analogy}[Two dancers who cannot see each other]
Synchronised distributors are like two dancers in separate rooms performing the same routine to the
same music. If the music is shared (a correction signal on the line), they stay in step for ever. If
each relies on an internal sense of tempo, they drift, and after a while one is a whole step ahead.
Start--stop framing is the compromise: the dancers only need to keep time for one short phrase, and
a loud clap (the start element) re-aligns them before every phrase. Chapter~\ref{ch:sync} is, in a
sense, the theory of the clap.
\end{analogy}

Two refinements turned Baudot's idea into the teleprinter. First, the start--stop principle,
developed for the teleprinters of the Morkrum company in the United States in the 1900s and
1910s, dispensed with continuously synchronised distributors. Each character was framed by a
\emph{start} element, which started the receiving mechanism, and a \emph{stop} element, which gave it
time to come to rest. The receiver had only to hold its speed for the duration of one character. The
same start--stop framing survives in every UART, which is why serial ports are configured as ``8N1''
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_startstop}). Second, keyboard-to-tape perforation separated typing from
transmission, so that messages could be prepared off line and sent at the full speed of the line.
Teleprinters connected through switched networks---TWX in the United States and the telex networks of
Europe from the early 1930s---became the backbone of business and press communication until fax and
e-mail displaced them.

\mdcfig{ch01_startstop}{Start--stop framing, then and now. Top: the letter A in the five-unit ITA2
code, framed by a start element and a 1.5-unit stop element, as sent by a 1930s teleprinter. Bottom:

\emph{mark}; the start element's drop to \emph{space} tells the receiver to begin timing.}

\begin{keyidea}[Three ways to share a channel]
Every multiplexing scheme ever built divides the channel's resources along some dimension so
that the signals do not interfere. Baudot divided \emph{time}. Carrier telephony
(Section~\ref{sec:ch01:carrier}) divided \emph{frequency}. Code-division systems, a century
later, divided the signal space using nearly orthogonal \emph{codes}, and multi-antenna systems
divide \emph{space}. Chapter~\ref{ch:multipleaccess} treats all four as one idea: making the
signals of different users approximately orthogonal.
\end{keyidea}

\subsection{Printing telegraphs, tickers and the Victorian Internet}

\chOnePair{ch01_hughes}{A Hughes printing telegraph of about 1860 (Science Museum, London). The
operator plays a piano-like keyboard; a type wheel spinning in step with the sender's prints the
letters on paper tape.}{ch01_ticker}{An Edison stock ticker of the 1870s under its glass dome
(National Museum of American History): the first real-time data broadcast service.}

Long before Baudot, inventors had sought to print messages directly in plain letters. Royal House's
printing telegraph (1846) and David Edward Hughes's instrument of 1855 used a continuously rotating
type wheel at the receiver, synchronised with a similar wheel at the sender; a pulse sent at the
moment the desired letter passed caused it to be printed. The Hughes printer (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_hughes}) became the standard
instrument of continental European telegraph administrations.

In 1867 Edward Calahan adapted the printing telegraph to send stock prices from the New York exchange
to brokers' offices on a narrow paper tape---the stock \emph{ticker}
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_ticker}), named for its sound---and in
1869--71 the young Thomas Edison made his first fortune by improving it. Ticker tape was the first
real-time data broadcast service, delivering prices to many subscribers on a shared circuit. (The
paper snowstorms of New York's ticker-tape parades are its souvenir.)

Charles Wheatstone's automatic transmitter of 1858 read messages from punched paper tape, and by the
1880s automatic Wheatstone circuits carried a few hundred words per minute on good lines, far beyond
any human hand. Punched tape also introduced \term{store-and-forward} switching. A message arriving at
a relay office was punched onto tape, the tape was torn off and carried to the transmitter for the
next leg, and the message continued when that line was free. Torn-tape relay centres were still
handling enormous volumes of telegrams in the 1950s, and they are the direct ancestor of message
switching and, through it, of the packet switching of Section~\ref{sec:ch01:internet}.

The social effect of the telegraph was immense, and Tom Standage's phrase ``the Victorian Internet''
is apt. News agencies were built on it: Paul Julius Reuter used carrier pigeons in 1850 to bridge a
gap in the telegraph between Aachen and Brussels before moving to London in 1851. Weather forecasting
became possible once observations from many places could be gathered faster than the weather moved;
Robert FitzRoy's forecasts were published in \emph{The Times} from 1861. Railways ran on telegraphed
train orders, Greenwich time was distributed electrically from 1852, and financial markets in distant
cities began to move together. Each of these is an application that only a fast, wide-area data
network makes possible, and each reappeared on the Internet.

\begin{history}[1865: the first international standards body]
Every country's telegraph network ended at its border, where messages were written out by hand,
carried across and re-sent under a different code, tariff and set of rules. In 1865, twenty
European states met in Paris and signed the International Telegraph Convention, creating the
International Telegraph Union to agree on common operating rules, tariffs and codes, including the
International Morse code. Renamed the International Telecommunication Union in 1932, and now a
specialised agency of the United Nations, the ITU is the oldest agency of the UN system and one of
the oldest intergovernmental organisations in the world. Its descendants are everywhere in this
book: ITU-T recommendations define the PCM standard G.711 and the V-series modems
(Chapters~\ref{ch:pcm} and~\ref{ch:wireline}); ITU-R manages the radio spectrum and orbital slots
(Chapter~\ref{ch:satellite}); and IMT-2020 is the ITU name for what the industry calls 5G
(Chapter~\ref{ch:cellular}). Interoperability by international agreement is as old as international
telecommunication itself.
\end{history}

\subsection{Gutta-percha and the first cables}

An overhead wire is insulated by air and porcelain. A cable lying on the sea bed needs an insulator
that is waterproof, durable and workable, and in the 1840s nothing man-made would do. The answer came
from the forests of the Malay peninsula: gutta-percha, a natural latex that softens in hot water, can
be extruded around a copper wire, and hardens into a tough, water-resistant coating (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_cable_xsec}). Werner von
Siemens used it for underground lines in Prussia in 1847, and in 1850 a single gutta-percha-covered
wire was laid across the English Channel from Dover to Cap Gris-Nez.

\mdcfig[0.88]{ch01_cable_xsec}{The cores of the 1858 and 1866 Atlantic cables drawn to the same scale,
with radii estimated from the published masses of copper and gutta-percha per nautical mile. The 1866
core had nearly three times the copper---lower $R$---which, more than anything, made it faster. (Armour
wires are schematic.)}

It carried a few messages before it failed---according to the usual story, a French fisherman hauled
it up and cut out a piece, convinced he had found a new kind of seaweed with a gold core. In 1851 an
armoured cable, its core protected by a sheath of iron wires, succeeded, and within a few years cables
linked Britain to Ireland, the Netherlands and the Continent. The obvious next step was the
Atlantic.

\subsection{The 1858 Atlantic cable}

The most dramatic engineering story of the nineteenth century is the laying of the transatlantic
telegraph cable. The American businessman Cyrus Field raised the money, and in 1857 and again in
1858 two warships, HMS \emph{Agamemnon} and USS \emph{Niagara}, each carried half of the cable to
mid-ocean, spliced the ends and steamed in opposite directions paying out cable. After several
heartbreaking failures the cable was completed in August 1858, linking Valentia Island in Ireland to
Trinity Bay in Newfoundland, about 3000\,km apart. It carried Queen Victoria's 98-word message to
President Buchanan---which took about sixteen hours to transmit---and set off celebrations in New York.
Within about three weeks it had failed entirely.

What went wrong is a story every engineer should know by heart. The cable's electrician, Wildman
Whitehouse, a surgeon turned electrical experimenter, believed that a cable could be worked at any
speed if it were driven hard enough. He applied pulses from large induction coils, reported at about
2000 volts, to overcome the weak and sluggish signals, and the gutta-percha insulation, already
flawed by manufacture and storage, broke down.

\chOnePair{ch01_kelvin}{William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin (photographed in 1902), the physicist
who understood the long cable.}{ch01_mirror_galv}{Thomson's mirror galvanometer in use (from an early
electrical handbook). A lamp shines on the tiny mirror inside the galvanometer coil; the reflected
spot of light swings across the scale at the right in response to microamperes.}

William Thomson, the young professor of natural philosophy at Glasgow and a director of the company,
had argued all along for a different approach: low voltages, a thicker conductor, and a far more
sensitive receiver. His \term{mirror galvanometer} (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_mirror_galv}), in which a
tiny magnet carried a mirror that deflected a spot of light across a scale, could detect currents far
too small for any relay. During the cable's short life it was Thomson's instrument, not Whitehouse's,
that made the cable work at all. Whitehouse was dismissed. Think about what that spot of light meant:
instead of shouting louder at the sending end, Thomson listened more carefully at the receiving end.
A better receiver is worth more transmitted power, and a receiver that is better by a factor of
a hundred is worth a hundred times the power---a lesson that the radio engineers of
Chapter~\ref{ch:noise}, chasing every fraction of a decibel of noise figure, would learn all over
again. Thomson's galvanometer was, in effect, the first low-noise amplifier.


The failure prompted the first serious engineering inquiry into a communication system. A joint
committee of the British Board of Trade and the Atlantic Telegraph Company took evidence from 1859 to
1861 on everything from the purity of copper to the testing of insulation. One of its legacies was
the demand for agreed standards of electrical resistance, which led the British Association to set up
a committee on electrical standards in 1861---the origin of the ohm as a practical unit. Precise
measurement, standards and acceptance testing entered communication engineering through the failure
of a cable.

\subsection{Kelvin's model: the cable as a diffusion line}

Here is the puzzle Thomson had to solve. Send a sharp, crisp pulse into one end of a 3000\,km cable.
What comes out of the other end is not a pulse at all but a slow, low swell that takes seconds to rise
and much longer to die away. Send pulses quickly, and the swells pile on top of one another until the
message is a blur. Why?

\chOneFigH[0.52]{ch01_rc_ladder}{Kelvin's model of a cable: every short piece $\Delta x$ has a little
series resistance in the copper and a little capacitance to the sea. Charging all those capacitors
through all those resistors is what makes the cable slow.}

\begin{analogy}[Pushing honey through a garden hose]
Imagine a very long, slightly stretchy garden hose filled with honey. Squeeze a pump at one end: the
pressure at the far end does not jump; it creeps up, because the honey near you must first bulge the
hose (store charge, like a capacitor) and then force its way past the friction of the honey ahead (a
resistor). Pump in short squirts and the far end sees only a slow, smeared rise and fall. Make the
hose twice as long and both the friction \emph{and} the bulging double, so everything takes four
times as long. That is the whole of Kelvin's theory. The limit of the analogy: real honey has
inertia, and so, it turns out, does electricity---the piece Kelvin left out, and Heaviside put back.
\end{analogy}

The man who understood what was really happening was Thomson, later Lord Kelvin
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_kelvin}). In 1854--55 he
analysed a long submarine cable as a distributed resistance $R$ and capacitance $C$ per unit length
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_rc_ladder}).
The capacitance arises because the copper conductor and the sea water outside the insulation form a
long cylindrical capacitor; the resistance is that of the copper. Neglecting inductance and leakage,
he found that the cable behaves exactly like a bar conducting heat, and that every time scale of the
cable is proportional to a single constant,
\begin{equation}
\tau = RC\ell^2 ,
\label{eq:ch01:kr}
\end{equation}
where $\ell$ is the length. Read it aloud: \emph{double the length of the cable and you must signal
four times more slowly}. This is Thomson's ``law of squares'', usually called the \term{KR law} after
the symbols then used for the total capacitance ($K$) and resistance ($R$) of the cable. (The box
below derives it.)


Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_cable} shows the consequence. When dots are sent slowly, each has time to rise
and fall; when they are sent faster, the slow tails of earlier pulses pile up on later ones and the
pattern is lost. The name for this is \term{intersymbol interference} (ISI), and it is the central
problem of Chapters~\ref{ch:baseband} and~\ref{ch:equalization}. A 5G modem fighting multipath echoes
and a 100\,Gb/s SerDes fighting a lossy circuit board are fighting the ghost of the 1858 cable.

\mdcfig{ch01_cable}{Kelvin's model of a long submarine cable. Left: the response to a step of
voltage at three cable lengths; the delay scales with the square of the length. Right: a train of
pulses through the same cable at two keying speeds. At the faster rate each pulse is smeared into
its neighbours---intersymbol interference.}

\begin{deeper}[The diffusion equation of a cable]
With series resistance $R$ and shunt capacitance $C$ per unit length, and no inductance or leakage,
the voltage on the cable obeys a diffusion equation,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial v}{\partial t} = \frac{1}{RC}\,\frac{\partial^2 v}{\partial x^2},
\label{eq:ch01:diffusion}
\end{equation}
the same equation that governs the flow of heat in a bar. Its response at distance $\ell$ to a
voltage step applied at $t=0$ is
\begin{equation}
v(\ell,t) = \operatorname{erfc}\!\left(\frac{\ell}{2}\sqrt{\frac{RC}{t}}\right),
\label{eq:ch01:erfc}
\end{equation}
and its response to a short pulse of area $A$ (the derivative of the step response) is
\begin{equation}
h(\ell,t) = \frac{A\,\ell}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\sqrt{\frac{RC}{t^3}}\;\exp\!\left(-\frac{RC\ell^2}{4t}\right),
\end{equation}
which peaks at $t = RC\ell^2/6$ and then decays slowly, as $t^{-3/2}$. Both responses depend on $t$
only through the ratio $t/(RC\ell^2)$, which is why every time scale is proportional to
$\tau=RC\ell^2$.

In the frequency domain the same physics reads differently. A semi-infinite RC line has the transfer
function $H(\omega)=\exp(-\ell\sqrt{j\omega RC})$, whose magnitude in decibels falls in proportion
to $\sqrt{\omega}$. High frequencies are attenuated far more than low ones, so the sharp edges of a
telegraph pulse are stripped away and only a slow, rounded hump survives. A pattern of alternating
dots (``reversals''), whose fundamental frequency is $1/(2T)$ for element duration $T$, arrives with
its swing reduced by roughly $\exp(-\sqrt{\pi\tau/2T})$.
\end{deeper}

The left panel of Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_cable_speed} shows how quickly the received swing of an
alternating pattern collapses once the element duration $T$ falls below about a quarter of $\tau$:
there is a fairly sharp ``speed limit'' for any given cable. The right panel turns that into the law
of squares for the two Atlantic cables.

\mdcfig{ch01_cable_speed}{Left: received peak-to-peak swing of an alternating pattern through an
RC cable, as a fraction of the transmitted swing, against element duration in units of
$\tau=RC\ell^2$. Right: the law of squares. Taking a 10\% swing as the limit of readability, the
maximum signalling speed falls as $1/\ell^2$; the two lines use the per-kilometre resistance and
capacitance estimated in the worked example for the 1858 and 1866 cable designs.}


\begin{worked}[How fast could the Atlantic cable signal?]
Contemporary descriptions give the 1858 cable a conductor of about 107\,lb of copper per nautical
mile, insulated with about 261\,lb of gutta-percha, and the 1866 cable about 300\,lb of copper and
400\,lb of gutta-percha per nautical mile (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_cable_xsec}). Treat these as
approximate and estimate $R$ and $C$.

\emph{Resistance.} Copper has density 8960\,kg/m$^3$ and resistivity $1.72\times10^{-8}\,\Omega$m.
For 1866: $300\,\mathrm{lb}=136$\,kg spread over 1852\,m gives a cross-section of
$136/(8960\times1852)\approx8.2\,\mathrm{mm}^2$, so $R\approx1.72\times10^{-8}/8.2\times10^{-6}\approx
2.1\,\Omega$/km. For 1858, the cross-section is $2.9\,\mathrm{mm}^2$ and $R\approx5.9\,\Omega$/km (the
copper of the day was less pure, so the true value was higher).

\emph{Capacitance.} The cable is a coaxial capacitor with $C = 2\pi\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r/\ln(b/a)$.
Gutta-percha has a density close to that of water and a relative permittivity of roughly 3--4;
the masses give conductor and insulation radii of about $a=1.6$\,mm and $b=5.9$\,mm for 1866
($\ln(b/a)\approx1.3$), so with $\varepsilon_r\approx3.5$, $C\approx0.15\,\mu$F/km. The 1858 core gives
$C\approx0.12\,\mu$F/km.

\emph{Time constant.} With $\ell\approx3000$\,km,
\[
\tau_{1866}\approx 2.1\times0.15\times10^{-6}\times(3000)^2\approx2.8\ \mathrm{s},\qquad
\tau_{1858}\approx 6.5\ \mathrm{s}.
\]

\emph{Speed.} If a 10\% swing is readable, $T\approx0.24\tau$: about 0.7\,s per element for 1866
and 1.6\,s for 1858. Cable code sent each dot and dash as a single element of opposite polarity,
and a word needed roughly 19 elements including spaces, so the estimates are about
$60/(19\times0.7)\approx4.6$ and $2.0$ words per minute.

\emph{Comparison.} The 1866 cable worked at around eight words per minute in service, so a
one-line model with rough materials data gets within a factor of two---and the gap was closed by
Thomson's better receivers, which could read smaller swings, and by \emph{curb} signalling, in
which each pulse was followed by a short pulse of opposite polarity that pulled the line back
toward zero (a hand-made pre-emphasis filter of the kind used in every modern SerDes;
Chapter~\ref{ch:baseband}). The 1858 cable, damaged and over-driven, achieved far less than even
its modest potential: about a tenth of a word per minute for the Queen's message.
\end{worked}

\chOneFigH{ch01_power_vs_isi}{Power fights noise, not distortion (simulated eye diagrams, each drawn to its
own scale). When noise closes the eye, tripling the transmitted voltage opens it. When intersymbol
interference closes it---as on the 1858 cable---tripling the voltage just scales the same closed eye.}

\begin{tryit}[Be the cable engineer]
Lab 34's cable experiment is a Kelvin RC-cable simulator. Start at the 1866 settings (3000\,km,
$R=2.1\,\Omega$/km, $C=0.15\,\mu$F/km) and drag the \emph{keying speed (wpm)} slider up until the
received pattern becomes unreadable. Now halve the \emph{cable length}: how much faster can you key?
Then try Whitehouse's ``fix''---there is no voltage slider, and that is the point.
\end{tryit}


\begin{pitfall}[More power does not cure dispersion]
Whitehouse's mistake is still made. When received pulses are smeared together, raising the
transmitted voltage raises the smeared signal and the ISI by exactly the same factor; the eye
does not open (Chapter~\ref{ch:baseband}). More power helps against \emph{noise}, not against
\emph{distortion}. The cures for distortion (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_power_vs_isi}) are to signal more slowly, to change the medium (lower
$R$ and $C$), to shape the transmitted pulses, or to equalise at the receiver---which is what
Thomson did, in that order of preference. A modern engineer who turns up the transmit power on a
link that is failing because of multipath or chromatic dispersion is repeating 1858.
\end{pitfall}

\subsection{The Great Eastern and the 1866 success}

The American Civil War delayed a second attempt until 1865. The new cable was built on Thomson's
principles---heavier conductor, thicker insulation, careful testing at every stage of manufacture and
laying---and it was loaded into the \emph{Great Eastern}, by far the largest ship afloat and the only
one that could carry the whole cable at once. Two-thirds of the way across, the cable parted and sank
in deep water. Undaunted, the company tried again.


\chOnePair{ch01_great_eastern}{The \emph{Great Eastern}, photographed in 1866, the year she laid
the first durable Atlantic cable. Her size let her carry the entire cable in one load; after landing
the new cable at Heart's Content she went back to mid-ocean to grapple and complete the cable lost
the year before.}{ch01_siphon}{Kelvin's siphon recorder (from the 1911 \emph{Encyclopaedia
Britannica}): a fine glass siphon, moved by the received current, drew the cable signal as an ink
trace on a moving paper tape.}

In 1866 the \emph{Great Eastern} laid a new cable successfully, landing it at Heart's Content,
Newfoundland, in July (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_great_eastern}). Then she returned to mid-ocean, grappled
the lost 1865 cable from a depth of more than 3\,km---imagine fishing for a garden hose from the top
of a skyscraper three times over, in a swell---spliced it and completed it as well. From September
1866 there were two working Atlantic cables, and messages between London and New York took minutes
rather than ten days by steamship. Thomson was knighted for his part.

Cable engineering then advanced quickly. Thomson's \emph{siphon recorder} of 1867
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_siphon}) drew the received signal as an ink trace on a moving tape, a continuous
waveform record from which skilled clerks read the message. It was in effect a strip-chart
oscilloscope, and it let the operators \emph{see}, and correct for, the wandering baseline of the
cable---the same baseline wander that DC-free line codes are designed to prevent in
Chapter~\ref{ch:baseband}. Duplex working was adapted to cables with elaborate artificial lines that
mimicked the real cable's distributed $RC$, so that a cable station could send and receive at the
same time. By the end of the century a web of cables connected every continent, and the British
Empire's ``All Red Line'' circled the globe without touching foreign soil. The cable companies became
some of the most valuable businesses on Earth, and the submarine-cable industry they founded still
carries the overwhelming majority of intercontinental traffic today---on glass fibre rather than
copper and gutta-percha (Section~\ref{sec:ch01:solidstate}).

\begin{inpractice}[The Victorian cable industry, still at sea]
The cable ship, the sea-bed survey, the grapnel, the splice made at sea and the acceptance test run
before a cable is landed are all inventions of the 1850s and 1860s that are still in daily use.
Today some hundreds of submarine cable systems, together roughly a million kilometres of them, carry
almost all intercontinental Internet traffic; satellites carry a small fraction. When a modern cable
is cut by a ship's anchor or an undersea landslide, a repair ship goes out, grapples it from the sea
bed and splices in a new length---exactly as the \emph{Great Eastern} did in 1866, but with
optical-fibre fusion splicers in place of copper and gutta-percha (Chapter~\ref{ch:wireline}).
\end{inpractice}

\subsection{Heaviside and the telegrapher's equations}
\label{sec:ch01:heaviside}

\chOnePairH{ch01_heaviside}{Oliver Heaviside (1850--1925), self-taught and reclusive,
combative and usually right.}{ch01_loading_coil}{An early toroidal telephone loading coil, its iron-dust core
partly cut away. Coils like this, potted in cases in manholes every 1.83\,km, made long telephone loops
possible---and later had to be removed for DSL.}

Kelvin's model omitted two parameters, and that omission hid the cure. Oliver Heaviside
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_heaviside}), a self-taught telegraph engineer whose uncle by marriage was
Charles Wheatstone, wrote down the general equations of a line with series resistance $R$, series
inductance $L$, shunt conductance $G$ (leakage through the insulation) and shunt capacitance $C$, all
per unit length. These are the \term{telegrapher's equations}, and every transmission line in this
book---twisted pair, coaxial cable, printed-circuit trace, antenna feeder---obeys them.
The box below gives them. What matters here is what they say about pulses. For Kelvin's cable, with
negligible $L$ and $G$, high frequencies are attenuated more \emph{and} travel faster than low
frequencies, so a pulse is both shrunk and smeared: different parts of its spectrum arrive at
different times and in different proportions. Heaviside asked a brilliant question: \emph{is there
any line on which every frequency is attenuated equally and travels at the same speed?} If so, a
pulse would arrive smaller but with its shape intact. The answer is yes, when
\begin{equation}
\frac{L}{R} = \frac{C}{G}.
\label{eq:ch01:distortionless}
\end{equation}
Such a line is \term{distortionless}. In words: the line's series time constant $L/R$ must match its
shunt time constant $C/G$, so that the inductance's tendency to hold the current back exactly
balances the capacitance's tendency to soak it up.


\begin{deeper}[The telegrapher's equations and Heaviside's condition]
For a line with series $R$, $L$ and shunt $G$, $C$ per unit length,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial v}{\partial x} = -R\,i - L\frac{\partial i}{\partial t},\qquad
\frac{\partial i}{\partial x} = -G\,v - C\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}.
\label{eq:ch01:telegrapher}
\end{equation}
For a sinusoid at angular frequency $\omega$ the solutions are waves $e^{-\gamma x}$ with
propagation constant
\begin{equation}
\gamma = \alpha + j\beta = \sqrt{(R+j\omega L)(G+j\omega C)}.
\label{eq:ch01:gamma}
\end{equation}
The real part $\alpha$ is the attenuation in nepers per unit length and the imaginary part $\beta$
the phase shift; the phase velocity is $\omega/\beta$. For a cable with negligible $L$ and $G$,
$\gamma\approx\sqrt{j\omega RC}$ and both $\alpha$ and the velocity grow as $\sqrt\omega$: that is
Kelvin's diffusion line.

To find a frequency-independent line, write
$\gamma = \sqrt{RG}\,\sqrt{(1+j\omega L/R)(1+j\omega C/G)}$. If $L/R=C/G$ the two brackets are equal,
the square root is exact, and
\[
\gamma = \sqrt{RG} + j\omega\sqrt{LC}.
\]
The attenuation $\sqrt{RG}$ is then the same at every frequency and the delay $\sqrt{LC}$ per unit
length is constant. For small $R$ and $G$ (that is, $\omega L\gg R$ and $\omega C\gg G$),
$\alpha\approx \tfrac{R}{2}\sqrt{C/L} + \tfrac{G}{2}\sqrt{L/C}$, which falls as $L$ rises until the two
terms balance---at exactly Heaviside's condition.
\end{deeper}

Real lines had far too little inductance to satisfy~\eqref{eq:ch01:distortionless}---$L/R$ much
smaller than $C/G$---and Heaviside proposed the counter-intuitive remedy of \emph{adding} inductance.
Engineers of the day thought inductance was the enemy (it ``slows'' current), so this sounded like
curing a limp by tying weights to the patient's leg. But increasing $L$ moves the line toward the
distortionless condition and, as a bonus, reduces the attenuation. Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_distortionless}
shows the effect on a pulse.

\mdcfig{ch01_distortionless}{Heaviside's insight in one picture. A smooth pulse (left) is sent down
three lines with the same $R$ and $C$ (right; each received pulse is scaled to the same peak so the
shapes can be compared). Kelvin's cable smears it into a long tail. Adding inductance turns the front
into a sharp, delayed wavefront, though a tail remains. A line that also meets $L/R=C/G$ delivers the
pulse undistorted, only delayed and attenuated.}

\begin{history}[Heaviside, Pupin and Campbell]
Heaviside published his analysis in the 1880s, mostly in the trade journal \emph{The Electrician},
in a compressed, operator-laden style that few of his contemporaries could follow. He also
reformulated Maxwell's twenty equations into the four vector equations every engineering student
now learns, developed the operational calculus that became the Laplace transform method, and
predicted the conducting layer in the upper atmosphere that bears his name. His suggestion of
loading was ignored for years, partly because of his combative relations with the Post Office's
chief engineer, William Preece. Around 1899--1900, Michael Pupin of Columbia University and George
Campbell of AT\&T independently worked out how to approximate continuous inductance with discrete
\term{loading coils} at regular intervals; Pupin secured the patent and AT\&T bought the rights.
Heaviside received neither money nor much recognition, and died in straitened circumstances in 1925.
For submarine cables, the Danish engineer Carl Emil Krarup introduced \emph{continuous} loading in
1902, wrapping the conductor in fine iron wire; later cables used permalloy tape and signalled
several times faster than unloaded ones. Paul Nahin's biography (see Further Reading) does justice
to Heaviside.
\end{history}


\subsection{Loading coils and their cutoff}

Pupin's and Campbell's practical answer was to put the inductance in lumps: a coil every mile or so,
rather than inductance smeared along the wire. That works, but it comes with a catch. Discrete loading
creates a periodic structure: a series inductance $L_c$ every distance $d$, with the cable's own
capacitance $Cd$ between coils. Such a ladder of series inductors and shunt capacitors is a low-pass
filter. Below its cutoff frequency
\begin{equation}
f_c = \frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{L_c\,C\,d}}
\label{eq:ch01:loadcut}
\end{equation}
it behaves almost like a uniform distortionless line; above it, the attenuation rises abruptly. So
loading buys a beautifully flat, low-loss voice band and pays for it with a brick wall a little above
it. Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_loading} shows the effect on a typical telephone pair, and
Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_loading_coil} shows the hardware that did it.


\begin{deeper}[Why a ladder has a cutoff]
Treat each loading section as a series inductance $L_c$ followed by a shunt capacitance $Cd$, and
ignore losses. A wave can propagate along such a periodic ladder only if the phase shift per section
is real, which for this ``constant-$k$'' low-pass section requires
$\omega^2 L_c C d/4 \le 1$. The boundary, $\omega_c = 2/\sqrt{L_c C d}$, is
Equation~\eqref{eq:ch01:loadcut}. Above it the per-section phase shift becomes complex and the wave
decays exponentially from coil to coil, however little resistance the wire has.
\end{deeper}

\mdcfig{ch01_loading}{A 22-gauge telephone pair (loop resistance about 106\,$\Omega$/km,
capacitance about 0.052\,$\mu$F/km) with and without H88 loading (88\,mH coils every 1.83\,km),
computed from the telegrapher's equations with the coils treated as lumped elements. Left: loading
cuts the voice-band attenuation by more than half and makes it nearly flat---but above the
3.5\,kHz cutoff the loss climbs steeply. Right: loading also makes the phase velocity nearly
constant across the voice band (and much slower). The shaded band is 300--3400\,Hz.}

\begin{worked}[Why the telephone loop stops at 3.5 kHz]
The standard North American loading for subscriber cables, designated H88, places an 88\,mH coil
every 6000\,ft (1.83\,km). A typical cable pair has a capacitance of about 0.052\,$\mu$F/km, so
each loading section contributes $Cd\approx0.052\times1.83\approx0.094\,\mu$F. From
Equation~\eqref{eq:ch01:loadcut},
\[
f_c = \frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{0.088\times0.094\times10^{-6}}} = \frac{1}{\pi\times9.1\times10^{-5}}
\approx 3.5\ \mathrm{kHz}.
\]
That cutoff sits just above the 3.4\,kHz upper edge of the telephone voice band, and it is no
coincidence: the loading plan was designed for the voice band, and the voice band was in turn
standardised at what the loaded plant could carry. Below 3.4\,kHz the loaded pair loses about
0.5\,dB/km instead of 1--2\,dB/km, which extended the reach of a subscriber loop from a few
kilometres to more than ten. Seventy years later the same coils became an obstacle. DSL signals
occupy frequencies up to megahertz, far above $f_c$, so every loading coil on a loop had to be
removed before DSL could be offered on it (Chapter~\ref{ch:wireline}). And when the Bell System
put digital T1 repeaters on its interoffice cables in 1962, it put them at the manholes where the
loading coils had been---every 6000\,ft.
\end{worked}

\subsection{Voice over a wire}

The telegraph sent discrete symbols; the telephone sent a continuous waveform---the actual shape of a
human voice---and it demanded a new kind of engineering. Several inventors had been working on the
electrical transmission of sound. Philipp Reis in Germany demonstrated a device that could transmit
musical tones and, with difficulty, speech in 1861, and Antonio Meucci's earlier work in New York
remains the subject of priority claims. But the decisive patent was Alexander Graham Bell's, filed on
14 February 1876, the same day Elisha Gray filed a caveat on a similar idea, and granted on 7 March.

\mdcsidephoto[0.30]{ch01_bell_liquid}{Bell's liquid transmitter of March 1876: a needle on the
diaphragm dipped into acidulated water, so the sound varied the resistance of the circuit.}
Bell, a teacher of the deaf with a deep knowledge of acoustics, had been working on a ``harmonic
telegraph'' that would send several telegraph messages at once on different audio
tones---frequency-division multiplexing, forty years early. On 10 March 1876 he used a liquid
transmitter (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_bell_liquid}) to summon his assistant Thomas Watson from the next room, and within a year he was
demonstrating telephony over tens of kilometres using magneto transmitters, in which a diaphragm
moving near a magnet induced the speech current directly.


Bell's transmitter converted the pressure variations of speech into a proportional current: an
\emph{analog} signal. A telegraph signal only had to be detectable; a telephone signal had to be
reproduced \emph{faithfully}, which meant controlling the frequency response, noise and distortion of
every component in the path. Telephone engineers therefore developed, over the next fifty years,
nearly all the concepts of linear systems engineering: frequency response, impedance matching,
transmission loss in decibels, crosstalk, echo and the subjective testing of quality.

Experiments with listeners established that intelligible speech needs roughly the band from 300 to
3400\,Hz. That number---about 3.1\,kHz of useful bandwidth inside a 4\,kHz channel allocation---was
fixed by the 1920s and still defines the digital telephone network: the 8\,kHz sampling rate of
pulse-code modulation (Chapter~\ref{ch:pcm}) is twice the 4\,kHz channel width. It is also why your
friend's voice on an old-fashioned call sounds ``tinny'', and why ``s'' and ``f'' are so easily
confused: their energy lies mostly above 3.4\,kHz (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_voiceband}).

\mdcphoto[0.62]{ch01_bell_boxphone}{Bell's ``box'' telephone of 1876--77, one of the first
commercially available telephones (National Museum of American History). The same magneto
instrument served as both mouthpiece and earpiece: you spoke into the opening, then moved it to your
ear to listen.}

\mdcfig{ch01_voiceband}{Where speech lives (an illustrative long-term speech spectrum) and how much of
it three systems carry. Most of the power is below 1\,kHz, but much of the \emph{intelligibility}---the
consonants---lies higher. The telephone's 300--3400\,Hz band was the economical compromise.}

\subsection{The carbon microphone: the first amplifier}

A magneto transmitter generates its own current from the sound energy, and speech carries very
little energy. The breakthrough that made the telephone practical over city distances was the
\term{carbon transmitter}, developed in 1877--78 by Thomas Edison, Emile Berliner, David Hughes
(who coined the word \emph{microphone}) and, in the form that dominated for a century, Henry
Hunnings with loose carbon granules. Sound pressure varies the contact resistance of the granules,
and the granules sit in series with a battery. The microphone therefore does not \emph{generate} the
speech current; it \emph{modulates} a much larger current drawn from the battery. The electrical
signal power delivered to the line can exceed the acoustic power that drives the diaphragm: the
device has power gain.

\chOnePair{ch01_carbon_button}{Cutaway of a carbon ``button'' transmitter: the diaphragm presses on a
small chamber of carbon granules whose resistance varies with the sound pressure.}{ch01_candlestick}{A
``candlestick'' telephone with rotary dial: carbon transmitter at the top, receiver on the hook, dial
for the automatic exchange at the base.}

\begin{analogy}[A hand on the tap]
A magneto microphone is like trying to water a garden by blowing through a straw: all the energy
comes from you. A carbon microphone is a hand on the tap of a water main. Your voice only wiggles the
tap; the battery, like the water pressure in the mains, supplies the power. A small effort controls
a large flow, and that is the definition of an amplifier. The vacuum tube and the transistor work the
same way---a small signal on the grid or gate controls a large current from the supply---which is why
the carbon microphone deserves the title of the first amplifier in telecommunications.
\end{analogy}

The carbon microphone was the only practical amplifier in telecommunications for forty years, and it
is the reason a telephone line carries a DC loop current: the exchange's battery, standardised at
48\,V, powers the carbon granules. Carbon transmitters were noisy and distorted, but cheap and
robust, and telephones used them into the 1980s.

A telephone set also had to solve a problem that sounds trivial and is not. One pair of wires carries
speech in both directions, but the mouthpiece and earpiece are separate. The induction coil in the
telephone set was arranged as a \term{hybrid}, a bridge circuit (the descendant of the duplex
telegraph's balanced relay) that couples the microphone to the line and the line to the receiver
while partly cancelling the path from the microphone to the user's own ear. A little of the user's
voice, the \emph{sidetone}, was allowed through deliberately, because people talking into a dead
earpiece shout. The same two-wire-to-four-wire hybrid appears at every point where the two-wire local
loop meets the four-wire long-distance network, and its imperfect balance is the source of the
\emph{echo} that long-distance circuits---and later every modem---had to suppress or cancel
(Chapters~\ref{ch:equalization} and~\ref{ch:wireline}).

\subsection{Exchanges, operators and the first automation}

A telephone is useless unless it can reach many other telephones, and connecting every pair of $N$
subscribers directly would need $N(N-1)/2$ lines---for a town of 1000 subscribers, half a million
pairs of wire. The \emph{exchange} replaced that full mesh with a star
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_topologies}): each subscriber has one line to a central switchboard, where an
operator connects any two lines with a patch cord. The first commercial switchboard opened in New
Haven, Connecticut, in January 1878 with 21 subscribers.

\mdcphoto[0.80]{ch01_switchboard}{Operators at a Bell System manual switchboard. Each operator could
reach any line in the exchange through the jacks of the ``multiple'' in front of her; the cords on
the shelf are the switches.}

Telephone companies first hired teenage boys from the telegraph offices as operators, found them rude
to customers, and from 1878, beginning with Emma Nutt in Boston, turned to women, who dominated the
profession for a century (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_switchboard}). The large \emph{multiple} switchboards
of the 1890s let any of dozens of operators reach any of thousands of lines, and as networks grew they
were organised hierarchically: local exchanges connected to toll centres, toll centres to regional
centres. Star, hierarchy and later mesh are the topologies of every network in this book.

\mdcfig[0.98]{ch01_topologies}{The evolution of network topology. A relay chain (optical and early
electric telegraph lines); a star centred on an exchange (the telephone switchboard, 1878); a
hierarchy of exchanges (the long-distance toll network); a distributed mesh with many alternative
paths (Paul Baran's proposal for a survivable network, the ancestor of the Internet); and cellular
coverage, in which a grid of small cells reuses the same frequencies in cells far enough apart
(colours indicate frequency sets).}

\chOnePair{ch01_strowger}{Strowger step-by-step switches (Telephone Museum, Waltham,
Massachusetts). Each dialled pulse lifts or rotates a wiper one step across a bank of contacts.}{ch01_erlang}{Agner Krarup Erlang (1878--1929), who turned telephone traffic into
mathematics.}

\mdcside[0.36]{ch01_dtmf}{The Touch-Tone keypad: each key sends one tone from the low group and one
from the high group at the same time.}
The first automatic switch was invented by Almon Strowger, an undertaker in Kansas City who,
according to the story, suspected that the local operator was diverting his customers to a
competitor. His step-by-step switch (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_strowger}), patented in 1891 and first used
at La Porte, Indiana, in 1892, moved a wiper in response to pulses from the subscriber's telephone:
dialling ``3'' produced three pulses that stepped the switch three positions. The rotary dial, which
generated those pulses at about ten per second, arrived a few years later. Panel and crossbar
switches followed, then in 1965 the stored-program electronic switch (Chapter~\ref{ch:wireline} tells
the story), and in 1963 the Bell System introduced Touch-Tone dialling (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_dtmf}), which signals each digit as
the sum of two tones, one from a low group (697, 770, 852, 941\,Hz) and one from a high group (1209,
1336, 1477\,Hz). The frequencies were chosen so that no tone is a harmonic of another and speech
rarely produces a valid pair; detecting them is a classic exercise in digital filtering
(Chapter~\ref{ch:dsp}). Next time an automated phone menu asks you to ``press 1'', remember that your
phone is playing a two-note chord that a Goertzel filter in some distant computer is listening for.

The telephone network became the largest machine on Earth, and its growth drove the development of
switching theory, of the theory of relay circuits---Claude Shannon's 1937 master's thesis showed that
Boolean algebra could design them, and so founded digital circuit design---and of \emph{traffic
theory}.

\subsection{How many lines? Erlang's formula}

\chOneFigH{ch01_erlang_curves}{Erlang's formula at work. Left: blocking probability against the number
of trunks for three traffic loads; the dashed line is the traditional 1\% grade of service. Right:
the average utilisation of a trunk group designed for 1\% blocking. A small group must sit mostly idle
to absorb random peaks; a large one can run more than 80\% full.}

Here is a question every telephone company had to answer, and every cellular operator still does: a
village wants to call the town next door; how many lines should you build between them? Build one
line per subscriber and you waste a fortune, because most people are not on the phone most of the
time. Build too few and callers hear the dreaded busy signal. The Danish mathematician A.~K.~Erlang
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_erlang}), working for the Copenhagen Telephone Company, cracked the problem. He
showed in 1909 that calls arrive approximately as a Poisson process---independently, at random, at
some average rate---and in 1917 derived the probability that a call finds all $N$ circuits busy when
the offered traffic is $A$ erlangs (the average number of calls in progress),
\begin{equation}
B(A,N) = \frac{A^N/N!}{\sum_{k=0}^{N} A^k/k!}.
\label{eq:ch01:erlangb}
\end{equation}
The \term{Erlang B formula} still sizes trunk groups, cellular base stations and call-centre staffing
(Chapter~\ref{ch:multipleaccess}). Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_erlang_curves} shows its two most important
lessons: you always need more circuits than the average load, and big groups are far more efficient
than small ones.


\begin{worked}[How many trunks does a village need?]
In the busiest hour a village's subscribers make 100 calls to the neighbouring town, each lasting
three minutes on average. The offered traffic is $A = 100\times3/60 = 5$ erlangs: on average five
circuits are busy. With exactly five trunks the network would block a large fraction of calls,
because calls arrive at random. The recursion $B(A,0)=1$,
$B(A,n)=A\,B(A,n-1)/[n+A\,B(A,n-1)]$ (a numerically stable form of~\eqref{eq:ch01:erlangb}) gives
$B(5,8)\approx7.0\%$, $B(5,10)\approx1.8\%$ and $B(5,11)\approx0.83\%$. To meet the traditional
1\% grade of service the village needs 11 trunks, more than twice the average load. Large trunk
groups are more efficient: 100 erlangs at 1\% blocking need about 117 circuits, only 17\% above
the average. This \emph{trunking efficiency} of large groups is one reason networks concentrate traffic
in hierarchies, and one reason a cellular operator prefers wide carriers shared by many users to
many narrow ones.
\end{worked}

\subsection{Amplification and the long-distance problem}
\label{sec:ch01:repeaters}

A telephone signal cannot be relayed like a telegraph signal: there is nothing to decide, only a
waveform to preserve. Long-distance telephony was therefore a battle against attenuation, fought
first with sheer copper. The New York--Chicago line of 1892 (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_bell_1892}) used
open-wire pairs of very heavy copper, about 4\,mm in diameter, on poles; loading coils, from 1900,
roughly doubled the reach of both open wire and cable. By about 1911 loaded heavy open wire carried
speech from New York to Denver, perhaps 3000\,km, at the limit of intelligibility.

\chOnePairH{ch01_bell_1892}{Alexander Graham Bell opens the New York--Chicago line in 1892. Heavy
copper and, later, loading coils stretched voice circuits to about 1500\,km and beyond---but not across a
continent.}{ch01_deforest}{Lee de Forest with Audion tubes. His three-electrode Audion could amplify, but
erratically; AT\&T's engineers turned it into a dependable high-vacuum triode.}

Beyond that the signal simply became too weak, and no practical amount of copper would help. The
attenuation in decibels grows linearly with distance, so a coast-to-coast line would lose roughly
twice as many decibels as the Denver line: the received power would be the \emph{square} of an
already tiny fraction. AT\&T had publicly promised a transcontinental service for the 1915
Panama--Pacific Exposition in San Francisco, and it needed a miracle.


The miracle was the \term{repeater}, an amplifier inserted into the line, and the device that made it
possible was the vacuum tube. John Ambrose Fleming's two-electrode valve (1904) could rectify but not
amplify. Lee de Forest's three-electrode \emph{Audion} (1906; Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_deforest}) added a
grid between filament and plate, and could amplify, but only erratically: de Forest's tubes contained
residual gas, and he did not fully understand how they worked. In 1912--13 engineers at AT\&T, led by
Harold Arnold, and independently Irving Langmuir at General Electric, showed that a
\emph{high-vacuum} triode behaved predictably and could be manufactured to specification. AT\&T's
engineers built repeaters from them, and on 25 January 1915 the first transcontinental telephone call
connected Bell in New York with Watson in San Francisco, over some 5400\,km of loaded open wire.
Thirty-nine years after summoning Watson from the next room, Bell summoned him from across a
continent.

\mdcfig{ch01_level_diagram}{A level diagram of the transcontinental line (illustrative numbers from
the worked example). Without repeaters the signal sinks 81\,dB, deep into the noise and induced
interference. With four repeaters spread along the route, it is lifted before it sinks, and arrives
25\,dB down---loud enough for a conversation.}

\begin{worked}[A gain budget for the transcontinental line]
The numbers here are illustrative but representative. Suppose loaded heavy open wire has an
attenuation of about 0.015\,dB/km at 1\,kHz in good weather. Over 5400\,km the line loses
\[
0.015\times5400 \approx 81\ \mathrm{dB},
\]
a power ratio of about $10^{-8}$. If the overall loss from talker to listener must not exceed about
25\,dB for satisfactory conversation, the repeaters must supply about $81-25=56$\,dB of gain in
total.

Why not put all of it at one end? At the sending end, 56\,dB of gain would require an output power
far beyond what tubes, wires and insulators could tolerate, and crosstalk into neighbouring pairs
would be intolerable. At the receiving end, the signal would already have fallen to a level
comparable with the noise and induced interference on the line, and amplifying it would amplify
the noise equally. Gain must be \emph{distributed}, so that the signal is lifted before it sinks
into the noise (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_level_diagram}).

How much gain per repeater? The early repeaters were \emph{two-wire} repeaters: one amplifier
for each direction, joined to the line through hybrids. Any imbalance between the hybrid and the
line lets some of each amplifier's output leak into the other amplifier's input, forming a loop.
If the gain around that loop exceeds the loss through the two hybrids, the circuit oscillates
(``sings''). With the hybrid balance achievable against a real line---a return loss of perhaps
10--15\,dB at each end of the loop---each repeater could safely provide perhaps 15--20\,dB.
Dividing 56\,dB by 15--20\,dB gives three or four repeaters, the same order as the handful used
on the first transcontinental circuit.

The same arithmetic, with noise, echo and stability margins, sized every analog long-haul system
that followed, and it is the ancestor of the link budgets of
Chapters~\ref{ch:noise},~\ref{ch:channels} and~\ref{ch:satellite}.
\end{worked}

\begin{inpractice}[Where the decibel came from]
Telephone engineers needed a unit for loss, and their first one was physical: the \emph{mile of
standard cable} (MSC), the attenuation of one mile of a particular reference cable at a frequency
of about 800\,Hz, equivalent to about 1.06 of today's decibels. A circuit had ``a loss of 20 miles''.
Because the unit depended on a specific cable and frequency, in 1923--24 the Bell System replaced
it by the \emph{transmission unit} (TU), defined as ten times the common logarithm of a power ratio,
chosen to be numerically close to the MSC. In 1928 the TU was renamed the \term{decibel}, a tenth of
a ``bel'' in honour of Alexander Graham Bell, and international bodies adopted it shortly after. The
decibel survives because attenuation along a line multiplies, and logarithms turn the
multiplication into the addition of a link budget. The neper (from the natural logarithm, used in
Equation~\eqref{eq:ch01:gamma}) was its European rival; 1\,Np $=8.686$\,dB.
\end{inpractice}

\subsection{Black's negative-feedback amplifier}
\label{sec:ch01:feedback}

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering

Amplifiers solved one problem and created another. A tube's gain drifts with temperature, filament
voltage and age, and a tube amplifier is not perfectly linear: it adds harmonic and intermodulation
distortion. A circuit with dozens of repeaters in tandem accumulates every drift and every distortion
product, and when many telephone channels share one amplifier (Section~\ref{sec:ch01:carrier}), the
intermodulation between them appears as unintelligible crosstalk---other people's conversations
bleeding faintly into yours. By the 1920s this was the limiting problem of long-distance transmission.

Harold S.~Black, a young engineer at the Western Electric laboratory that became Bell Telephone
Laboratories in 1925, had been working on it for years. In August 1927, by his own account while
crossing the Hudson on the Lackawanna ferry, he sketched the solution on a page of the \emph{New York
Times}: the \term{negative-feedback amplifier}. Take an amplifier of large but unreliable gain $A$,
and feed a fraction $\beta$ of its output, inverted, back to its input
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_feedback}). The amplifier now amplifies only the \emph{difference} between what
you asked for and what it is actually producing, so it continually corrects its own mistakes. The
output is $y = A(x-\beta y)$, so the closed-loop gain is
\begin{equation}
G = \frac{y}{x} = \frac{A}{1+A\beta}\;\xrightarrow{A\beta\gg1}\;\frac{1}{\beta}.
\label{eq:ch01:feedback}
\end{equation}
In other words: when the \emph{loop gain} $A\beta$ is large, the overall gain depends almost entirely
on $\beta$---a stable network of passive resistors---and hardly at all on the drifting tube.



\begin{analogy}[Steering by watching the road]
A driver with sloppy steering still keeps the car in its lane, because she watches the road and
corrects continuously; the quality of the steering box hardly matters as long as she corrects
quickly and strongly enough. Take away her eyes (no feedback) and every play in the linkage puts the
car in the ditch. In Black's amplifier the tube is the sloppy steering, the resistor network is the
driver's eyes, and the loop gain is how hard she corrects. The limit, as any learner driver knows:
over-correct with a delay and you weave from side to side. That is oscillation, and it is why
Nyquist's stability criterion followed so quickly.
\end{analogy}

\mdcfig[0.5]{ch01_feedback_gain}{Closed-loop gain against open-loop gain with $\beta=0.01$. Once
$A\gg100$, large changes in $A$ (shaded) barely move the closed-loop gain.}
How much does feedback help? Differentiating Equation~\eqref{eq:ch01:feedback} (the box below does
it) shows that fractional changes of the tube's gain are \emph{divided by} $1+A\beta$ before they
reach the output, and distortion generated inside the amplifier is suppressed by the same factor.
Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_feedback_gain} shows the flattening: past a certain point, the closed-loop gain
simply stops caring what the tube does. The price is gain: the amplifier must have $1+A\beta$ times
more gain than is finally used. Black's insight was that, thanks to the vacuum tube, gain had become
cheap and linearity had not---so trade the cheap thing for the expensive one. It is the same move
Armstrong would make with bandwidth and noise a few years later, and that coding theorists make today
with computation and power: whenever you hold a resource in abundance, look for a way to spend it on
the one you lack. Engineers keep rediscovering this principle because it keeps working.

\begin{deeper}[The sensitivity of a feedback amplifier]
Differentiate $G=A/(1+A\beta)$ with respect to $A$:
$dG/dA = 1/(1+A\beta)^2$. Dividing by $G$,
\begin{equation}
\frac{dG}{G} = \frac{1}{1+A\beta}\,\frac{dA}{A}.
\label{eq:ch01:sensitivity}
\end{equation}
A distortion product $d$ generated at the amplifier's output sees the same loop: the output becomes
$y = A(x-\beta y) + d$, so the distortion term appears as $d/(1+A\beta)$. Both drift and distortion
are reduced by the \emph{return difference} $1+A\beta$.
\end{deeper}

\begin{worked}[What feedback buys]
An amplifier has open-loop gain $A=10^4$ (80\,dB) and is wrapped in feedback with $\beta=0.01$.
The loop gain is $A\beta=100$ (40\,dB), and the closed-loop gain is $10^4/101\approx99.0$ (about
40\,dB). Now let the tubes age so that $A$ falls by 10\%, to 9000. The closed-loop gain becomes
$9000/91\approx98.9$: a change of 0.11\%, ninety-one times smaller than the 10\% change in the tube,
as~\eqref{eq:ch01:sensitivity} predicts. Third-order distortion generated inside the amplifier is
likewise reduced by about 40\,dB. In a carrier system with fifty repeaters in tandem, each
contributing intermodulation, that 40\,dB was the difference between a working multi-channel system
and an unusable one (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_intermod}). The cost was 40\,dB of gain that had to be
built and then thrown away.
\end{worked}

\mdcfig{ch01_intermod}{What feedback does to distortion (simulation). Two test tones pass through an
amplifier with a small cubic nonlinearity. Open loop (left), intermodulation products appear beside the
tones at $2f_1-f_2$ and $2f_2-f_1$---in an FDM system, right on top of other people's channels. With a
loop gain of 100 (right), the same products are some 40\,dB lower.}

Black's patent took nine years to issue; the Patent Office, it is said, treated the claim that an
amplifier could be improved by throwing away most of its gain as it would a perpetual-motion machine.
The new amplifier raised a new question: a loop with high gain can oscillate, as the two-wire
repeaters had shown. Harry Nyquist's 1932 stability criterion and Hendrik Bode's work on gain and
phase margins in the 1930s and 1940s answered it, and in doing so founded classical control theory.
Negative feedback is now so universal---in operational amplifiers, voltage regulators, power-amplifier
linearisers, phase-locked loops and automatic gain control---that it is hard to imagine electronics
without it.

\subsection{Carrier telephony: frequency-division multiplexing}
\label{sec:ch01:carrier}

Once a wire could carry frequencies far above the voice band, and amplifiers could boost them, it
was natural to put many conversations on it at once, each shifted to its own frequency slot---just as
many radio stations share the air by sitting at different places on the dial. This is
\term{frequency-division multiplexing} (FDM). It needed three inventions.

The first was the \term{electric wave filter}, developed by George Campbell at AT\&T and Karl Willy
Wagner in Germany around 1915, which could pass one band of frequencies and reject its neighbours;
Otto Zobel's image-parameter design methods of the 1920s turned filter design into engineering. The
second was \term{single-sideband} (SSB) modulation, for which John Carson of AT\&T filed a patent in
1915: multiplying a voice signal by a carrier produces two mirror-image sidebands, each carrying the
whole message, and by transmitting only one of them (and suppressing the carrier) each voice channel
occupies only its own 4\,kHz (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_ssb}). The third was the stable, linear
amplifier---Black's.

\mdcfig{ch01_ssb}{Single-sideband modulation in three steps. A voice signal (top; a real signal's
spectrum has a mirror image at negative frequencies) is multiplied by a carrier, which copies the
spectrum to both sides of the carrier frequency (middle). A filter keeps one sideband (bottom), so each
conversation occupies a single 4\,kHz slot. The lower sideband is inverted: high audio frequencies end
up at the low edge.}

The first commercial carrier system entered service in 1918, adding a few channels above the voice
band of an open-wire pair. Through the 1920s and 1930s open-wire and cable carrier systems multiplied
the capacity of the long-distance plant, and in 1941 the first \emph{coaxial} carrier system, L1,
opened between Minneapolis and Stevens Point, Wisconsin, carrying 480 telephone channels on a pair of
coaxial tubes (it was later expanded to 600). Its successors stacked ever more: L3 (1953) about 1860
channels per tube, L4 (1967) 3600, and L5 (1974) 10\,800 voice channels on each coaxial tube, with
repeaters every mile or so.

\begin{analogy}[An apartment block for conversations]
Think of the FDM hierarchy as housing. Twelve voice channels share a floor (a \emph{group}); five
floors make a building (a \emph{supergroup}); a city block of buildings is a \emph{mastergroup}. Each
level is built in exactly the same way from the level below---modulate, filter, stack---so a handful
of standard prefabricated modules can build any size of city. The limit of the analogy is that the
tenants are not quite soundproofed: every amplifier's nonlinearity leaks a little of each floor into
the others, which is why Black's amplifier had to come first.
\end{analogy}

The channels were organised into a standard hierarchy, agreed internationally through the CCITT so
that equipment from different administrations could interconnect (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_fdm}). Twelve
voice channels, each translated by SSB modulation to a 4\,kHz slot, formed a \emph{group} occupying
60--108\,kHz; five groups formed a 60-channel \emph{supergroup} at 312--552\,kHz; and supergroups were
combined into a \emph{mastergroup}---five supergroups (300 channels, 812--2044\,kHz) in the CCITT plan,
ten (600 channels, 564--3084\,kHz) in the Bell System's L600 plan. Larger systems stacked mastergroups
into ``jumbogroups''. Every level was built by the same operation---modulate each block onto a carrier,
select one sideband, add---so a handful of standard modules could build any capacity.
Chapter~\ref{ch:analog} develops SSB and this hierarchy from first principles, and
Chapter~\ref{ch:ofdm} shows how OFDM does the same thing with one FFT.

\chOneFigH{ch01_fdm}{Top: the CCITT basic group. Each of twelve voice channels (300--3400\,Hz) is
translated by single-sideband modulation to the lower sideband of a carrier at 64, 68, \ldots,
108\,kHz, so channel 1 occupies the top slot. The sloping shapes show that the lower sideband is
inverted: high audio frequencies lie at the low edge of each slot. Bottom: the first three levels of
the FDM hierarchy, each built from five or ten blocks of the level below.}

\begin{keyidea}[What the telephone era bequeathed: linearity and the decibel]
The telegraph needed only detection; the telephone needed fidelity, and fidelity forced engineers
to think in terms of linear systems: frequency response, impedance, loss and gain in decibels,
noise and distortion budgets, and the stability of feedback loops. Every concept of
Chapters~\ref{ch:signals} and~\ref{ch:noise} was developed, or first applied on a large scale, in the
telephone plant between 1900 and 1940. The carrier hierarchy added a second legacy: complex systems
built from a few standard, interchangeable modules connected by agreed interfaces.
\end{keyidea}

\subsection{Across the ocean by voice}

Telephony across the Atlantic came first by radio. On 7 January 1927 AT\&T and the British Post
Office opened a commercial transatlantic radiotelephone service using single-sideband transmission on
long waves; a three-minute call between New York and London cost \$75, a substantial sum. Shortwave
links soon added capacity, but radio circuits were subject to fading, noise and the ionospheric
disturbances of Section~\ref{sec:ch01:shortwave}, and they could carry only a handful of calls.

A telephone cable across the ocean needed amplifiers on the sea bed, and amplifiers on the sea bed
had to work for twenty years without maintenance---there is no service call at a depth of 4\,km.
TAT-1, opened on 25 September 1956 between Oban in Scotland and Clarenville, Newfoundland, used two
cables, one for each direction, with dozens of submerged repeaters built around specially
manufactured long-life vacuum tubes. It carried 36 telephone circuits at first.

Around 1960 a remarkable invention increased its effective capacity. \term{Time-assignment speech
interpolation} (TASI) exploited the fact that in a conversation each direction is silent more than
half the time---you listen, you pause, you breathe---and assigned a circuit to a talker only while he
or she was actually speaking. With enough simultaneous conversations the statistics allowed roughly
twice as many calls as circuits (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_tasi}). TASI was statistical
multiplexing---the principle that makes packet switching efficient---applied to analog speech.

\mdcfig{ch01_tasi}{Why TASI worked (illustrative simulation). Left: talkspurts of twelve speakers;
each direction of a conversation is active well under half the time. Right: of 36 one-way speech
channels, rarely more than about 20 are active at once, so a shared pool of circuits can carry far
more calls than it has circuits.}

\begin{analogy}[Seats on a commuter train]
A railway does not build one seat per resident of the suburbs; it builds enough seats for the people
who are travelling at once, and most residents are at home or at work at any given moment. TASI did
the same for the silences in conversation. The analogy also shows the risk: on the rare morning when
everyone travels at once, someone stands---and in TASI, the first syllable of a talkspurt that found
no free circuit was clipped.
\end{analogy}

\subsection{Microwave radio relay}

The other long-haul medium of the mid-century was microwave radio. The wartime development of
microwave tubes, waveguides and antennas (Section~\ref{sec:ch01:radar}) made it possible to send
wideband FDM signals and television between towers on hilltops, each within line of sight of the next.
The Bell System's TD-2 system opened between New York and Chicago in 1950 and coast to coast in 1951,
using the 3.7--4.2\,GHz band and relay stations spaced roughly 40--50\,km apart. Its first
transcontinental showpiece, in September 1951, was the first live coast-to-coast television broadcast.

\mdcsidephoto[0.32]{ch01_hogg_horns}{Horn-reflector antennas of a microwave relay route on a tower in
Seattle. Each ``sugar scoop'' horn beams its signal to the next tower, typically 40--50\,km away.}
Each radio channel carried hundreds of telephone circuits, frequency-modulated as a single FDM
baseband, or one television programme, and by the 1970s microwave relay carried most of North
America's long-distance traffic. The towers, with their distinctive horn-reflector antennas
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_hogg_horns}), became a familiar sight along highways and on mountain tops. The
4\,GHz band is worth remembering: when commercial satellites arrived in the 1960s, they were given the
same band for their downlinks (the ``C band'' of Chapter~\ref{ch:satellite}), because the technology
already existed---and satellite earth stations have had to coordinate with terrestrial microwave links
ever since. The horn-reflector antenna itself was chosen because it is unusually quiet: it hardly
looks at the warm ground at all. That property, as the next story shows, won somebody a Nobel Prize.

\mdcphoto[0.66]{ch01_holmdel}{The Holmdel horn antenna in New Jersey today, built by Bell Labs for
satellite communication experiments. Its exceptionally low noise let Penzias and Wilson detect the
cosmic microwave background in 1964.}

\begin{history}[The noise that won a Nobel Prize]
In 1959 Bell Labs built a large horn-reflector antenna at Holmdel, New Jersey, to receive signals
reflected from the Echo balloon satellite, and later used it with Telstar. The horn-reflector design
had been developed for microwave relay because it has very low sidelobes and so picks up very little
thermal radiation from the warm ground. In 1964 two radio astronomers, Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson,
began using the antenna and its exceptionally quiet receiver for radio astronomy, and found that they
could not account for about 3.5\,K of excess antenna noise temperature, the same in every direction,
at every time of day and season. They checked every component and, famously, evicted a pair of
pigeons and cleaned out their droppings. The noise remained. It was the cosmic microwave background,
the afterglow of the Big Bang, and Penzias and Wilson shared the 1978 Nobel Prize in Physics for it.
The story is a lesson in noise budgets: only an engineer who has accounted for every kelvin can
recognise the one that does not belong (Chapter~\ref{ch:noise}).
\end{history}

\subsection{From Maxwell to Hertz}

In 1865 James Clerk Maxwell published ``A Dynamical Theory of the Electromagnetic Field'', the paper
in which the equations that unify electricity, magnetism and light first appeared together. Hidden in
them was a startling prediction: electromagnetic waves, travelling through empty space at the speed of
light---indeed, light itself was one of them. The prediction was not widely accepted until Heinrich
Hertz, at Karlsruhe between 1886 and 1888, generated and detected such waves in his laboratory.

\chOneFigH[0.50]{ch01_hertz}{One of Hertz's experimental arrangements, from his own account
of the experiments: an induction coil drives a spark-gap oscillator with large plates, and loops of
wire with tiny gaps detect the waves it radiates.}

His transmitter was a dipole with a spark gap at its centre, driven by an induction coil
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_hertz}); each spark set the dipole ringing at its resonant frequency, of the
order of a hundred megahertz in his best-known experiments. His receiver was a loop of wire with a
tiny gap, in which a faint spark appeared when the waves arrived---Hertz often had to work in a
darkened room to see it. With this humble equipment he measured standing waves, showed that the waves
could be reflected, refracted, focused by parabolic mirrors and polarised, and measured their
wavelength and speed. Every antenna, every radar dish and every Wi-Fi router is a descendant of that
spark gap and that loop. Asked what use his discovery might have, Hertz is often reported to have
thought it had none, and he died in 1894, aged 36, before anyone had proved him wrong. Within seven
years of his death, a young Italian would send a signal across the Atlantic with waves Hertz had
first made in a lecture room.

\subsection{Detectors: coherers and crystals}

The weak point of early wireless was the receiver. A spark in a gap is a very insensitive detector.
In 1890 Edouard Branly found that a tube of loose metal filings, normally of high resistance, became
conducting when an electromagnetic wave struck it, and stayed conducting until tapped. Oliver Lodge
improved the device, named it the \term{coherer}, added an automatic tapper, and in a lecture at
Oxford in August 1894 demonstrated the reception of signals in Morse-like code from a nearby building.

\chOnePair{ch01_coherer}{A Marconi coherer tube (drawing of 1903): a pinch of metal filings between two
metal plugs in a small glass tube. A radio wave makes the filings cling together and conduct;
a tap resets them.}{ch01_catwhisker}{A ``cat's whisker'' crystal detector: a fine wire touching a
crystal of galena forms a point-contact rectifier---the first semiconductor device in mass use.}

In Calcutta, Jagadish Chandra Bose experimented with wavelengths of a few millimetres---frequencies of
tens of gigahertz, the band of today's 5G millimetre-wave radios---and devised improved detectors,
including one of the first semiconductor detectors, a point contact on galena, patented in the United
States in 1904. In Russia, Alexander Popov built a coherer receiver in 1895 to record atmospheric
discharges and demonstrated it in signalling experiments.

The coherer was a binary threshold device, and a slow one: it needed tapping after every dot
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_coherer}). The \term{crystal detector}---a fine wire (the ``cat's whisker'')
touching a crystal of galena, silicon or carborundum, patented in several forms in 1904--06
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_catwhisker})---was a true rectifier, and with headphones it could hear not just
the presence of a signal but its modulation. Crystal sets became the cheap receivers of the early
broadcasting era, and the point-contact rectifier was rediscovered in the 1940s as the silicon
microwave mixer diode of radar---and, at Bell Labs, as the starting point of the transistor.

\subsection{Marconi and the spark era}

Guglielmo Marconi was neither the first to send a wireless signal nor a physicist, but he was the
first to treat wireless as a system to be engineered and sold. Beginning in 1895 at his family's
estate near Bologna, he found that the range of spark-gap systems grew dramatically when the antenna
was raised high and the other side of the spark gap was connected to earth---a monopole over a ground
plane. He moved to England in 1896, where the Post Office's chief engineer, William Preece, supported
his trials, and founded his company in 1897 (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_marconi}). He signalled across the
English Channel in 1899. Then he set out to do what most experts said was impossible.

\chOnePair{ch01_marconi}{Guglielmo Marconi in 1897 with the spark transmitter and coherer
receiver he brought to England.}{ch01_poldhu}{Marconi's transmitting station at Poldhu, Cornwall, in about 1902, with
its great ring of masts supporting the antenna. Its spark signals were the first to cross the
Atlantic.}

On 12 December 1901 Marconi reported receiving the letter S in Morse code---three faint clicks---at
Signal Hill, Newfoundland, on an antenna held aloft by a kite, sent from a powerful spark station at
Poldhu in Cornwall (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_poldhu}), across 3500\,km of ocean. Many scientists doubted
the claim, because the Earth's curvature should have blocked the signal and the reception was
marginal; the explanation, unknown to Marconi, was reflection from the ionised upper atmosphere
(Section~\ref{sec:ch01:shortwave}). Within a few years his company was operating regular
transatlantic wireless telegraph service.



Spark transmitters were extremely wasteful of spectrum. A spark discharge shock-excites the antenna
circuit, which rings as a \emph{damped} sinusoid, like a bell struck with a hammer; each wave train
decays within a few tens of cycles and the next spark starts another. A damped oscillation has a wide
spectrum. If the amplitude decays by a factor $e^{-\delta}$ per cycle (the \term{logarithmic
decrement} $\delta$), the power spectrum is a Lorentzian of half-power bandwidth
\begin{equation}
B_{3\,\mathrm{dB}} \approx \frac{\delta f_0}{\pi},
\label{eq:ch01:spark}
\end{equation}
and its skirts fall only at 6\,dB per octave. In words: the faster the ringing dies away, the wider
the band of frequencies it splatters over. Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_spark} compares a spark and a
continuous wave at 500\,kHz.

\mdcfig{ch01_spark}{Left: a spark transmitter's damped wave trains (logarithmic decrement 0.2 per
cycle) and a continuous wave, both at 500\,kHz, the international maritime calling and distress
frequency of the wireless-telegraph era. Right: their spectra. The spark occupies tens of kilohertz
and has slowly decaying skirts; the continuous wave occupies a bandwidth set only by its keying. Spark
transmitters were progressively outlawed from the 1920s onward for this reason.}

\begin{worked}[How many spark stations fit in the band?]
A ship's spark transmitter at $f_0=500$\,kHz with decrement $\delta=0.2$ has a half-power
bandwidth of $0.2\times500/\pi\approx32$\,kHz, and its spectrum is still only about 20\,dB down
160\,kHz away from the carrier. The whole medium-frequency band used for marine telegraphy, a few hundred
kilohertz wide, could accommodate only a handful of such stations without mutual interference.

A continuous-wave (CW) transmitter keyed at 20\,words per minute has elements of $T_u=60$\,ms; with
shaped keying (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_morse}) its spectrum is essentially confined to a band of the order
of $5/T_u\approx 80$\,Hz. In the same 32\,kHz the CW transmitter could share the band with roughly four
hundred others. Moving from spark to CW multiplied the capacity of the radio spectrum by more than two
orders of magnitude, with no change in data rate. The United States' Radio Act of 1912 began the
process by limiting the decrement of licensed transmitters, and international regulations eventually
banned spark transmitters altogether.
\end{worked}

The early 1900s were spent on two remedies: \emph{tuning}, and \emph{continuous waves}. Lodge's
``syntonic'' patent of 1897 and Marconi's famous patent No.~7777 of 1900 used tuned circuits at
transmitter and receiver to select one station's frequency, and Karl Ferdinand Braun's coupled
circuits separated the spark gap from the antenna so that the antenna rang longer and more purely;
Marconi and Braun shared the 1909 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Continuous waves came from three directions. Valdemar Poulsen's arc converter (1903) used the negative
resistance of an electric arc to sustain oscillation. Ernst Alexanderson at General Electric built
enormous high-frequency alternators---rotating machines whose rotor carried hundreds of poles and spun
at thousands of revolutions per minute---that generated tens to hundreds of kilowatts directly at very
low frequencies. Think of a bicycle dynamo scaled up to the size of a locomotive and spun so fast
that its output is not 50\,Hz but tens of kilohertz. One of them, the 200\,kW alternator at Grimeton in
Sweden (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_grimeton}), built in the early 1920s, is preserved in working order as a
UNESCO World Heritage Site and still transmits on 17.2\,kHz on special occasions. The third source,
the vacuum-tube oscillator, eventually displaced the others: it was smaller, cheaper, could be tuned
to any frequency, and needed no machine shop.

\mdcphoto[0.62]{ch01_titanic_radio}{The Marconi wireless room of the \emph{Titanic}, with operator Harold
Bride at the desk---the only known photograph of it (the image is a double exposure). From this room
the distress calls of April 1912 went out.}

\chOnePair{ch01_grimeton}{The Alexanderson alternator at Grimeton, Sweden: a rotating machine that

\begin{inpractice}[500 kHz, SOS and the Titanic]
For most of the twentieth century every ship's radio operator listened on 500\,kHz, the international
calling and distress frequency of the wireless-telegraph era. The distress signal SOS was adopted at
the Berlin radiotelegraph conference of 1906 (and in force from 1908) because its Morse pattern, three
dots, three dashes, three dots, is unmistakable and easy to send; the letters stand for nothing.
When the \emph{Titanic} struck an iceberg in April 1912, the nearest ship, the \emph{Californian}, had a
single wireless operator, who had gone to bed; the \emph{Carpathia}, further away, heard the
distress calls and rescued the survivors. Within months the United States' Radio Act of 1912 and the
London International Radiotelegraph Convention of the same year required continuous radio watch on
passenger ships, licensed operators and stations, and began the international management of the
radio spectrum. Morse telegraphy on 500\,kHz was finally replaced in 1999 by the satellite and digital
selective-calling systems of the Global Maritime Distress and Safety System.
\end{inpractice}


\subsection{The vacuum tube, the heterodyne and voice by radio}

Reginald Fessenden (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_fessenden}), a Canadian engineer who had worked for Edison,
understood earlier than most that radio should be a continuous wave, and that a continuous wave could
be \emph{modulated} by voice. He also invented, around 1901, the \term{heterodyne} principle: mix the
incoming radio frequency with a locally generated wave a little different in frequency, and the
difference frequency falls in the audio range, where a CW telegraph signal becomes an audible tone.
Every CW receiver's ``beat frequency oscillator'' and every superheterodyne mixer descends from it.
On Christmas Eve 1906, using an early Alexanderson alternator at Brant Rock, Massachusetts, Fessenden
reportedly broadcast speech and music to ships at sea; historians have debated the details, but by
1906 the idea of amplitude-modulated voice transmission was established. Imagine being a ship's
wireless operator, used to nothing but the crackle of Morse in your headphones, suddenly hearing a
human voice and a violin out of the static. Even if the details are legend, that moment came to
somebody in those years, and broadcasting was born from it.

\begin{analogy}[Two piano strings, slightly out of tune]
Strike two piano strings tuned a few hertz apart and you hear a slow ``wah-wah-wah'': the beat. Its
rate is the \emph{difference} of the two frequencies. Fessenden's heterodyne does this
electrically. A 500\,kHz radio wave is far too high to hear, but mix it with a local 501\,kHz
oscillator and out comes a 1\,kHz beat, right in the middle of the audio band---and it switches on
and off exactly as the distant Morse key does. The analogy's limit: the mixer also produces the
\emph{sum} frequency, and a second, unwanted input (the ``image'') that beats to the same difference,
both of which a real receiver must filter away (Chapter~\ref{ch:analog}).
\end{analogy}

What made radio practical was the vacuum tube, in three roles. As an \emph{amplifier}, de Forest's
Audion and its high-vacuum successors gave receivers sensitivity no crystal could match. As an
\emph{oscillator}, a tube with positive feedback produced clean continuous waves at any frequency, at
first at low power and later at hundreds of kilowatts. As a \emph{modulator}, it impressed speech on
the carrier. By the end of the First World War, the tube had made the spark, the arc and the
alternator obsolete, and the field was ready for broadcasting.

They rectify, but cannot amplify.}{ch01_audion}{A de Forest Audion detector of the 1910s, its tube mounted on
the front of the box. The third electrode, the grid, lets a small voltage control a large current.}

\subsection{Armstrong's three inventions}

The receivers of the early tube era were poor, and three inventions by Edwin Howard Armstrong
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_armstrong}) fixed them. The first, the \term{regenerative receiver} (1912,
patented 1914), fed part of a tube amplifier's output back to its input with positive feedback,
raising the gain and selectivity of a single tube enormously; pushed past the threshold of
oscillation, the same circuit became a CW oscillator. Lee de Forest claimed the invention too, and
after twenty years of litigation the US Supreme Court decided in de Forest's favour in 1934---a
judgment most engineers of the time regarded as wrong. The second, the \term{superheterodyne
receiver} (1918, devised while Armstrong was serving with the US Army Signal Corps in Paris; Lucien
L\'evy in France and Walter Schottky in Germany had related ideas), mixed every incoming signal down to
a fixed \emph{intermediate frequency} (IF), where selective, high-gain amplifiers could be built once
and optimised (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_superhet}). Nearly every radio built since---including the
software-defined radio in your lab, which is a superheterodyne whose IF happens to be zero---follows
this architecture. The third, wideband \emph{frequency modulation} (FM, patented 1933), is the
subject of Section~\ref{sec:ch01:fm}.

\begin{keyidea}[The superheterodyne principle]
It is hard to build a filter that is both very selective and tunable over a wide range. It is easy to
build a very selective filter at one fixed frequency. The superhet therefore moves the signal to the
filter rather than moving the filter to the signal, using a mixer and a tunable local oscillator.
Chapter~\ref{ch:analog} derives the image-frequency problem that this creates; Chapter~\ref{ch:sdr}
shows how modern zero-IF and low-IF receivers solve it digitally, and Chapter~\ref{ch:dsp} shows that
a digital down-converter is a superheterodyne built from multipliers and decimating filters.
\end{keyidea}

\subsection{The broadcasting era}

Broadcasting was an accident of amateur enthusiasm and commercial opportunity. Radio amateurs had been
transmitting voice and music to one another for years when, in 1920, the Westinghouse company, looking
for a market for its receivers, licensed the station KDKA in Pittsburgh. Its broadcast of the
Harding--Cox presidential election returns on 2 November 1920 is usually taken as the start of
scheduled commercial broadcasting.

Within three years there were hundreds of American stations, the British Broadcasting Company had
begun service (1922; it became the publicly chartered British Broadcasting Corporation in 1927), and
the sale of receivers had become a major industry. Families gathered around the set in the evening the
way later generations gathered around the television and then scattered to their own screens. The
broadcasting boom forced the first serious spectrum management. American stations initially crowded
onto a few frequencies and interfered chaotically; the Radio Act of 1927 created the Federal Radio
Commission to assign frequencies, powers and hours, and the Communications Act of 1934 replaced it
with the Federal Communications Commission. The medium-wave AM band settled at roughly
535--1605\,kHz (later extended to 1705\,kHz) with channels spaced 10\,kHz apart in the Americas and
9\,kHz elsewhere---a channel spacing that, as Chapter~\ref{ch:analog} explains, limits AM audio to
about 5\,kHz of bandwidth.

The idea that the spectrum is a public resource, allocated by a regulator to services and licensed to
users, dates from these years; Chapters~\ref{ch:cellular} and~\ref{ch:wifi} show how its alternatives,
spectrum auctions and unlicensed bands, emerged sixty years later.

\subsection{Short waves and the ionosphere}
\label{sec:ch01:shortwave}

The professional consensus of the 1910s was that long-distance radio needed long waves---tens of
kilometres of wavelength, enormous antennas and hundreds of kilowatts---because ground waves at low
frequencies follow the Earth's curvature. Amateurs, restricted by regulation to the supposedly useless
wavelengths of 200\,m and below (frequencies above 1.5\,MHz), discovered otherwise. In 1921 American
amateur signals on 200\,m were heard in Scotland, and in November 1923 amateurs in France and the United
States made the first two-way transatlantic contact on about 100\,m, with a few hundred watts. Short
waves travelled thousands of kilometres by reflection from the ionised layers of the upper atmosphere
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_skywave}). The experts had given the amateurs the ``worthless'' band, and the
amateurs had found the most valuable long-distance channel of the century in it.

\chOneFigH{ch01_skywave}{How short waves cross oceans (heights greatly exaggerated). The ground wave
dies out within tens of kilometres at high frequencies, but a sky wave reflected by the ionosphere
returns to Earth hundreds or thousands of kilometres away, and can hop again off the ground. Between
the end of the ground wave and the first hop lies a \emph{skip zone} where nothing is heard.}

Arthur Kennelly and Oliver Heaviside had independently proposed such a conducting layer in 1902 to
explain Marconi's transatlantic signal. Edward Appleton demonstrated it experimentally in 1924, by
varying a transmitter's frequency and observing the interference between ground wave and sky wave,
and won the Nobel Prize for the work in 1947. In 1925 Gregory Breit and Merle Tuve measured the
height of the layer by timing the echoes of short radio pulses---the first pulse radar, pointed
straight up.

The ionosphere's reflectivity depends on frequency, time of day, season and the sunspot cycle, which
is why shortwave links fade, why broadcasters change frequency between day and night, and why HF
communication became a craft of propagation forecasting. Marconi's shortwave ``beam'' stations of the
late 1920s carried imperial telegraph traffic at a fraction of the cost of cables, and shortwave
remained the medium of international broadcasting, maritime and military communication for most of the
century. Chapter~\ref{ch:channels} treats propagation mechanisms and fading.

\subsection{FM: the answer to a different question}
\label{sec:ch01:fm}

In 1922 John Carson analysed frequency modulation as a way of saving bandwidth and showed, correctly,
that it could not: an FM signal always occupies at least as much bandwidth as the equivalent AM signal.
He concluded that FM ``inherently distorts without any compensating advantages.'' Armstrong asked a
different question: what if we deliberately use \emph{more} bandwidth? His 1936 paper showed that
wideband FM trades bandwidth for signal-to-noise ratio: a broadcast FM signal that uses about 180\,kHz
to carry 15\,kHz of audio is dramatically quieter than AM.

FM also has a \term{capture effect}: of two signals on the same channel, the receiver locks onto the
stronger and suppresses the weaker, which makes co-channel interference far less audible and later made
FM attractive for mobile radio. This was the first practical demonstration of a principle that Shannon
would make exact twelve years later: bandwidth and signal power are interchangeable currencies
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_currencies}).

\mdcfig{ch01_currencies}{Bandwidth and power as currencies, in the language Shannon would supply in
1948 (Equation~\eqref{eq:ch01:shannon}). Left: at fixed transmitter power, spending more bandwidth
raises the capacity, up to a ceiling---Armstrong's trade. Right: in a fixed 3.1\,kHz channel, spending
power buys capacity only logarithmically: each doubling (+3\,dB) adds about one bit per sample.}

Armstrong built his own FM station at Alpine, New Jersey, and FM broadcasting began in the 42--50\,MHz
band around 1940. In 1945 the FCC moved FM to 88--106\,MHz (later extended to 108\,MHz), making every
existing FM receiver obsolete; Armstrong spent his last years in patent litigation against the large
radio companies and died in 1954. His widow eventually won or settled the suits. FM broadcasting, the
audio of analog television, two-way police and military radio, microwave relay and the first cellular
systems were all built on his invention (Chapters~\ref{ch:analog} and~\ref{ch:cellular}).

\subsection{Radar and the cavity magnetron}
\label{sec:ch01:radar}

Radar was invented in several countries in the 1930s, but its decisive development came in Britain
under the threat of air attack. On 26 February 1935 Robert Watson-Watt and Arnold Wilkins showed that
a bomber flying near the BBC's shortwave transmitter at Daventry produced a detectable reflection, and
by 1939 the Chain Home stations, working at 20--30\,MHz with huge wire antennas strung from steel
towers (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_chainhome}), watched the approaches to the British coast. Long wavelengths
meant poor angular resolution and enormous antennas; what was needed was a high-power source of
centimetre waves that could fit in an aircraft.

\chOnePair{ch01_chainhome}{A surviving Chain Home transmitter tower (Stenigot, Lincolnshire), about
110\,m tall. Between such towers hung the curtain antennas of Britain's 1939 radar
network.}{ch01_magnetron}{The original cavity magnetron of 1940 (Science Museum, London):
six resonant cavities cut into a copper block around the central space where the electrons
spiral.}

In February 1940 John Randall and Harry Boot at the University of Birmingham built the \emph{cavity
magnetron}\index{cavity magnetron} (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_magnetron}), a vacuum tube in which electrons
spiralling in a magnetic field excite resonant cavities cut into a copper anode block---blowing across
the mouths of a ring of bottles, but with electrons instead of breath. It produced hundreds of watts at
a wavelength of about 10\,cm---orders of magnitude more than any previous microwave source---and pulsed
versions soon produced tens of kilowatts. In September 1940 the Tizard Mission carried a magnetron to
the United States, and the MIT Radiation Laboratory, founded that autumn, grew to thousands of staff
developing microwave radar.

Its engineers built the science of microwave engineering: waveguides and cavities, crystal mixers,
low-noise receivers, duplexers, antennas, and the statistical theory of detecting signals in noise,
published after the war in the 28-volume Radiation Laboratory Series. The \emph{matched
filter}\index{matched filter}, which maximises the signal-to-noise ratio of a known pulse in noise, was
derived by D.~O.~North at RCA in 1943 for radar; it is the optimal receiver front end of
Chapter~\ref{ch:baseband}. Microwave relay, satellite communication, radio astronomy and the microwave
oven in your kitchen---which still uses a magnetron---are all children of this wartime effort.

\subsection{Television}

The idea of sending pictures by wire is as old as the telephone, and the basic solution---\emph{scan}
the image line by line, turning two dimensions into one, and send the brightness along each line as a
signal---was patented by Paul Nipkow in 1884, using a spinning disc with a spiral of holes. It is
exactly how you would read a page to someone over the phone: left to right, line by line, top to
bottom (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_scan}).

\mdcfig{ch01_scan}{Scanning: the picture is read line by line (left), and the brightness along each
line becomes a voltage (right). Short sync pulses between lines tell the receiver where each line
starts---the same synchronisation problem Baudot's distributors faced.}

\mdcsidephoto[0.36]{ch01_baird}{John Logie Baird with an early mechanical television receiver.}
John Logie Baird (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_baird}) demonstrated a working mechanical television system in
London in January 1926, with 30 lines, and 30-line experimental broadcasts over BBC transmitters began
in 1929. Mechanical scanning could not go much further: a spinning disc cannot scan hundreds of lines
fast enough. Electronic scanning---Philo Farnsworth's image dissector camera of 1927 and Vladimir
Zworykin's iconoscope at RCA, with the cathode-ray tube as display---could. The BBC began the world's
first regular ``high-definition'' (as it was then called) television service in November 1936; after a
few months of alternating with Baird's 240-line system it standardised on the all-electronic 405-line
Marconi-EMI system, and in the United States the National Television System Committee (NTSC)
standardised 525 lines at 30 frames per second in 6\,MHz channels in 1941.

Television forced bandwidth engineering on a new scale. A picture of 525 lines, refreshed 30 times a
second with comparable horizontal and vertical resolution, needs a video bandwidth of about 4\,MHz,
a thousand times that of a telephone channel. Sending it as double-sideband AM would need 8\,MHz;
NTSC used \term{vestigial-sideband} modulation, which transmits one full sideband and a small vestige
of the other, to fit the picture, a guard band and an FM sound carrier 4.5\,MHz above the vision carrier
into 6\,MHz (Chapter~\ref{ch:analog}). \emph{Interlace}---sending the odd lines and then the even lines
in alternate fields at 60 per second---halved the bandwidth needed for flicker-free display.

\mdcfig{ch01_ntsc_interleave}{The trick behind compatible colour. A scanned picture has a spectrum
made of lines at multiples of the line rate $f_H$ (navy). Placing the colour subcarrier at an odd
multiple of $f_H/2$ puts the colour information's lines (red) neatly in the gaps, so the two share
the same band with little mutual interference.}

\begin{inpractice}[Why American television ran at 29.97 frames per second]
Colour television had to be \emph{compatible}: a colour broadcast had to look right on the tens of
millions of existing black-and-white sets, in the same 6\,MHz channel. The NTSC colour standard of 1953
solved this by adding the colour information as a quadrature-modulated subcarrier inside the luminance
band---an early use of the I/Q modulation of Chapter~\ref{ch:modulation}---at a frequency chosen so that
its spectral lines fall exactly between those of the luminance signal, which are clustered at
multiples of the line rate (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_ntsc_interleave}). That requires the subcarrier to be
an odd multiple of half the line rate: $f_{sc}=\tfrac{455}{2}f_H$. To keep the beat between the colour
subcarrier and the 4.5\,MHz sound carrier from being visible, the sound carrier was also made an exact
multiple of the line rate, $4.5\,\mathrm{MHz} = 286\,f_H$. With the sound carrier fixed, the line rate
had to move slightly:
\begin{gather*}
f_H = \frac{4.5\times10^6}{286} \approx 15\,734.27\ \mathrm{Hz},\qquad
f_{\text{frame}} = \frac{f_H}{525}\approx 29.97\ \mathrm{Hz},\\
f_{sc} = \tfrac{455}{2}f_H \approx 3.579\,545\ \mathrm{MHz}.
\end{gather*}
The black-and-white rates had been 15\,750\,Hz and 30\,Hz; the change of 0.1\% was small enough for
existing receivers to follow. The 29.97\,Hz frame rate and its ``drop-frame'' timecode survive in
digital video today, and 3.579545\,MHz crystals, made by the hundreds of millions for television sets,
became the cheapest frequency reference in electronics---which is why so many early microcomputers ran
their clocks at multiples of it.
\end{inpractice}

\section{The theorists}
\label{sec:ch01:theory}
%======================================================================

While engineers strung wires and raised towers, a handful of theorists, many of them at AT\&T and Bell
Labs, asked a different kind of question: not ``how do we build it?'' but ``what is the most that
\emph{any} system could ever do?'' Their answers are the foundation of this book, and every one of
them came out of a practical problem in the telephone or telegraph plant.


\mdcfig{ch01_hartley_levels}{Why noise limits the alphabet. Each coloured hump is the spread of
received values around one transmitted level. At 15\,dB signal-to-noise ratio four levels are
cleanly separated; at 35\,dB sixteen fit in the same range. Shannon's formula replaces Hartley's
integer $M$ by the smooth quantity $\sqrt{1+S/N}$, a ceiling that good codes approach.}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt,itemsep=4pt]
\item[Harry Nyquist,] a Swedish-born engineer at AT\&T, asked in 1924 how the speed of telegraphy
depends on the line and the code, and showed in 1928 that a channel of bandwidth $B$ can carry at
most $2B$ independent pulses per second without intersymbol interference. He derived the condition a
pulse must satisfy to achieve it---the \emph{Nyquist criterion} of Chapter~\ref{ch:baseband}. With John
B.~Johnson he also explained thermal noise in 1928: every resistor at temperature $T$ generates noise
with available power $kTB$, the floor under every radio receiver (Chapter~\ref{ch:noise}). His 1932
stability criterion founded the analysis of feedback loops.
\item[Ralph Hartley] proposed in 1928 that information be measured as the logarithm of the number of
distinguishable messages. A sequence of $n$ symbols from an alphabet of $M$ symbols can form $M^n$
messages, so its information is $n\log M$, and a channel sending $2B$ symbols per second from an
alphabet of $M$ distinguishable levels carries
\begin{equation}
R = 2B\log_2 M\quad\text{bits per second}.
\label{eq:ch01:hartley}
\end{equation}
Hartley saw that noise limits $M$, but he did not have a theory of how.
\item[Vladimir Kotelnikov] in the Soviet Union (1933), following E.~T.~Whittaker (1915), stated the
sampling theorem: a signal band-limited to $B$ is completely determined by samples taken $2B$ times
per second. Shannon gave it its best-known proof in 1949 (Chapter~\ref{ch:pcm}).
\item[Norbert Wiener] and Andrey Kolmogorov developed, during the Second World War, the theory of
optimal linear filtering of noisy signals, the ancestor of the MMSE equalizer (Chapter~\ref{ch:equalization})
and of the Kalman filter.
\item[Stephen O.~Rice] of Bell Labs published in 1944--45 the mathematical analysis of random noise that
gave the Rayleigh and Rician distributions their place in fading channel theory (Chapter~\ref{ch:channels}).
\item[Dennis Gabor,] in his 1946 paper ``Theory of communication'', introduced the analytic signal and
the time--frequency ``logons'' that anticipate both the complex baseband representation of
Chapter~\ref{ch:signals} and the time--frequency grids of OFDM (Chapter~\ref{ch:ofdm}).
\end{description}

Hartley's formula is worth a picture. If the receiver must decide which of $M$ voltage levels was
sent, the levels must be far enough apart that noise rarely pushes one into its neighbour's territory
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_hartley_levels}). Quiet channel, many levels; noisy channel, few. Hartley knew
this but could not turn it into a number. Shannon could.



\subsection{Claude Shannon}

\chOneFigH[0.34]{ch01_shannon}{Claude Shannon (1916--2001), whose 1948 paper founded information
theory.}
In 1948 Claude Shannon (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_shannon}), then 32 and working at Bell Labs, published
``A Mathematical Theory of Communication.'' It is one of the rare papers that created a field fully
formed---as if someone had published the first paper on mechanics and it had contained Newton's laws,
the conservation of energy and the theory of the steam engine all at once. Shannon modelled
communication with the block diagram of Figure~\ref{fig:ch01:shannon}, defined information as a
measurable quantity---the entropy of the source, in bits, a word he credited to his colleague John
Tukey---and proved two theorems that define the limits of the field. The \emph{source coding theorem}
says that a source can be compressed to its entropy and no further. The \emph{channel coding theorem}
says that every channel has a capacity $C$: at any rate below $C$ it is possible to communicate with
arbitrarily small error probability, and at any rate above it it is not. Before Shannon, engineers
assumed that more speed always meant more errors, and that the only way to make a noisy channel
reliable was to slow down to a crawl. Shannon proved that this was false---that up to a precise speed
limit, you can have both speed and reliability at once.

For the band-limited Gaussian channel the speed limit is
\begin{equation}
C = B\log_2\!\left(1 + \frac{S}{N}\right)\quad\text{bits per second.}
\label{eq:ch01:shannon}
\end{equation}
Compare this with Hartley's~\eqref{eq:ch01:hartley}. Shannon's formula has the same form with the
number of distinguishable levels replaced by $\sqrt{1+S/N}$: noise, not the engineer's choice, sets
how many levels can be told apart, and the answer is a smooth function of the signal-to-noise ratio
rather than an integer. Read it back in words: \emph{double the bandwidth and you double the
capacity; double the power and you gain only about one bit per sample.}

Shannon's proof was not constructive: it showed that good codes exist by averaging over randomly chosen
codes, and it said nothing about how to build or decode one. It was a treasure map with an X on it and
no path. The rest of the history of coding theory, from Marcel Golay's and Richard Hamming's codes of
1949--50 to polar codes in 2009, is the story of the search for practical codes that approach Shannon's
limit. Chapters~\ref{ch:infotheory}--\ref{ch:moderncodes} tell it. Shannon's paper also contains,
almost in passing, results that this chapter has already used: the capacity of the constrained Morse
telegraph channel (0.539 bits per unit time, Section~\ref{sec:ch01:morse}) and the estimate that the
redundancy of English text is roughly one half.

\begin{worked}[The capacity of a telephone line]
A good analog telephone connection passes roughly 300--3400\,Hz, a bandwidth of about $B=3.1$\,kHz,
with a signal-to-noise ratio of about 35\,dB ($S/N\approx3160$) on a clean digital-backbone connection.
Equation~\eqref{eq:ch01:shannon} gives
\[
C = 3100\times\log_2(1+3160)\approx3100\times11.6\approx36\ \mathrm{kb/s}.
\]
At 30\,dB the figure is about 31\,kb/s, and at 40\,dB about 41\,kb/s. The V.34 modem of 1994--96,
using a rich QAM constellation, trellis coding, precoding, adaptive equalisation and constellation
shaping, reached 33.6\,kb/s: within a decibel or two of capacity on a typical line
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_modems}). The 56\,kb/s V.90 modems of 1998 did not break Shannon's law; they
exploited the fact that the downstream path from an Internet provider's digital connection to the local
exchange is not an analog channel with additive noise at all but a 64\,kb/s PCM stream, and only the
final analog loop had to be crossed. Hartley's formula, by comparison, could not have told the modem
designers how close to the limit they were, because it has no noise in it.
\end{worked}

\mdcfig{ch01_modems}{Voiceband modem rates against the Shannon capacity of a 3.1\,kHz telephone channel at
30, 35 and 40\,dB signal-to-noise ratio. Years are approximate dates of introduction or standardisation.
After thirty years of progress, V.34 in the mid-1990s came within a few decibels of the limit; V.90's
56\,kb/s downstream rate is above the analog-channel capacity because most of its path is digital.}

\begin{keyidea}[Shannon's lesson for the working engineer]
Before designing anything, compute the capacity of the channel you have. It tells you whether your goal
is impossible, easy or somewhere in between, and it tells you whether to spend your effort on more power,
more bandwidth, more antennas or a better code. Every link budget in this book ends with that comparison.
\end{keyidea}

\section{War, secrecy and the birth of digital communication}
\label{sec:ch01:digital}
%======================================================================

The Second World War accelerated communication technology as much as any peacetime decade. Radar and
the magnetron were described in Section~\ref{sec:ch01:radar}; two other developments stand out.

\emph{Pulse-code modulation}, invented by Alec Reeves of the International Telephone and Telegraph
laboratories in Paris in 1937, represented a voice signal as a sequence of numbers: sample the waveform,
quantise each sample to one of a finite set of levels, and send the level as a binary code
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_pcm_steps}; Chapter~\ref{ch:pcm}). At first it seemed absurdly wasteful---a voice
channel that needs 4\,kHz as an analog signal needs tens of kilohertz as PCM---but it had one decisive
advantage: a digital signal can be \emph{regenerated}.

\mdcfig{ch01_pcm_steps}{Pulse-code modulation in three steps. Sample the waveform at regular instants
(dots), round each sample to the nearest of a few allowed levels (squares; 8 levels here, 256 in the
telephone network), and send the level number in binary (codes along the bottom). From then on the
voice is just a stream of bits---which can be regenerated, switched, encrypted and stored.}

\chOnePair{ch01_sigsaly}{Part of a SIGSALY terminal, the Bell Labs secure telephone system of
1943: a room full of equipment to digitise, encrypt and transmit one conversation.}{ch01_lamarr}{Hedy Lamarr in 1940. With the composer George
Antheil she patented a frequency-hopping radio control for torpedoes in 1942.}

The first operational digital voice system was SIGSALY (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_sigsaly}), built by Bell
Labs for the secure telephone link between Washington and London from 1943. It digitised a vocoder's
slowly-varying channel levels and enciphered them with a one-time key taken from phonograph records of
random noise, of which only two copies existed (Chapter~\ref{ch:spreadspectrum} has the details). Each
terminal weighed tens of tonnes and filled a room, and Allied leaders used it for their most sensitive
conversations. The voice quality was poor, but the principle
was proved: a conversation can be turned into numbers, the numbers protected, and the conversation
rebuilt at the far end.

\emph{Frequency hopping} was the subject of a 1942 patent by the actress Hedy Lamarr
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_lamarr}) and the composer
George Antheil, who proposed hopping a torpedo's radio-control link among 88 frequencies in a pattern
stored on synchronised piano rolls, so that an enemy could not jam it. The patent was not used, and its
significance is easily overstated, but it states clearly the principle of spreading a signal over a wide
band in a pattern known only to the intended receiver---\term{spread spectrum}---which, developed in
secret through the Cold War, became central to military communications, GPS, Bluetooth and code-division
cellular systems (Chapter~\ref{ch:spreadspectrum}).


\subsection{Why digital wins: regeneration}

The relay of Section~\ref{sec:ch01:telegraph} and the PCM regenerator share the property that makes
digital transmission win over long distances---the copyist versus the photocopier. An analog repeater
amplifies whatever it receives, signal and noise alike. If each of $N$ identical spans adds the same
noise, the noise powers add and the end-to-end signal-to-noise ratio is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SNR}_{\text{analog}} = \frac{\mathrm{SNR}_{\text{span}}}{N},
\label{eq:ch01:analogcascade}
\end{equation}
falling by 10\,dB for every factor of ten in distance. A digital regenerator instead decides which
symbol was sent and transmits a clean new one. Noise does not accumulate; only decision \emph{errors}
do, and if each span has error probability $p$, the end-to-end error probability is
\begin{equation}
P_{\text{digital}} \approx Np \qquad (Np\ll1).
\label{eq:ch01:digitalcascade}
\end{equation}
Because $p$ falls exponentially with the per-span SNR (Chapter~\ref{ch:baseband}), a modest increase in
SNR per span compensates for an enormous number of spans. In other words, analog quality decays
\emph{in proportion to} distance, while digital quality decays only with the \emph{logarithm} of it.
Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_regeneration} compares the two.

\chOneFigH{ch01_regeneration}{Left: end-to-end SNR of a chain of analog repeaters, each span having the
indicated SNR; noise accumulates and the SNR falls by 10\,dB per decade of repeaters. Right: end-to-end
bit error rate of a chain of binary regenerators (antipodal signalling, per-span SNR as indicated); the
error rate grows only in proportion to the number of spans. A transcontinental digital link needs only
a few decibels more per span than a single hop.}


\begin{worked}[A thousand kilometres of repeaters]
A coaxial route needs a repeater every 2\,km, so a 2000\,km route has $N=1000$ spans. For an analog
FDM system to deliver an end-to-end SNR of 40\,dB, each span must achieve $40+10\log_{10}1000=70$\,dB,
a demanding figure for amplifiers carrying thousands of channels, and every repeater's
intermodulation adds to the total. For a binary digital system, a per-span SNR of 17\,dB gives
$p=\Q(\sqrt{50})\approx7\times10^{-13}$, so the 1000-span route has an error probability of about
$7\times10^{-10}$---and every regenerated bit is a perfect copy of the original. The digital system
therefore needs some 50\,dB less SNR per span. It pays for this in bandwidth (a 64\,kb/s PCM channel
needs far more than 4\,kHz) and in electronics, which is why digital transmission took over only when
transistors made the electronics cheap and fibre made the bandwidth abundant.
\end{worked}

\mdcfig{ch01_regen_waveform}{The photocopier and the copyist, simulated. Top: a binary signal passed
through three analog repeaters; each hop adds its own noise and nothing removes it. Bottom: the same
signal through three regenerators; each hop decides and re-sends a clean pulse, so the noise never
accumulates (until, rarely, it causes a decision error).}

\begin{tryit}[Race the repeaters]
The last experiment in Lab 34 races a chain of analog repeaters against a chain of regenerators. Set
\emph{spans} to 1000 and drag the \emph{per-span SNR} slider down from 22\,dB. The analog chain's
end-to-end SNR is hopeless long before the digital chain's bit error rate even begins to rise; then
find the SNR at which the regenerators finally break down, and notice how abruptly it happens---the
famous digital ``cliff''.
\end{tryit}

\begin{keyidea}[What the digital era bequeathed: regenerate, then correct]
Once a signal is represented by discrete symbols, noise can be stopped from accumulating at every
regenerator, and the residual errors can be corrected by coding. Together with Shannon's theorem,
which says that with enough coding the residual errors can be made as small as desired at any rate
below capacity, this is why every analog system---telephony, broadcasting, television, cellular---has been
replaced by a digital one. The analog era's legacy is not abandoned but absorbed: inside every digital
system are analog filters, amplifiers and mixers whose design follows Sections~\ref{sec:ch01:repeaters}
to~\ref{sec:ch01:radar}.
\end{keyidea}

\section{Transistors, satellites and fibre}
\label{sec:ch01:solidstate}
%======================================================================

\subsection{The solid-state revolution}

\chOneFigH[0.28]{ch01_transistor}{A replica of the first point-contact transistor (Bell Labs,
December 1947): two gold contacts pressed onto a slab of germanium.}
In December 1947 John Bardeen and Walter Brattain, in William Shockley's group at Bell Labs,
demonstrated the point-contact transistor (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_transistor})---a crude-looking lump of
germanium, a plastic wedge and a strip of gold foil that did what a vacuum tube did, without the
heat, the glass or the glowing filament. Shockley's junction transistor followed in 1948--51, and the
three shared the 1956 Nobel Prize in Physics. Bell Labs' motive was the telephone network: vacuum tubes
were power-hungry, hot and unreliable, and electromechanical switches were slow. The integrated circuit
followed in 1958--59 (Jack Kilby at Texas Instruments, Robert Noyce at Fairchild), and with it the
exponential scaling that would eventually make it cheaper to process a radio signal digitally than with
analog components. A modern smartphone's modem chip contains billions of transistors; the one in the
photograph had one. Every chapter of this book after this one assumes, silently, that arithmetic is
almost free---an assumption that would have seemed absurd to anyone before 1947.

The telephone network's first large digital transmission system, the T1 carrier of 1962, sent 24 PCM
voice channels over two twisted pairs. Each channel was sampled 8000 times a second with 8 bits per
sample, and one framing bit was added to each 192-bit frame:
$(24\times8+1)\times8000 = 1.544$\,Mb/s. Its regenerative repeaters sat every 6000\,ft (1.83\,km),
exactly where the loading coils had been (Section~\ref{sec:ch01:heaviside}), and the bipolar line code it
used is analysed in Chapter~\ref{ch:baseband}. T1 and its European counterpart E1 (2.048\,Mb/s, 30 channels)
built the digital hierarchy that, by the 1980s, had replaced analog FDM throughout the long-distance
network.

\subsection{Communication satellites}

\mdcside[0.40]{ch01_geo}{Clarke's 1945 proposal, to scale: three relays in geostationary orbit, each
seeing about a third of the Earth.}
In a 1945 article in \emph{Wireless World}, the science-fiction writer and radar engineer Arthur
C.~Clarke observed that a satellite at an altitude of about 35\,786\,km above the equator orbits once
per sidereal day and therefore appears fixed in the sky; three such satellites could cover almost the
whole Earth (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_geo}). Twelve years later the Soviet Union launched Sputnik~1
(4 October 1957), whose radio beacons on 20 and 40\,MHz were tracked by amateurs worldwide---and whose
Doppler shift, measured at the Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory, led directly to the Transit
satellite navigation system and eventually to GPS (Chapter~\ref{ch:spreadspectrum}). The idea of
listening to a satellite's beeps and working out where it was, turned inside out, became the idea of
listening to several satellites and working out where \emph{you} are.

Progress was then rapid. Project SCORE (1958) broadcast a recorded Christmas message from orbit.
NASA's Echo~1 (1960) was a passive 30\,m metallised balloon that simply reflected signals. AT\&T's
Telstar~1 (1962; Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_telstar}) carried the first live transatlantic television, but in
a low orbit it was visible to its ground stations for only minutes at a time. Syncom~2 (1963) reached
geosynchronous orbit, and Syncom~3 (1964), in true geostationary orbit, relayed television of the Tokyo
Olympics to the United States (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_syncom}). Intelsat~I, ``Early Bird'', began
commercial transatlantic service in 1965 with 240 telephone circuits.

\chOnePair{ch01_telstar}{Telstar (a model in the Mus\'ee des Arts et M\'etiers, Paris). Telstar~1
(1962) was the first satellite to relay live television across the Atlantic; its faceted surface is
covered in solar cells.}{ch01_syncom}{Syncom (NASA illustration), the first geosynchronous
communication satellite: Syncom~2 reached its orbit in 1963, Syncom~3 relayed the 1964 Tokyo
Olympics.}

Satellites then carried most intercontinental telephone traffic until fibre-optic cables overtook them
in the 1990s, and satellite television broadcasting, from the 1970s, changed the media of whole
continents. Chapter~\ref{ch:satellite} develops orbital mechanics, satellite link budgets and modern
systems from DVB-S2 broadcasting to the low-Earth-orbit constellations that, sixty years after
Telstar, have returned to Telstar's orbit in their thousands.

\subsection{Light}

\chOnePair{ch01_kao}{Charles Kao, who showed in 1966 that purified glass could carry light
far enough to rival copper. Nobel Prize in Physics, 2009.}{ch01_fibre}{Inspecting an optical fibre in a laboratory. A strand of glass about as thick
as a hair can carry tens of terabits per second.}

In 1966 Charles Kao (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_kao}) and George Hockham, at Standard Telecommunication
Laboratories in England, argued that the high loss of glass fibre was due to impurities rather than to
anything fundamental, and that purified silica could reach losses below 20\,dB/km, making optical
fibre competitive with coaxial cable. At the time the idea seemed fanciful: ordinary window glass is so
lossy that a few metres of it look green and murky edge-on. Corning achieved that figure in 1970;
modern fibre loses about 0.17--0.2\,dB/km at 1550\,nm, so light can travel 100\,km and lose only 99\%
of its power (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_fibre_loss}). If seawater were as clear as modern fibre, you could
see the bottom of the deepest ocean trench. Semiconductor lasers that ran continuously at room
temperature arrived in the same year as Corning's fibre.


\mdcfig[0.55]{ch01_fibre_loss}{The collapse of fibre loss (approximate
milestones). From about 1000\,dB/km for typical glass of 1966 to about 0.2\,dB/km by 1979: a kilometre
of the old glass would pass $10^{-100}$ of the light, a kilometre of modern fibre about 96\%.}

The erbium-doped fibre amplifier (1987) amplified light directly, without converting it to
electricity, and amplified many wavelengths at once, which made wavelength-division multiplexing
economical---the FDM of Section~\ref{sec:ch01:carrier} reborn at 200\,THz. The first transatlantic fibre
cable, TAT-8 (1988), carried 280\,Mb/s on each fibre pair; a modern submarine cable carries hundreds of
terabits per second using coherent detection, the same QAM and DSP you will study in
Chapters~\ref{ch:modulation}--\ref{ch:equalization}, applied to light (Chapter~\ref{ch:wireline}).
Kao received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2009.

\section{Networks of computers}
\label{sec:ch01:internet}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Modems}

Computers began talking over telephone lines in the 1950s, in the US Air Force's SAGE air-defence
system, which linked radars to computers with \emph{modems}---modulator-demodulators that turned bits
into tones the telephone network would carry. The data modems that followed are a history of
physical-layer engineering in miniature (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_modems}): 300\,b/s frequency-shift keying in
the Bell 103 of 1962; phase-shift keying at 1200 and 2400\,b/s; 9600\,b/s with 32-point QAM and
trellis-coded modulation (Gottfried Ungerboeck's 1982 invention, Chapter~\ref{ch:classiccodes}) in V.32;
and 33.6\,kb/s in V.34, with adaptive equalisers and echo cancellers that would have astonished the
engineers of the 1950s. If you are old enough to remember the screech and hiss of a dial-up modem
connecting, you were listening to those equalisers training and those echo cancellers converging. When
the telephone channel was exhausted, DSL and cable modems moved the same techniques to the wider
bandwidth of the bare copper pair and the coaxial cable (Chapter~\ref{ch:wireline}).
Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_rates} puts these numbers in perspective.

\mdcfig{ch01_rates}{Approximate data rates of landmark systems over two centuries. Each family advanced by
roughly a factor of ten every six to ten years once it was digital; the values are order-of-magnitude
illustrations of typical or peak rates rather than precise specifications.}

\subsection{Packet switching}

The telephone network was \emph{circuit switched}\index{circuit switching}: a call reserved a path through
the network for its whole duration, which suited continuous speech. Computer traffic is bursty---long
silences punctuated by bursts of data---and reserving a circuit for it is wasteful
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_circuit_packet}). Three people arrived independently at the alternative.

\mdcfig{ch01_circuit_packet}{Circuit versus packet switching for three bursty users (illustrative).
Top: each user owns a fixed third of the link, which sits idle most of the time. Bottom: the same
bursts sent as packets over the whole link, one after another as they arrive. The link is busy far
less, and each burst finishes sooner---at the cost of occasional queueing when bursts collide.}

Paul Baran at the RAND Corporation, studying in 1960--64 how a communication network might survive a
nuclear attack, proposed a distributed mesh (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_topologies}) in which messages were
broken into small blocks, each routed independently from node to node along whatever paths survived.
Donald Davies at the National Physical Laboratory in Britain arrived at the same idea in 1965 for
reasons of efficiency, and named the blocks \emph{packets}\index{packet switching}. Leonard
Kleinrock's queueing-theory analysis of message-switched networks (thesis 1962, book 1964) provided the
mathematics of delay and throughput.

\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\begin{minipage}[b]{0.30\linewidth}\centering\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{figs/photos/ch01_imp}\caption{An ARPANET packet switch (IMP).\mdccredit{ch01_imp}}\label{fig:ch01_imp}\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[b]{0.69\linewidth}\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/photos/ch01_arpanet}\caption{The ARPANET's logical map in March 1977: a few dozen packet switches
(IMPs and TIPs) and the computers attached to them, from Hawaii to London. The whole Internet once
fitted on one sheet of paper.\mdccredit{ch01_arpanet}}\label{fig:ch01_arpanet}\end{minipage}
\end{figure}

\begin{analogy}[The private train and the parcel service]
Circuit switching is like booking a private train from London to Edinburgh for the whole afternoon
because you might want to send a few parcels: the track is yours whether you use it or not. Packet
switching is the parcel service: you hand in each parcel with an address, it shares lorries and
sorting depots with everybody else's, and different parcels may even take different roads. It is
vastly more efficient for bursty traffic. The limit: parcels can be delayed in a busy depot, arrive
out of order or go missing, and the protocols above (TCP, for one) exist to repair exactly that.
\end{analogy}

The US Advanced Research Projects Agency funded the ARPANET, whose first link, between UCLA and the
Stanford Research Institute, carried its first message on 29 October 1969: the intended ``LOGIN''
was cut short by a crash after ``LO''. Packet switches called IMPs (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_imp}), built by Bolt Beranek
and Newman, connected the hosts (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_arpanet}). Vinton Cerf and Robert Kahn's 1974 paper on
internetworking led to TCP/IP, to which the ARPANET switched on 1 January 1983; Tim Berners-Lee's
World Wide Web (proposed at CERN in 1989) made the Internet a mass medium in the 1990s.



Meanwhile, on the physical layer, Norman Abramson's ALOHAnet (1971) linked the islands of Hawaii by
packet radio with a random-access protocol, and Robert Metcalfe's Ethernet (1973) adapted it to a
shared coaxial cable with ``listen before talk'' carrier sensing. Both are the subject of
Chapter~\ref{ch:multipleaccess}. Every Wi-Fi network you have ever joined still listens before it
talks, exactly as Ethernet's designers taught it to.


\begin{keyidea}[What the network era bequeathed: statistical multiplexing and layering]
Packet switching shares a link among many users not by fixed slots of time or frequency, as Baudot and
the carrier hierarchy did, but statistically\index{statistical multiplexing}: each packet takes the link
when it needs it. Because bursty sources rarely all transmit at once, a link can serve many more users
than its capacity divided by their peak rates (TASI, Section~\ref{sec:ch01:carrier}, exploited the same
statistics). The price is queueing delay and the possibility of loss, which the protocols above must
handle. The Internet's second legacy is \emph{layering}: the physical layer that this book is about
delivers bits, and need know nothing about the applications above it---which is why the same fibre,
Wi-Fi link or 5G carrier can carry telephone calls, video and web pages.
\end{keyidea}

\section{Cellular radio}
\label{sec:ch01:cellular}
%======================================================================

Mobile telephony before cells was a luxury. The first American mobile telephone service, opened in
St.~Louis in 1946, used one high-power transmitter covering a whole city with a handful of channels;
in the 1970s New York's mobile service could serve only a few hundred customers and had a long waiting
list (Chapter~\ref{ch:multipleaccess}). Picture a whole city of millions sharing a dozen or so radio
channels, like a city with a single telephone booth on each of a dozen street corners.

\chOnePairH{ch01_cooper_dynatac}{Martin Cooper in 2007 with a Motorola DynaTAC, the phone
family he used for the first public handheld cellular call in 1973.}{ch01_dynatac_galaxy}{Thirty years of mobile phones side by side: a Motorola DynaTAC
of the 1980s and a smartphone of the 2010s. The brick carried one analog FM voice call; the slab
carries a broadband Internet connection, a camera, a GNSS receiver and several radios.}

The cellular concept was described in a 1947 Bell Labs memorandum by Douglas Ring, building on ideas
of W.~Rae Young: instead of one powerful transmitter, use many low-power base stations, each covering
a small \emph{cell}, and reuse the same frequencies in cells far enough apart
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_topologies}). Capacity then grows with the number of cells rather than being
fixed by the spectrum. Want twice the capacity? Split every cell in two. The idea waited three decades
for the technology---microprocessors to manage handoff between cells, and spectrum allocations to use.

The first commercial cellular network opened in Tokyo in 1979; in the United States, the Advanced
Mobile Phone System (AMPS) launched in Chicago in October 1983, ten years after Martin Cooper of
Motorola (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_cooper_dynatac}) had made the first public handheld cellular call on a
New York street in April 1973. Table~\ref{tab:ch01:gens} summarises what followed. The European GSM
system, the product of an unprecedented multinational standards effort, carried its first call in
Finland in July 1991; the first text message was sent over a GSM network in December 1992, and SMS
became an unexpected global habit. Each generation brought the newest physical-layer ideas of its time
into billions of devices: digital modulation and speech coding in 2G, code-division multiple access and
turbo codes in 3G, OFDM and MIMO in 4G, LDPC and polar codes and massive antenna arrays in 5G. The
smartphone---Apple's iPhone of 2007 is the conventional landmark---turned the mobile network from a
telephone service into the primary way most of humanity reaches the Internet
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_dynatac_galaxy}). Chapter~\ref{ch:cellular} tells the full story, including the
``CDMA wars'' of the early 1990s, when a small company named Qualcomm---founded by Irwin Jacobs and
Andrew Viterbi, the inventor of the Viterbi algorithm---persuaded a sceptical industry that CDMA could
work.

\section{Unlicensed radio, Wi-Fi and the coding renaissance}
\label{sec:ch01:modern}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Unlicensed radio and Wi-Fi}

In 1985 the US Federal Communications Commission opened the industrial, scientific and medical (ISM)
bands at 902\,MHz, 2.4\,GHz and 5.8\,GHz to unlicensed spread-spectrum communication. Nobody expected
much of it: 2.4\,GHz is where microwave ovens heat water. The IEEE 802.11 standard followed in 1997 at
1--2\,Mb/s, 802.11b reached 11\,Mb/s in 1999, and the industry adopted the brand name Wi-Fi; Bluetooth,
named after a tenth-century Danish king who united warring tribes, appeared in the same band in 1999.

Today more Internet traffic reaches its final destination over Wi-Fi than over any other medium, and
the bands that were once a spectral junkyard are among the most valuable in the world. The lesson for
the spectrum engineer is that flexible rules and cheap hardware can create more value than exclusive
licences. Chapter~\ref{ch:wifi} follows 802.11 from 1\,Mb/s to Wi-Fi~7's 46\,Gb/s peak.

\subsection{The coding renaissance and the MIMO revolution}

Between 1993 and 2009, coding theory went from ``several decibels from capacity'' to ``within a fraction
of a decibel''---forty-five years after Shannon drew the X on the map, engineers finally found a path
to it. In 1993 Claude Berrou, Alain Glavieux and Punya Thitimajshima presented \emph{turbo codes},
which used two simple convolutional codes and an iterative decoder to come within 0.7\,dB of the
Shannon limit; many experts initially assumed the results were wrong. In 1996 David MacKay and Radford
Neal rediscovered Robert Gallager's 1960 \emph{low-density parity-check} codes and showed they were as
good. And in 2009 Erdal Ar{\i}kan introduced \emph{polar codes}, the first codes proven to achieve
capacity with low-complexity encoding and decoding. 5G uses both LDPC and polar codes.
Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_coding_gap} shows the march.

\mdcfig{ch01_coding_gap}{Forty-five years of closing the gap (approximate figures for codes of rate
about 1/2). The bar shows the signal energy per bit needed for one error in $10^5$ bits; the dashed
line is Shannon's limit for this code rate. Uncoded transmission is almost 10\,dB away; turbo and LDPC
codes are within a fraction of a decibel.}

At the same time, Gerard Foschini at Bell Labs (1996) and Emre Telatar (1995) showed that a channel with
multiple antennas at both ends has a capacity that grows linearly with the number of antennas---an
entirely new resource, obtained from the same bandwidth and power by exploiting the multipath scattering
that radio engineers had always regarded as a nuisance. Siavash Alamouti's two-antenna space-time code
(1998) was simple enough to put into every handset. Multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) transmission is
now in every Wi-Fi access point and cellular base station (Chapter~\ref{ch:mimo}).

\subsection{The software-defined radio}

For most of the twentieth century a radio \emph{was} its hardware: its filters, oscillators and detectors
fixed what it could do. As digital processing grew cheaper, it became possible to digitise the signal
close to the antenna and do everything else in software. Joseph Mitola coined the term \emph{software
radio} in 1992. GNU Radio, an open-source framework for building radios in software, began in 2001, and
the Universal Software Radio Peripheral (USRP) from Ettus Research gave students and researchers
affordable hardware for it. Today the ``software radio'' idea is simply how radios are built: a 5G base
station is largely software running on general-purpose or specialised processors, and the Ettus B200 on
your bench is architecturally a scaled-down cousin of a cellular radio unit (Chapter~\ref{ch:sdr}).
Armstrong would recognise its block diagram; the mixers and filters are now arithmetic.

\subsection{The road ahead}

\mdcphoto[0.55]{ch01_starlink}{A stack of Starlink satellites in orbit, moments before
deployment (SpaceX): low-Earth-orbit broadband, sixty years after Telstar.}
The story does not end. As this book is written, thousands of low-Earth-orbit satellites
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_starlink}) are delivering broadband to ships, aircraft and remote villages, and
mobile phones are beginning to talk to satellites directly. Sixth-generation research is integrating
radar-like sensing into communication waveforms (the Breit--Tuve pulse and the Chain Home echo reborn
inside a cellular signal), exploring new modulations for high-mobility channels, putting machine
learning into the physical layer, and merging terrestrial and satellite networks into one system.
Chapter~\ref{ch:sixg} surveys these directions. Every one of them will meet the same four adversaries
as Chappe's operators---limited alphabets, noise, dispersion and the need to share---and the engineers
who build them will, knowingly or not, reuse the ideas of this chapter. Some of those engineers will
be reading this book. The chapters that follow give you the tools; this one has tried to give you the
company.

\section{Lessons from two centuries}
\label{sec:ch01:lessons}
%======================================================================

Looking back over this history, a few patterns recur so often that they amount to engineering
principles. Pin them above your desk. The second is the lesson Whitehouse learned the hard way
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_power_vs_isi}); the sixth is drawn in Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_old_ideas}.



\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Every channel is eventually limited by noise and dispersion.} Kelvin's cable, Nyquist's
telegraph and a 5G link differ in scale, not in kind. Intersymbol interference and noise are the two
enemies, and every technique in this book fights one or both.
\item \textbf{Power fights noise; it does not fight distortion.} Whitehouse's 2000 volts made the 1858
cable fail faster. Distortion yields to better media, pulse shaping and equalisation.
\item \textbf{Digital wins because it can be regenerated.} Once a signal is represented by discrete
symbols, errors can be prevented from accumulating and corrected by coding. This is why every analog
system has been replaced by a digital one.
\item \textbf{Bandwidth, power, antennas and complexity are interchangeable currencies.} Armstrong traded
bandwidth for SNR; Shannon quantified the exchange rate; turbo and LDPC codes traded computation for
power; MIMO traded antennas for bandwidth. Good design is choosing the cheapest currency.
\item \textbf{Codes should fit both the source and the channel.} Vail's frequency-ordered Morse code
and Chappe's codebook compressed the source; Baudot's fixed-length code fitted the machine. Modern
systems separate the two jobs, as Shannon proved they may.
\item \textbf{Old ideas return when technology catches up.} Gallager's LDPC codes, the cellular concept,
frequency hopping, multicarrier modulation (first proposed in the 1960s) and even Bell's harmonic
telegraph all waited decades for cheap enough electronics.
\item \textbf{Compatibility shapes everything.} The 4\,kHz voice channel, the 8\,kHz sampling rate,
29.97 frames per second and the 15\,kHz subcarrier spacing persist because new systems had to live with
old ones.
\item \textbf{Standards and measurement are engineering.} The 1861 inquiry into the Atlantic cable gave
us the ohm; the ITU of 1865 gave us interoperable networks; 3GPP gives us a phone that works on every
continent.
\end{enumerate}

\problems
\markboth{Problems}{Problems}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\thechapter.\arabic*]
\item \emph{(Chappe.)} The Chappe telegraph had 98 positions, of which 92 carried data, at about one
sign every 30\,s. (a) Compute its information rate in bits per second using Hartley's measure. (b)
Compare with a Morse operator sending 20 words per minute (assume 5 letters per word and 4.2 bits per
letter). (c) The Swedish shutter telegraph had 10 binary shutters. If it could display a new symbol
every 15\,s, which system had the higher rate?
\item \emph{(Pipelining.)} A semaphore line of $N$ stations has per-hop delay $d$ and can accept a new
sign every $T$ seconds. Derive the time to deliver a message of $K$ signs. For $N=120$ (roughly
Paris--Toulon), $d=30$\,s and $T=30$\,s, how long does a 40-sign message take? What fraction of that
time is due to latency rather than throughput?
\item \emph{(Morse code.)} Using Equation~\eqref{eq:ch01:morsedur}, verify that ``PARIS'' with its
trailing word gap occupies 50 units. Then find the assignment of the 26 existing Morse codewords to
letters that minimises the average duration for English (sort the codewords by duration and the letters
by frequency). How much better than Morse's own assignment is it?
\item \emph{(Baudot.)} A Baudot quadruplex line runs its distributors at 3 revolutions per second, each
revolution carrying one five-unit character for each of four operators. (a) What is the line's
signalling rate in baud? (b) How many words per minute does each operator achieve (6 characters per
word)? (c) If the distributors drift apart by 0.1\% in speed, how many revolutions pass before a unit is
misrouted, assuming no correction?
\item \emph{(Law of squares.)} If the 1866 cable (about 3000\,km) supported 8 words per minute, what rate
would a 1500\,km cable of the same construction support? What would halving the capacitance per unit
length achieve? What does doubling the copper conductor's cross-section do to $R$, to $C$ (assume the
insulation thickness is unchanged), and to the speed?
\item \emph{(Telegrapher's equations.)} Starting from~\eqref{eq:ch01:gamma}, show that when $\omega L\gg R$
and $\omega C\gg G$ the attenuation is $\alpha\approx\tfrac{R}{2}\sqrt{C/L}+\tfrac{G}{2}\sqrt{L/C}$. Find the
value of $L$ that minimises $\alpha$ for given $R$, $G$ and $C$, and show that it satisfies Heaviside's
condition~\eqref{eq:ch01:distortionless}.
\item \emph{(Loading.)} European practice used 88\,mH coils every 1.83\,km on some cables and lighter
loading (for example 44\,mH) on others. Compute the cutoff frequencies for a cable of 0.052\,$\mu$F/km
with 88\,mH and with 44\,mH coils at 1.83\,km spacing, and with 88\,mH at 0.915\,km. Which plan would you
choose for a carrier system that needs 12\,kHz of bandwidth?
\item \emph{(Repeaters.)} A 4000\,km loaded line has an attenuation of 0.02\,dB/km. The overall loss must
not exceed 20\,dB and each two-wire repeater can provide at most 18\,dB without singing. How many
repeaters are needed? How many would be needed if four-wire operation allowed 30\,dB per repeater?
\item \emph{(Feedback.)} An amplifier has open-loop gain $A=2000$ that varies by $\pm20\%$ with temperature.
What feedback factor $\beta$ is needed for the closed-loop gain to vary by no more than $\pm0.5\%$? What is
the resulting closed-loop gain? If the open-loop amplifier produces a third harmonic 30\,dB below the
fundamental at full output, what is the harmonic level with feedback?
\item \emph{(Carrier multiplexing.)} A carrier telephone system stacks 600 single-sideband voice channels,
each allotted 4\,kHz. What total bandwidth does it require? If double-sideband AM had been used instead,
how many channels would fit in the same band? In the basic group of Figure~\ref{fig:ch01_fdm}, at what
frequency does a 1\,kHz test tone on channel 5 appear?
\item \emph{(Erlang.)} A rural exchange offers 12 erlangs of busy-hour traffic to a trunk group. Using the
recursion of the worked example, find the number of trunks needed for 1\% and for 0.1\% blocking. Compare
the utilisation (carried traffic per trunk) of the two designs.
\item \emph{(Spark versus CW.)} Using~\eqref{eq:ch01:spark}, find the decrement that would let a spark
transmitter at 500\,kHz occupy no more than 5\,kHz (half-power). How many cycles does its wave train
last before decaying to 1\% of its initial amplitude?
\item \emph{(Television.)} Estimate the video bandwidth of a 405-line, 25 frame-per-second system and of a
525-line, 30 frame-per-second system, assuming equal horizontal and vertical resolution, a 4:3 aspect ratio,
that about 92\% of lines and 83\% of each line period are active, and a Kell factor of 0.7. Then verify the
NTSC colour numbers $f_H$, $f_{\text{frame}}$ and $f_{sc}$ quoted in the text.
\item \emph{(Shannon.)} Use Equation~\eqref{eq:ch01:shannon} to compute the capacity of a 3.1\,kHz
telephone channel at 30, 35 and 40\,dB signal-to-noise ratio. What SNR would a 56\,kb/s modem need if the
channel really were analog? Compare with Hartley's formula: how many levels $M$ give the same rate as the
35\,dB capacity?
\item \emph{(Simulation.)} Modify \texttt{fig\_regeneration} in \texttt{ch01\_figs.py} to simulate, rather than
compute, a chain of 20 binary regenerators and a chain of 20 analog amplifiers carrying the same random
binary signal, with a final decision after the analog chain. Plot the end-to-end bit error rate of both
against the per-span SNR and find the SNR advantage of regeneration at a BER of $10^{-6}$.
\end{enumerate}
