\chapter{Digital Filters and Multirate Processing}
\label{ch:dsp}

\begin{chapterintro}
A software-defined radio is, to a first approximation, a collection of digital filters
running at different sample rates. Channel selection, pulse shaping, matched filtering,
interpolation for timing recovery, decimation after the ADC and interpolation before the DAC
are all filtering problems, and the efficiency with which they are solved determines whether
a design fits in a phone, an FPGA or a satellite. This chapter is the working engineer's
guide to digital filters: how to specify them, design them, implement them in fixed and
floating point, and above all how to change sample rates efficiently with polyphase
structures, half-band filters, CIC decimators and filter-bank channelizers. It closes with the
oscillators and rotation engines---NCOs and CORDIC---that do the mixing in every digital
radio.
\end{chapterintro}

\section{Discrete-time systems in one page}

A discrete-time linear time-invariant (LTI) system is characterised by its impulse response
$h[n]$ and computes the convolution $y[n]=\sum_k h[k]\,x[n-k]$. Its \term{z-transform}
$H(z)=\sum_n h[n]z^{-n}$ turns convolution into multiplication, and its frequency response is
$H(e^{j\omega})$, the z-transform on the unit circle, with $\omega=2\pi f/f_s$. Most practical
systems are described by a difference equation,
\begin{equation}
y[n]=\sum_{k=0}^{M}b_k\,x[n-k]-\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_k\,y[n-k]
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
H(z)=\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{M}b_kz^{-k}}{1+\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_kz^{-k}}.
\end{equation}
The roots of the numerator are the \term{zeros} and those of the denominator the
\term{poles}. A causal system is stable if and only if all its poles lie strictly inside the
unit circle. The magnitude response dips near zeros and peaks near poles; with a little practice
you can sketch a frequency response from a pole-zero plot, and every experienced DSP engineer
does.

If $a_k=0$ for all $k$, the filter has only zeros and a finite impulse response: it is an
\term{FIR} filter. Otherwise feedback makes the impulse response infinite: an \term{IIR}
filter. Which to use is the first decision in any filter design.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{p{3.2cm}p{5.3cm}p{5.3cm}}
\toprule
& FIR & IIR \\
\midrule
Phase & Exactly linear if coefficients are symmetric & Nonlinear, worst near band edges \\
Stability & Always stable & Must be ensured; sensitive to quantization \\
Order for a sharp filter & High (tens to hundreds of taps) & Low (4 to 10) \\
Multirate efficiency & Excellent (polyphase) & Limited \\
Fixed-point behaviour & Benign & Limit cycles, overflow, scaling \\
Typical use in radios & Pulse shaping, channel filters, decimators & DC blockers, AGC loops, audio \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

In communications, the need for linear phase (a filter with nonlinear phase smears pulses
in time, which is intersymbol interference; Chapter~\ref{ch:baseband}) and for efficient
rate changes makes FIR filters the default. IIR filters earn their place where phase does not
matter or where a very low order is essential.

\section{Specifying a filter}

A low-pass specification has four numbers: the passband edge $f_p$, the stopband edge $f_{st}$,
the maximum passband ripple $\delta_p$ (or $A_p$ in dB) and the minimum stopband attenuation
$A_s$ in dB. The transition width $\Delta f=f_{st}-f_p$ and $A_s$ largely determine the cost. A
useful estimate of the number of taps of an optimal FIR filter is fred harris's rule of thumb,
\begin{equation}
N\approx\frac{A_s}{22\,(\Delta f/f_s)},
\label{eq:ch06:harris}
\end{equation}
so a filter with 60\,dB of attenuation and a transition width of 1\% of the sample rate needs
about 270 taps. Two lessons follow. Halving the transition doubles the cost. And the transition
width is relative to the sample rate at which the filter runs, which is the key to the
multirate savings later in this chapter: \emph{run sharp filters at low rates}.

\section{FIR filter design}

\subsection{The window method}

The ideal low-pass filter has the infinite impulse response $h_d[n]=2f_c\sinc(2f_cn)$.
Truncating it to $N$ taps is the same as multiplying by a rectangular window, which convolves
the ideal response with the window's spectrum and produces the \emph{Gibbs} ripple of about 9\%
(21\,dB stopband) no matter how long the filter. Tapering the truncation with a smooth
\term{window}---Hann, Hamming, Blackman, Kaiser---trades a wider transition for lower
sidelobes. The \term{Kaiser window} has an adjustable parameter $\beta$ and an empirical design
formula that gives $\beta$ and $N$ directly from $A_s$ and $\Delta f$. Windowed designs are quick
and robust, and adequate for many tasks, but not optimal: their ripple is largest near the band
edge and decays away from it, wasting attenuation where it is not needed.

\subsection{Optimal designs}

The \term{Parks--McClellan} algorithm (1972), based on the Remez exchange, designs the FIR filter
that minimises the maximum weighted error over the specified bands. The result is
\emph{equiripple}: the error oscillates with equal amplitude across each band, and for a given
length no other filter has lower peak error. \emph{Least-squares} designs instead minimise the
integrated squared error; they have slightly higher peaks but less total stopband energy, which
can be better when the stopband contains broadband noise rather than a single interferer.
Figure~\ref{fig:ch06_fir_design} compares window, least-squares and equiripple designs of the
same length. At 41 taps the equiripple filter gains about 15\,dB of minimum stopband
attenuation over a Hamming window with a similar transition.

\mdcfig{ch06_fir_design}{Four 41-tap low-pass designs with a transition band from 0.10 to 0.15
cycles/sample (shaded). The Parks--McClellan filter's stopband is equiripple; the windowed
designs have ripples that decay away from the band edge. Right: the equiripple impulse
response, symmetric and therefore exactly linear phase.}

\subsection{Linear phase}

An FIR filter with symmetric coefficients, $h[n]=h[N-1-n]$, has frequency response
$H(e^{j\omega})=A(\omega)e^{-j\omega(N-1)/2}$ with $A(\omega)$ real. Every frequency is delayed by
exactly $(N-1)/2$ samples, so pulse shapes pass undistorted. Symmetry also halves the multiplier
count: add $x[n-k]$ and $x[n-N+1+k]$ first, then multiply once. The four types of linear-phase
FIR (odd or even length, symmetric or antisymmetric) differ in whether they can have zeros at
DC and $f_s/2$; an antisymmetric filter is required for a Hilbert transformer or
differentiator.

\section{IIR filter design}

\subsection{From analog prototypes}

IIR filters are usually designed by transforming classic analog prototypes. The
\term{bilinear transform}
\begin{equation}
s=\frac{2}{T_s}\,\frac{1-z^{-1}}{1+z^{-1}}
\end{equation}
maps the entire $j\Omega$ axis onto the unit circle once, so there is no aliasing, but it warps
frequency as $\Omega=\frac{2}{T_s}\tan(\omega/2)$. The designer \emph{pre-warps} the critical
frequencies before designing the analog prototype. The prototypes are the classical families:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Butterworth}: maximally flat passband, monotonic, gentlest roll-off.
\item \textbf{Chebyshev type I}: equiripple passband, monotonic stopband, sharper.
\item \textbf{Chebyshev type II}: flat passband, equiripple stopband.
\item \textbf{Elliptic (Cauer)}: equiripple in both bands; the sharpest transition for a given
order, with zeros on the unit circle in the stopband.
\item \textbf{Bessel}: maximally flat group delay; nearly linear phase, very gentle magnitude
roll-off (and the bilinear transform spoils its phase property, so digital designs usually
approximate it directly).
\end{itemize}
Figure~\ref{fig:ch06_iir_compare} contrasts three sixth-order designs. The elliptic filter
reaches 60\,dB of attenuation in a small fraction of the transition the Butterworth needs, but
its group delay peaks sharply at the band edge---exactly the kind of phase distortion that would
cause intersymbol interference in a data receiver.

\mdcfig{ch06_iir_compare}{Sixth-order IIR low-pass filters with cutoff 0.1 cycles/sample.
Left: magnitude. Centre: group delay, which peaks at the band edge, most severely for the sharpest
design. Right: the elliptic filter's poles cluster near the unit circle and its zeros lie on it.}

\subsection{Implementation structures}

An $N$th-order IIR filter can be implemented as one difference equation (\emph{direct form})
or as a cascade of second-order sections (\term{biquads}). Mathematically they are equivalent.
Numerically they are not. The poles of a high-order polynomial are extremely sensitive to its
coefficients when they are clustered, as they are in any sharp filter. Figure~\ref{fig:ch06_coef_quant}
shows an eighth-order elliptic filter: rounding the direct-form coefficients to 16 or 12 bits
moves poles outside the unit circle and the filter becomes unstable, while the same filter as
four biquads with 12-bit coefficients is almost indistinguishable from the ideal. \emph{Always
implement IIR filters of order above two as cascaded biquads}, with poles paired to their
nearest zeros and sections ordered by increasing pole radius to control internal gain.

\mdcfig[0.72]{ch06_coef_quant}{Coefficient quantization of an eighth-order elliptic filter.
The direct form becomes unstable with 16-bit and 12-bit coefficients; the cascade of biquads
tolerates 12 bits with negligible change.}

\section{Fixed-point implementation}

Floating point is standard on CPUs and GPUs, but FPGAs and ASICs, where most radio DSP at high
sample rates lives, favour fixed point for its cost and power. A number in \term{Q-format}
$Q m.n$ has $m$ integer bits (including sign) and $n$ fractional bits; a 16-bit word in $Q1.15$
represents $[-1,1)$ with resolution $2^{-15}$. Four issues dominate fixed-point design:
\begin{description}
\item[Coefficient quantization] changes the response, as above. FIR filters degrade
gracefully: each bit of coefficient precision buys roughly 6\,dB of achievable stopband.
\item[Product rounding] adds noise at every multiplier. A $b\times b$ product has $2b$ bits,
and keeping only $b$ adds error of variance $2^{-2b}/12$. Accumulating products at full
precision and rounding once at the output---as FPGA DSP slices with 48-bit accumulators do---is
far better than rounding each product.
\item[Overflow] must be prevented by scaling or handled by saturation. Two's-complement
wrap-around is catastrophic in IIR filters (it can sustain large oscillations) but harmless in
intermediate FIR sums as long as the final result fits, a useful property.
\item[Limit cycles] are small self-sustained oscillations in IIR filters with zero input,
caused by rounding in the feedback path. Magnitude truncation or careful structure choice
suppresses them.
\end{description}

\begin{inpractice}[title=Counting the cost]
The cost of a filter in hardware is measured in multiply-accumulates per second (MAC/s). A
100-tap FIR filter at 100\,MS/s needs $10^{10}$\,MAC/s: about 25 DSP slices of an FPGA
clocked at 400\,MHz, or a large fraction of a CPU core using SIMD instructions. GNU Radio's
VOLK library selects the best SIMD kernel for each CPU at run time. The multirate techniques
below routinely reduce such budgets by factors of 10 to 100.
\end{inpractice}

\subsection{Fast convolution}

For long filters on processors, convolution via the FFT is cheaper. An $N$-tap filter costs $N$
MACs per sample directly, but only about $2\log_2(2N)$ complex operations per sample with the
FFT, using the \emph{overlap-add} or \emph{overlap-save} method to process a stream in blocks.
The crossover is typically between 32 and 128 taps. GNU Radio's \texttt{fft\_filter} blocks use
overlap-save; OFDM receivers are, in a sense, a fast convolution built into the waveform.

\section{Multirate signal processing}

\subsection{Decimation and interpolation}

To \term{decimate} by an integer $M$ is to keep every $M$th sample. It divides the sample rate
by $M$ and, as Chapter~\ref{ch:pcm} warned, aliases everything between the new Nyquist
frequency $f_s/2M$ and the old one into the band. A decimator is therefore a low-pass filter
followed by a downsampler (Figure~\ref{fig:ch06_decimation}). To \term{interpolate} by $L$ is to
insert $L-1$ zeros between samples, which creates $L-1$ images of the spectrum, followed by a
low-pass filter that removes them. A rational rate change by $L/M$ interpolates by $L$ and then
decimates by $M$ with a single filter whose cutoff is the smaller of the two Nyquist frequencies.

\mdcfig{ch06_decimation}{Decimation by 4. Downsampling without filtering folds an out-of-band
tone at 0.27 cycles/sample into the band of interest; filtering first removes it.}

\subsection{The noble identities and polyphase decomposition}

The naive decimator is wasteful: it computes $M$ filter outputs and throws $M-1$ away. The
\term{noble identities} state that filtering by $H(z^M)$ and then downsampling by $M$ equals
downsampling and then filtering by $H(z)$ (and dually for upsampling). Combined with the
\term{polyphase decomposition}
\begin{equation}
H(z)=\sum_{p=0}^{M-1}z^{-p}E_p(z^M),\qquad e_p[k]=h[kM+p],
\end{equation}
they turn a decimating filter into $M$ short subfilters, each running at the \emph{output} rate
and fed by a commutator that deals input samples to them in turn. The computation drops by a
factor of $M$ with no change in the result. The same trick applies to interpolation: the $L$
polyphase subfilters produce the $L$ output phases, and the zeros are never multiplied.
Polyphase structures are the foundation of efficient DSP, and GNU Radio's
\texttt{pfb\_} (polyphase filter bank) blocks, used in Chapter~\ref{ch:sync} for symbol timing
recovery, are named after them.

\subsection{Half-band filters}

A \term{half-band filter} has its transition centred on $f_s/4$ with symmetric passband and
stopband ripples. Every second coefficient except the centre one is exactly zero, so it costs
about a quarter of a general filter of the same length. Cascades of half-band filters, each
decimating by 2, are the standard way to decimate by powers of two: the AD9364 in the B200
contains a chain of them (HB1, HB2, HB3) before its programmable FIR, and so does every digital
down-converter IC.

\subsection{CIC filters}

When the decimation ratio is large---hundreds or thousands---even polyphase FIR filters become
expensive. Eugene Hogenauer's \term{cascaded integrator-comb} (CIC) filter (1981) needs no
multipliers at all. $N$ integrators run at the high input rate, the signal is downsampled by $R$,
and $N$ comb stages $y[n]=x[n]-x[n-1]$ run at the low rate. The overall response is
\begin{equation}
H(e^{j\omega})=\left[\frac{\sin(\omega R/2)}{R\sin(\omega/2)}\right]^N,
\end{equation}
whose nulls fall exactly at the frequencies that alias onto DC after decimation
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch06_cic}). The price is passband droop, corrected by a short compensation FIR
at the low rate, and bit growth of $N\log_2R$ bits in the integrators, which relies on
two's-complement wrap-around being harmless. CIC decimators are the first stage in the B200's
FPGA and in almost every wideband digital receiver.

\mdcfig[0.72]{ch06_cic}{CIC decimator magnitude response for decimation $R=16$ and one, three
and five stages, plotted against the output sample rate. The nulls sit on the bands that alias to
DC; more stages give deeper nulls and more droop.}

\subsection{Multistage design}

Because FIR cost scales as $A_s/(\Delta f/f_s)$, one large decimation is best done in stages:
first a cheap filter with a wide transition (a CIC or half-band) at the high rate, then sharper
filters at progressively lower rates, where their relative transition width is larger. Designing
a decimate-by-64 channel filter as $2\times2\times16$ rather than one 64 can cut the computation
by an order of magnitude. This is the architecture of the digital down-converter:
NCO mixer, CIC, half-band cascade, final channel FIR.

\subsection{Arbitrary and fractional resampling}

Real systems often need irrational or awkward ratios: 48\,kHz audio to 44.1\,kHz ($147/160$), a
61.44\,MS/s LTE clock to a 56\,MS/s processing rate, or continuous tracking of a clock that drifts
by parts per million. Two methods cover these cases. A \emph{polyphase resampler} with many phases
(say 32 or 128) picks, for each output sample, the subfilter nearest to the required fractional
delay. A \term{Farrow} structure computes the interpolating filter as a polynomial in the
fractional delay $\mu$, so any delay can be synthesised from a few fixed subfilters and a
Horner evaluation. The cubic Lagrange interpolator used for timing recovery in
Chapter~\ref{ch:sync} and in \texttt{commlib} is a Farrow structure.

\section{Filter banks and channelizers}

A bank of $M$ bandpass filters that splits a wideband input into $M$ adjacent channels would
naively cost $M$ times one filter. The \term{polyphase DFT filter bank} computes all of them for
roughly the cost of one polyphase decimator plus an $M$-point FFT per output sample: the polyphase
subfilters of a common low-pass prototype feed an FFT, whose outputs are the $M$ channels
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch06_polyphase}). Channelizers built this way sit inside satellite payloads
that route hundreds of carriers, in spectrum-monitoring receivers, in radio telescopes, and in
software base stations that process many narrowband carriers from one wideband ADC.
Oversampled and non-maximally decimated variants avoid the aliasing at channel edges; the
perfect-reconstruction synthesis banks that recombine channels are the ancestors of the
filter-bank multicarrier (FBMC) waveforms studied for 5G.

\mdcfig[0.72]{ch06_polyphase}{An 8-channel uniform filter bank: every channel is a frequency-shifted
copy of one prototype low-pass filter, and a polyphase-plus-FFT structure computes them all at once.}

\section{Oscillators and rotations}

\subsection{The numerically controlled oscillator}

Digital mixing multiplies samples by $e^{-j2\pi f_0 n/f_s}$. A \term{numerically controlled
oscillator} (NCO), or direct digital synthesiser (DDS), keeps a $B$-bit phase accumulator that
adds a frequency word $F$ each clock, giving output frequency $f_0=F f_s/2^B$ with resolution
$f_s/2^B$---millihertz at 100\,MS/s with a 32-bit accumulator. The top $P$ bits address a sine
look-up table. Truncating the phase to $P$ bits creates periodic phase errors and hence spurs,
about $6P$\,dB below the carrier (Figure~\ref{fig:ch06_nco_spurs}); phase dithering converts the
spurs into a noise floor. Amplitude quantization in the table adds its own floor. NCOs tune every
digital down-converter and generate the chirps of FMCW radars and the carriers of many
transmitters.

\mdcfig{ch06_nco_spurs}{Output spectra of an NCO with a 32-bit accumulator when the phase is
truncated to 8 and to 14 bits before the sine look-up. Each additional phase bit lowers the spurs
by about 6\,dB.}

\subsection{CORDIC}

Jack Volder's \term{CORDIC} algorithm (1959), invented for the B-58 bomber's navigation computer,
rotates a vector by an arbitrary angle using only shifts and adds: at stage $i$ it rotates by
$\pm\arctan 2^{-i}$, choosing the sign to drive the residual angle toward zero. After $b$ stages
the result is accurate to about $b$ bits, scaled by a known constant $K\approx1.6468$. Run in
\emph{rotation mode} it is a mixer or an NCO; in \emph{vectoring mode} it computes magnitude and
phase ($\sqrt{I^2+Q^2}$ and $\arctan(Q/I)$), which is exactly what AM and FM demodulators,
AGC loops and phase detectors need. CORDIC engines appear in FPGA IP libraries and in the
digital front ends of most radio chips.

\subsection{Two more useful tools}

The \emph{Goertzel algorithm} computes a single DFT bin with one second-order recursion per
sample; it is the classic detector for dual-tone multi-frequency (DTMF) telephone signalling and
is cheaper than an FFT when only a few bins are needed. The \emph{DC blocker}
$H(z)=(1-z^{-1})/(1-\alpha z^{-1})$ with $\alpha$ close to 1 is a single-pole high-pass that removes
the residual DC offset of a direct-conversion receiver; it is one of the few IIR filters found
in almost every SDR data path.

\begin{keyidea}[title=Rules of thumb for radio DSP]
\begin{itemize}
\item Cost of an FIR $\approx A_s/(22\,\Delta f/f_s)$ taps: run sharp filters at low rates.
\item Decimate early and in stages: CIC, then half-bands, then the channel filter.
\item Use polyphase structures so no multiplier ever computes a discarded sample or multiplies a
stuffed zero.
\item Implement IIR filters as biquads; accumulate at full precision and round once.
\item Budget spurs: every bit of NCO phase is 6\,dB, every bit of coefficient 6\,dB of stopband.
\end{itemize}
\end{keyidea}
