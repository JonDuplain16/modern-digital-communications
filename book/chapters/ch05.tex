\chapter{Sampling, Quantization and Digital Telephony}
\label{ch:pcm}

\begin{chapterintro}
Pick up a phone and say hello. Within a few millionths of a second your voice has stopped being a
voice. A microphone has turned air pressure into a wobbling voltage, a chip has measured that
voltage eight thousand times a second, and each measurement has been squeezed into a single
byte. From then on, all the way to your friend's ear, there is no voice anywhere in the
network---only numbers, marching in single file at 64\,000 bits per second, exactly as they
have since a Bell System crew lit up the first T1 line in 1962. Put a compact disc in a player and
the same thing happens in reverse, 44\,100 times a second on each channel.

This chapter is about that boundary between the analog world and the digital one: what is lost
and what is kept when a continuous waveform becomes a list of numbers. We shall see why
sampling, done right, loses \emph{nothing}, and why the camera that films a wagon wheel turning
backwards is the same villain as the alias in your radio. We shall round numbers the way a
nurse rounds your height, add noise on purpose to make music sound \emph{cleaner}, borrow a
camera's auto-exposure trick to give an 8-bit telephone code the quiet-signal precision of 13
bits, and meet the sigma-delta converter---a fast, sloppy painter who corrects every stroke and
ends up more accurate than any careful one. We shall open up real converters and read their data
sheets, and then follow the bytes into the first great digital network: the T1 and E1 frames,
the switches that re-sort calls like a post office, the echo cancellers, the hierarchy, ISDN,
voice over IP and the sampling rates of digital audio, from the compact disc to DSD. Every later
chapter of this book assumes that the conversion described here has already happened.
\end{chapterintro}

\subsection{Two approximations, kept apart}

A microphone produces a voltage that varies continuously in time and can take any value in a
range. A computer can store only a finite list of numbers, each drawn from a finite set. Getting
from the first to the second takes two quite different approximations, and the single most useful
habit this chapter can give you is to keep them separate in your head
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_two_approx}):
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Sampling} makes \emph{time} discrete. It replaces the waveform $x(t)$ by the sequence
$x[n]=x(nT_s)$. Done correctly, on a band-limited signal, it loses \emph{nothing}: the
waveform can be rebuilt exactly from the samples.
\item \emph{Quantization} makes \emph{amplitude} discrete. It replaces each sample by the nearest of a
finite set of levels. It \emph{always} loses something, and the art is to lose only what
does not matter.
\end{itemize}
A third step, \emph{coding}, writes each quantized level as a group of bits. Together the
three steps are \term{pulse-code modulation} (PCM), the name Alec Reeves gave the idea in 1937.

\mdcfig{ch05_two_approx}{The two approximations of digitisation. (a) Sampling keeps the exact
amplitude but only at instants $nT_s$. (b) Quantization keeps every instant but rounds the
amplitude to a grid of levels. (c) PCM does both: the waveform becomes a list of grid points, each
of which can be written as a few bits.}

It is worth pausing on how strange the first claim is. Between two samples there are infinitely
many instants, and the sampler throws all of them away. How can nothing be lost? The answer, which
occupies Section~\ref{sec:ch05:sampling}, is that a signal with limited bandwidth cannot wiggle
arbitrarily fast, so its values between samples are pinned down by the samples themselves---as
the shape of a taut rope is pinned down by the positions of the hands holding it. Quantization
enjoys no such reprieve: once 0.3721\,V has been written down as ``0.37'', the 0.0021 is gone
for ever. Most of this chapter is about making that loss small, cheap and harmless.

Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_pcm_chain} shows the complete chain, from microphone to loudspeaker, with each
block labelled by the section of this chapter that analyses it. Notice that only one block, the
quantizer, is irreversible by nature. Everything else is lossless in principle and lossy only
through its imperfections---which, as we shall see, are plenty.

\subsection{Why bother? Regeneration}

PCM's advantage is not efficiency. A telephone voice channel that occupies 4\,kHz as an analog
signal becomes 64\,kb/s, which needs at least 32\,kHz of bandwidth even with ideal pulses
(Chapter~\ref{ch:baseband}). Eight times the spectrum for the same conversation sounds like a
terrible bargain. Its advantage is that a stream of bits can be \emph{regenerated}. An analog
amplifier in the middle of a long line cannot tell the voice from the hiss that has crept in, so it
amplifies both, and every hop adds more. A digital repeater only has to decide whether each pulse
is a one or a zero; if it decides correctly, it sends a perfect new copy onward and the noise
simply vanishes (Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_regeneration}). A call across a continent then sounds as
clean as a call across the street. Chapter~\ref{ch:history} (Section~\ref{sec:ch01:digital})
quantifies this: a chain of digital regenerators needs some 50\,dB less signal-to-noise ratio per
span than a chain of analog amplifiers.

\mdcfig{ch05_regeneration}{Why the telephone network went digital. Top: an analog signal relayed
through 1, 10 and 50 amplifiers accumulates the noise of every hop. Bottom: a digital signal
arrives just as noisy at each repeater (grey), but the repeater decides each bit and sends a
clean copy (navy), so the noise never accumulates.}

Once signals are numbers, they can also be switched by logic, stored without decay, copied without
loss, encrypted, compressed and protected by error-correcting codes. Every one of those tricks
needs the conversion in this chapter first.

\begin{history}[Alec Reeves and the philosophy of PCM]
Alec Reeves, an English engineer at International Telephone and Telegraph's laboratory in
Paris, conceived PCM in 1937 while working on multichannel radio links, and patented it in
France in 1938 (patent 852\,183) and later in Britain and the United States (US 2\,272\,070,
1942). His insight was that a noisy channel need only distinguish between ``pulse'' and ``no
pulse'', so that a signal sent as a code of pulses could be cleaned up completely at each
repeater. With vacuum tubes the idea was impractical for commercial telephony---each
channel would have needed racks of electronics---and Reeves, who also worked on radar
navigation during the war, lived to see it adopted only in the 1960s. The theory caught up
first. In 1948 Bernard Oliver, John Pierce and Claude Shannon of Bell Labs published ``The
philosophy of PCM'', which showed that PCM exchanges bandwidth for signal-to-noise ratio
\emph{exponentially}---each extra bit per sample improves the quantization SNR by 6\,dB at
the cost of a linear increase in bandwidth---whereas FM (Chapter~\ref{ch:analog}) makes the
same exchange only quadratically. The paper is short, clear and still worth reading. The
wartime SIGSALY system and the 1962 T1 carrier, the first operational and the first
commercial digital voice systems, are described in Chapter~\ref{ch:history}.
\end{history}

The numbers that come out of this chapter turn up everywhere in engineering, from the 125\,$\mu$s
frame that still clocks the world's optical networks to the 44.1\,kHz of every music file.
Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_by_numbers}, at the start of the chapter, collects the most famous of them; by
the end of the chapter you will know where each one comes from. Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_timeline}
sets the story in time.

\subsection{The wagon wheel that turns backwards}

Every child who has watched a Western has seen it: the stagecoach races forward, and its wheels
slowly turn \emph{backwards}. Nothing is wrong with the wheels. The film camera takes 24
pictures a second, and between two pictures each wheel turns almost---but not quite---a whole
number of spoke spacings. Your brain joins up the nearest spokes from frame to frame and
concludes, quite logically, that the wheel crept back a little.

Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_wagon_wheel} makes the trick precise. A wheel with one red spoke turns
$324^\circ$ forward between frames. The camera, which sees only the snapshots, cannot tell this
from a wheel turning $36^\circ$ \emph{backwards}, and the backwards story is the one it tells,
because it is the slowest motion consistent with the pictures. That is the whole of aliasing in
one image: \emph{a sampler cannot distinguish a fast motion from a slow one that lands in the same
places}. The rest of this section turns that observation into the most important theorem in
signal processing, and then into the practical rules that keep every converter honest.

\mdcfig{ch05_wagon_wheel}{The wagon-wheel effect. Between frames the wheel turns $324^\circ$
forward, but in the frames the red spoke appears to step $36^\circ$ backwards---the smallest motion
that fits the snapshots. A sampler always reports the slowest explanation of what it sees.}

\begin{analogy}[A movie camera filming a spinning wheel]
A sampler is a movie camera pointed at a waveform. If the scene changes slowly compared with the
frame rate, the film tells the truth. If it changes too fast, the film tells a convincing lie:
the fast motion is recorded as a slow one---a wheel turning backwards, a helicopter rotor frozen in
mid-air, a fan that seems to stand still under a strobe light. The rule for an honest film is that
the fastest thing in the scene must take at least two frames per cycle. The analogy's limit: a
camera can blur motion during its exposure, whereas the ideal sampler takes an instantaneous
snapshot, which is why the anti-alias \emph{filter} in front of it is not optional.
\end{analogy}

\subsection{Statement and proof}

\begin{keyidea}[title=The sampling theorem]
A signal $x(t)$ with no energy at frequencies $|f|\ge W$ is completely determined by its
samples $x(nT_s)$ taken at rate $f_s=1/T_s>2W$, and can be reconstructed exactly by
\begin{equation}
x(t)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}x(nT_s)\,\sinc\big(f_s(t-nT_s)\big).
\label{eq:ch05:interp}
\end{equation}
\end{keyidea}

The rate $2W$ is the \term{Nyquist rate} and half the sampling rate, $f_s/2$, is the
\term{Nyquist frequency}. Read~\eqref{eq:ch05:interp} in words: \emph{put a sinc pulse on every
sample, scale it by the sample's value, and add them all up}. Each sinc is exactly 1 at its own
sample instant and exactly 0 at every other, so the sum passes through every sample without the
pulses disturbing one another. Between samples the sincs overlap and blend, and their sum fills in
the one and only curve with bandwidth below $f_s/2$ that goes through all the dots
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_sinc_sum}). It is the band-limited version of joining the dots---and,
remarkably, it is exact.

\mdcfig{ch05_sinc_sum}{Sinc interpolation at work. Each sample (red dot) launches its own sinc
pulse (thin coloured curves), scaled by the sample value and zero at every other sample instant.
Their sum (navy) is the unique band-limited waveform through all the samples.}

Why must the rate exceed $2W$? Think of the spectrum. Sampling, as we shall prove in a moment,
makes copies of the signal's spectrum at every multiple of $f_s$, like the repeating pattern of a
wallpaper. If the spectrum is $2W$ wide and the copies are spaced more than $2W$ apart, they sit
side by side with gaps between them, and a low-pass filter can cut out the original cleanly. If
the copies are closer than $2W$, their edges overlap and add, and no filter can ever separate them
again (Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_sampling_spectra}). That overlap is aliasing; the wagon wheel is what
it looks like in time.

The proof deserves to be done carefully once, because every practical refinement in this chapter
is a variation on it. The key equation of the proof is worth carrying out of the box: the spectrum
of the sampled signal is
\begin{equation}
X_s(f)=f_s\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}X(f-kf_s),
\label{eq:ch05:replicas}
\end{equation}
the original spectrum plus copies---\emph{images} or \emph{replicas}---centred on every multiple of
$f_s$.

\begin{deeper}[Proving the sampling theorem in three steps]
\textbf{Step 1: sampling replicates the spectrum.} Represent ideal sampling as
multiplication by an impulse train, $x_s(t)=x(t)\sum_n\delta(t-nT_s)=\sum_n x(nT_s)\,
\delta(t-nT_s)$. The impulse train is periodic, so it has the Fourier series
$\sum_n\delta(t-nT_s)=f_s\sum_k e^{j2\pi kf_st}$ (Chapter~\ref{ch:signals}). Multiplying $x(t)$
by $e^{j2\pi kf_st}$ shifts its spectrum by $kf_s$, which gives~\eqref{eq:ch05:replicas}. The same
result, written for the sequence $x[n]$ rather than the impulse train, is the \emph{Poisson
summation formula}: the discrete-time Fourier transform of the samples is
$\sum_nx(nT_s)e^{-j2\pi fnT_s}=f_s\sum_kX(f-kf_s)$, a periodic function of $f$ with period $f_s$.

\smallskip
\textbf{Step 2: if the replicas do not overlap, one of them can be isolated.} If
$X(f)=0$ for $|f|\ge W$ and $f_s>2W$, the replica at $k=0$ occupies $|f|<W$ and its nearest
neighbours start at $f_s-W>W$. Multiplying $X_s(f)$ by an ideal low-pass response
$H(f)=T_s\,\mathrm{rect}(f/f_s)$, which is $T_s$ for $|f|<f_s/2$ and zero elsewhere, keeps
exactly $X(f)$.

\smallskip
\textbf{Step 3: the filter's impulse response gives the interpolation formula.} The
impulse response of $H(f)$ is $h(t)=\sinc(f_st)$, with $\sinc(u)=\sin(\pi u)/(\pi u)$.
Convolving it with the impulse train $x_s(t)$ places one sinc at each sample, weighted by the
sample value, which is~\eqref{eq:ch05:interp}.

\smallskip
\textbf{Two further facts.} First, the shifted sincs $\phi_n(t)=\sinc(f_s(t-nT_s))$
are \emph{orthogonal}, with $\int\phi_m\phi_n\,dt=T_s\,\delta_{mn}$, so~\eqref{eq:ch05:interp} is
an orthogonal expansion and the samples are (scaled) coordinates of $x(t)$ in that basis. The
energy is conserved: $\int|x(t)|^2dt=T_s\sum_n|x(nT_s)|^2$. A band-limited signal of duration
roughly $T$ therefore has about $2WT$ degrees of freedom, the dimension count that Shannon
used to derive channel capacity (Chapter~\ref{ch:infotheory}). Second, the condition
$f_s>2W$ is strict. A sinusoid exactly at $W=f_s/2$ sampled at its zero crossings gives a
sequence of zeros; at sampling rate exactly $2W$ the theorem holds only for signals whose
spectrum contains no impulse at $\pm W$.
\end{deeper}

\mdcfig{ch05_sampling_spectra}{Sampling in the frequency domain. Sampling replicates the
spectrum at multiples of $f_s$. Above the Nyquist rate the replicas separate and the
original can be recovered by low-pass filtering (green band); below it they overlap and the
sum is permanently corrupted.}

\begin{history}[A theorem with many fathers]
E.~T.~Whittaker published the interpolation series~\eqref{eq:ch05:interp}, his ``cardinal
function'', in 1915, as a problem in pure mathematics. Harry Nyquist showed in 1928 that a
telegraph channel of bandwidth $W$ can carry at most $2W$ independent pulse amplitudes per
second, the dual statement (Chapter~\ref{ch:baseband}). Vladimir Kotelnikov proved the theorem
explicitly for communications in a 1933 paper for the first All-Union Conference on
communications in Moscow, and in Russia it is still called Kotelnikov's theorem. Herbert Raabe
treated time-division multiplexing of sampled signals in his 1939 dissertation in Berlin, Isao
Someya published the theorem in Japan in 1949, and Claude Shannon stated and proved it in its
modern form in ``Communication in the presence of noise'', also in 1949, with characteristic
economy and a footnote acknowledging that it was ``common knowledge in the communication
art''. Names attached to it vary by country: Shannon, Nyquist--Shannon, WKS
(Whittaker--Kotelnikov--Shannon) or simply ``the sampling theorem''.
\end{history}

\subsection{Aliasing}

The overlap has a name, \term{aliasing}, because a frequency component masquerades as a
different one, like a spy travelling under an alias. A sinusoid at $f_0$ sampled at $f_s$
produces exactly the same samples as one at $f_0+kf_s$ for any integer $k$, and (for real signals)
as one at $kf_s-f_0$: $\cos(2\pi(kf_s\pm f_0)nT_s)=\cos(2\pi kn\pm2\pi f_0nT_s)=\cos(2\pi f_0nT_s)$.
The extra $2\pi kn$ is a whole number of turns---exactly the wagon wheel's ``almost a whole number
of spokes''. The apparent frequency of a real tone is found by \emph{folding}:
\begin{equation}
f_{\text{alias}}=\big|f_0-f_s\,\mathrm{round}(f_0/f_s)\big|,
\label{eq:ch05:fold}
\end{equation}
which maps any input frequency into $[0,f_s/2]$ like a sheet of paper folded at every multiple
of $f_s/2$ (Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_folding}). Climb the input frequency from zero and the apparent
frequency climbs with it until $f_s/2$, then turns round and comes back down to zero at $f_s$,
then up again: a zigzag, for ever.

\mdcpairany{ch05_folding}{The folding rule~\eqref{eq:ch05:fold} for $f_s=10$\,Hz. Input frequency
on the horizontal axis, what the samples say on the vertical. Every tone lands somewhere in
$[0,f_s/2]$; 9\,Hz and 21\,Hz both masquerade as 1\,Hz.}{ch05_strobe_ball}{Sampling made
visible: a bouncing ball photographed under a strobe light flashing at a fixed rate. The flashes
are samples; because the ball moves slowly compared with the flash rate, the arcs are easy to
reconstruct by eye.}

In Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_aliasing_time} a 9\,Hz cosine sampled at 10 samples per second is
indistinguishable from a 1\,Hz cosine: look at the black dots and try to decide which curve they
came from. The wagon wheels in Western films (24 frames per second), the strobe light that freezes
a spinning fan, and the moir\'e shimmer of a striped shirt on television are all the same
phenomenon.

\mdcfig{ch05_aliasing_time}{Aliasing in time: a 9\,Hz cosine (blue) and a 1\,Hz cosine
(dashed) pass through exactly the same samples at 10 samples per second.}

Once aliasing has happened, no processing can undo it, because the information needed to tell
the two frequencies apart was never recorded. A converter must therefore be preceded by an
\term{anti-aliasing filter} that removes everything that would fold into the band of interest
\emph{before} sampling. Notice the precise requirement: energy above $f_s/2$ is harmless if it
folds onto frequencies you will discard later. A component at frequency $f$ lands on
$f_s-f$, so to protect a wanted band $0\le f\le f_p$ the filter must attenuate only
frequencies above $f_s-f_p$, not above $f_s/2$. This observation is what makes oversampling
so powerful.

\subsection{Anti-aliasing filters in practice}
\label{sec:ch05:aa}

No real filter has a brick-wall response, so practical systems sample faster than the Nyquist
rate and leave a \term{transition band} between the edge of the wanted band $f_p$ and the
first frequency that can alias into it, $f_s-f_p$. Telephone speech, band-limited to
300--3400\,Hz, is sampled at 8\,kHz rather than 6.8\,kHz, leaving a transition band from 3.4
to 4.6\,kHz. CD audio, band-limited to 20\,kHz, is sampled at 44.1\,kHz, leaving only
20 to 24.1\,kHz. The ratio of the sample rate to the Nyquist rate, the \term{oversampling
ratio} (OSR), is the designer's lever: more oversampling means an easier analog filter and
more work for the digital hardware that follows.

How steep must the filter be? The required stopband attenuation is set by the dynamic range:
any interferer that aliases into the band must end up below the noise floor of the
converter, so a 16-bit system needs roughly 90 to 100\,dB. Think of it as a doorman who must turn
away everyone above a certain height: the closer the tallest welcome guest is to the shortest
unwelcome one, the sharper-eyed the doorman must be. For a Butterworth (maximally flat)
response of order $n$, the attenuation at frequency $f$ relative to a passband edge $f_p$
where the loss is $A_p$\,dB is $10\log_{10}[1+\varepsilon^2(f/f_p)^{2n}]$ with
$\varepsilon^2=10^{A_p/10}-1$, so the order needed to reach $A_s$\,dB at $f_{\text{st}}$ is
\begin{equation}
n\ge\frac{\log_{10}\big[(10^{A_s/10}-1)/(10^{A_p/10}-1)\big]}{2\log_{10}(f_{\text{st}}/f_p)}.
\label{eq:ch05:buttord}
\end{equation}
The denominator is the killer: the order is inversely proportional to the logarithm of the
transition ratio, and a ratio of 1.2 (CD audio) is very close to 1. The four classical
families trade differently:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Butterworth}: flat passband, monotonic, moderate group-delay variation, highest
order for a given transition band.
\item \emph{Chebyshev type I}: equiripple passband, much steeper skirt for the same order,
worse group delay.
\item \emph{Elliptic (Cauer)}: ripple in both bands and transmission zeros in the stopband;
the lowest possible order for a given specification, and the worst phase response.
\item \emph{Bessel}: maximally flat group delay, hence the best pulse response and the worst
selectivity; used where waveform shape matters more than rejection, as in measurement and
some data receivers.
\end{itemize}

\mdcfig{ch05_aa_filter}{Left: analog filter order needed to pass 0--20\,kHz with 0.1\,dB ripple
and attenuate everything that could alias into that band by 96\,dB, against sampling rate (in
multiples of 44.1\,kHz). At $1\times$ even an elliptic filter needs order 11 and a Butterworth
order 70; at $64\times$ a third-order filter suffices. Right: the two solutions in the
frequency domain.}

Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_aa_filter} computes the orders for CD audio. A Butterworth filter would
need order 70---absurd---and even the elliptic filter needs order 11, a large network of
precision inductors or op-amp sections whose phase response is far from linear near
20\,kHz. Early CD recorders and players used exactly such filters (``brick-wall'' filters
were a selling point in the brochures), and their phase distortion and component sensitivity
were the source of much of the criticism of early digital audio.

\begin{worked}[Anti-aliasing filter for CD audio]
A recorder samples at $f_s=44.1$\,kHz and must preserve 0--20\,kHz within 0.1\,dB while
suppressing aliases by 96\,dB (the range of 16 bits). The first frequency that aliases into
the passband is $44.1-20=24.1$\,kHz, so the transition ratio is $24.1/20=1.205$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:ch05:buttord} gives
\[
n\ge\frac{\log_{10}\big[(10^{9.6}-1)/(10^{0.01}-1)\big]}{2\log_{10}1.205}
=\frac{9.6+1.63}{2\times0.081}\approx69.4,
\]
so a 70th-order Butterworth filter. Standard design formulas for elliptic filters
(as in \texttt{scipy.signal.ellipord}) give order 11.

Now oversample by 64, to 2.8224\,MHz. Aliases into 0--20\,kHz now come only from above
$2.8224-0.02=2.80$\,MHz, a transition ratio of 140. The Butterworth order drops to
$\lceil 11.23/(2\log_{10}140)\rceil=\lceil 2.6\rceil=3$. A third-order RC filter with a
corner near 100\,kHz does the analog job, and a digital decimation filter running on the
oversampled stream, which can be made linear-phase and as sharp as desired, provides the
0.1\,dB to 96\,dB transition between 20 and 24.1\,kHz before the rate is reduced to
44.1\,kHz.
\end{worked}

\mdcfig[0.5]{ch05_complex_sampling}{Real against complex sampling. A real signal's spectrum is
symmetric, so its replicas mirror each other and only $0$ to $f_s/2$ is new information. A complex
(I/Q) signal can be lopsided: the whole band from $-f_s/2$ to $f_s/2$ carries independent
information.}

\begin{inpractice}[Where the anti-alias filter went]
Every modern audio ADC, from a smartphone codec to a studio converter, is an oversampling
sigma-delta converter (Section~\ref{sec:ch05:sigmadelta}) running at 64 to 256 times the
output rate, with a simple first- or second-order RC filter in front and a multistage
digital decimation filter behind. The steep filter has moved from the analog domain, where
it costs precision components and phase distortion, to the digital domain, where it costs
only gates and is exactly linear-phase. Software-defined radios do the same thing at radio
frequencies: the AD9364 transceiver in the USRP B200 (the single-channel member of Analog
Devices' AD9361 family) samples at several hundred megahertz and decimates by up to 48 in
half-band and programmable FIR stages, so that its analog baseband filter needs only a few
poles. The multirate techniques that make this cheap---polyphase decimators, half-band filters
and CIC filters---are the subject of Chapter~\ref{ch:dsp}.
\end{inpractice}

\subsection{Sampling real and complex signals}

A real signal with bandwidth $W$ needs $f_s>2W$ real samples per second. A complex baseband
signal occupying $-W/2<f<W/2$ needs $f_s>W$ complex samples per second: its two real
components, I and Q, each need $f_s>W$, so the total real sample rate is the same $2W$. There
is no free lunch, but there is a practical advantage: with complex sampling the spectrum
need not be symmetric about zero, so a receiver can see a signal and its mirror image as
different things (Chapter~\ref{ch:sdr}). Equation~\eqref{eq:ch05:fold} becomes simpler, too:
a complex sampler aliases $f_0$ to $f_0-f_s\,\mathrm{round}(f_0/f_s)$ in $[-f_s/2,f_s/2)$ with no
folding, because positive and negative frequencies are distinct. In wagon-wheel terms, a complex
sampler is a camera that can also tell clockwise from anticlockwise. This is why the
\texttt{rate} parameter of an SDR equals its usable bandwidth (minus the filter transition
bands), while a real-sampling audio card at the same rate captures only half as much
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_complex_sampling}).

\subsection{Bandpass sampling and Nyquist zones}
\label{sec:ch05:bandpass}

Here is a puzzle that has saved radio designers millions. A cellular base station must digitise a
20\,MHz-wide signal sitting at 70\,MHz. The theorem as stated seems to demand more than 160\,MS/s.
Yet engineers routinely do it at 56\,MS/s---well below the highest frequency in the signal. How?

\mdcfig[0.5]{ch05_iq_fs4}{Why IF samplers love $f_s/4$. Sampled four times per cycle, a cosine is the sequence $1,0,-1,0,\dots$ and a sine $0,1,0,-1,\dots$, so the digital down-converter needs no multipliers at all---only sign changes and the selection of alternate samples (Section~\ref{sec:ch05:bandpass}).}

The theorem as stated is for low-pass signals, but a signal that occupies only a band from
$f_L$ to $f_H=f_L+B$ carries information at a rate governed by $B$, not $f_H$. Aliasing, the villain
of the previous pages, can be turned into a servant. Divide the frequency axis into \term{Nyquist
zones} of width $f_s/2$: zone 1 is $[0,f_s/2]$, zone 2 is $[f_s/2,f_s]$, zone $k$ is
$[(k-1)f_s/2,\,kf_s/2]$. By~\eqref{eq:ch05:fold}, the sampler folds every zone onto zone 1, odd
zones directly and even zones mirrored (spectrally inverted). If the whole signal band lies inside
one zone, its replicas interleave without overlapping and the band appears cleanly in zone 1. That
is \term{bandpass sampling}, also called \emph{undersampling} or \emph{harmonic sampling}. It is the
strobe light used deliberately: a strobe flashing at almost the speed of a spinning fan makes the
fan appear to turn slowly, so you can inspect it.

The band $[f_L,f_H]$ fits inside zone $k$ if $(k-1)f_s/2\le f_L$ and $f_H\le kf_s/2$, which gives
the classical condition
\begin{equation}
\frac{2f_H}{k}\le f_s\le\frac{2f_L}{k-1},\qquad k=1,2,\dots,\lfloor f_H/B\rfloor.
\label{eq:ch05:bandpass}
\end{equation}
The minimum possible rate, at $k=\lfloor f_H/B\rfloor$, is close to $2B$---twice the
bandwidth, as the theorem promised---but it is reached only when $f_H$ is an integer multiple
of $B$, and then only with zero margin. Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_nyquist_zones} (right) plots the
allowed rates as white wedges: each wedge narrows towards its apex, so the higher the zone,
the more precisely the sample rate must be controlled and the narrower the guard bands. In
even zones the digitised spectrum is inverted, which the DSP can undo by negating every other
sample (multiplying by $(-1)^n$, a shift of $f_s/2$) or simply by conjugating the complex
baseband signal later.

\mdcfig{ch05_nyquist_zones}{Left: bandpass sampling of a 70\,MHz IF of 20\,MHz bandwidth at
56\,MS/s. The signal lies in the third Nyquist zone; sampling folds it to 4--24\,MHz, centred on
$f_s/4=14$\,MHz, with images (pale) mirrored about every multiple of $f_s/2$. The bandpass
anti-alias filter must remove everything in the neighbouring zones. Right: the allowed
sampling rates~\eqref{eq:ch05:bandpass} normalised to the bandwidth, as a function of the band
position $f_H/B$; grey regions cause overlap.}

A favourite choice in IF-sampling receivers puts the band centre $f_c$ at exactly a quarter of
the sample rate after folding, $f_s=4f_c/(2m+1)$ for integer $m$. Digital down-conversion
then needs no multipliers, because the samples of $\cos(2\pi nf_s/4\cdot1/f_s)$ are the sequence
$1,0,-1,0,\dots$ and those of the sine are $0,1,0,-1,\dots$. The I and Q streams are simply
alternate samples with alternating signs.

\begin{worked}[Bandpass sampling a 70\,MHz IF]
A receiver's IF strip delivers a signal of bandwidth $B=20$\,MHz centred on 70\,MHz, so
$f_L=60$\,MHz and $f_H=80$\,MHz. Which sample rates work, and which would you choose?

Here $f_H/B=4$, so $k\le4$. Equation~\eqref{eq:ch05:bandpass} gives
\begin{itemize}
\item $k=4$: $40\le f_s\le40$\,MHz, a single rate with no guard band at all; useless in practice.
\item $k=3$: $53.3\le f_s\le60$\,MHz.
\item $k=2$: $80\le f_s\le120$\,MHz.
\item $k=1$: $f_s\ge160$\,MHz (ordinary low-pass sampling).
\end{itemize}
Note that a seemingly natural rate such as 61.44\,MS/s fails: it lies in the gap between the
$k=3$ and $k=2$ wedges, and the band's lower edge would fold over onto itself near DC.

The $f_s/4$ rule suggests $f_s=4\times70/5=56$\,MS/s ($m=2$), inside the $k=3$ wedge. The band
folds to $60-56=4$ to $80-56=24$\,MHz, centred on 14\,MHz $=f_s/4$, upright because zone 3 is
odd, with 4\,MHz guard bands to DC and to $f_s/2=28$\,MHz. The anti-alias filter must
now pass 60--80\,MHz and reject 28--56\,MHz (zone 2) and 84--112\,MHz (zone 4), a bandpass filter
with 4\,MHz transition bands at 70\,MHz, which is a realistic SAW or LC design. The ADC's
track-and-hold must have an analog bandwidth comfortably above 80\,MHz, and the clock must be
clean enough for an 80\,MHz input: with 1\,ps RMS jitter,~\eqref{eq:ch05:jitter} below limits the
SNR to $-20\log_{10}(2\pi\times80\times10^6\times10^{-12})=66$\,dB. Finally, a $\pm1$\% error in
the sampling clock moves the folded centre by $\pm0.7$\,MHz, five times more than the same
error would at baseband---the price of working in a high zone.
\end{worked}

\mdcphoto[0.5]{ch05_strobe}{A General Radio Strobolume stroboscope. Flashing at almost the speed of a
rotating machine, it makes the machine appear to turn slowly enough to inspect---aliasing used on
purpose, which is exactly what bandpass sampling does.}

\begin{inpractice}[IF sampling, RF sampling and the end of the mixer]
Cellular base stations of the 2000s typically used one analog down-conversion to an IF of
roughly 70 to 250\,MHz and then a 14- or 16-bit pipeline ADC sampling in the second or third
Nyquist zone. Modern direct-RF-sampling converters go further: AMD's RFSoC devices and
comparable parts from Analog Devices and Texas Instruments sample at several gigasamples per
second with analog input bandwidths above 6\,GHz, so that a 3.5\,GHz 5G band can be digitised
directly in the second or third zone of a 3 to 4\,GS/s converter. The analog mixer and
synthesiser disappear; in their place are a bandpass filter, a very clean sampling clock
and a numerically controlled oscillator inside the chip's digital down-converter
(Chapter~\ref{ch:sdr}). The same principle lets an oscilloscope's ``equivalent-time'' mode
display a 50\,GHz repetitive signal with a sampler running at only a few hundred kilosamples per
second.
\end{inpractice}

causes a large amplitude error where the signal is steep (orange) and almost none at a crest. The

\subsection{Sampling-clock jitter: a shaky hand on the shutter}
\label{sec:ch05:jitter}

Take a photograph of a racing car with a slow, shaky hand and the car is a blur, while the
grandstand behind it is perfectly sharp. A sampling clock has the same problem. The sampling
theorem assumes that samples are taken at exactly $t=nT_s$, but real clocks wander by a few
hundred femtoseconds or a few picoseconds. That sounds negligible---light travels less than a
millimetre in a picosecond---yet it is often \emph{the} limit on a radio converter. The reason is
in Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_jitter_slope}: a timing error matters only in proportion to how fast the
signal is changing. A slightly late sample of a slowly moving signal reads almost the right value;
a slightly late sample of a signal racing through zero reads a very different one.

Suppose the $n$th sample is actually taken at $nT_s+\tau_n$, where $\tau_n$ is a small random
timing error with RMS value $\sigma_t$, the \term{aperture jitter}. To first order,
\begin{equation}
x(nT_s+\tau_n)\approx x(nT_s)+\tau_n\,x'(nT_s),
\end{equation}
so the jitter adds an error $\tau_nx'(nT_s)$ proportional to the slope of the signal. If the
jitter is independent of the signal, the error power is $\sigma_t^2\,\E[x'(t)^2]$. For a
sinusoid $x(t)=A\sin(2\pi ft)$ the derivative has power $(2\pi f)^2A^2/2$, while the signal
has power $A^2/2$, so the signal-to-jitter-noise ratio is
\begin{equation}
\text{SNR}_{\text{jitter}}=\frac{1}{(2\pi f\sigma_t)^2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{SNR}_{\text{jitter}}=-20\log_{10}(2\pi f\sigma_t)\ \text{dB}.
\label{eq:ch05:jitter}
\end{equation}
In other words: \emph{double the input frequency or double the jitter, and you lose 6\,dB---a whole
bit}. Three features of this result shape RF converter design. First, it depends on the
\emph{input frequency}, not the sample rate: undersampling a 1\,GHz signal at 100\,MS/s is
exactly as jitter-sensitive as sampling it at 3\,GS/s. Second, it does not depend on the
amplitude, since both the signal and the error scale with $A$. Third, it falls by 20\,dB per
decade of frequency, 6\,dB per octave, an unforgiving slope (Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_jitter}). For a
general signal with power spectrum $S_x(f)$, $\E[x'^2]=\int(2\pi f)^2S_x(f)\,df$, so the result
holds with $f$ replaced by the RMS frequency of the signal.

When the jitter on successive samples is correlated---as it is for the slow wander produced by
the close-in phase noise of an oscillator---the error is better described as phase modulation
of the signal: the clock's phase-noise spectrum, scaled by $(f/f_{\text{clk}})^2$, appears as
skirts around every input tone. Only the jitter components that vary quickly compared with
the observation interval appear as a white floor. In a receiver the skirts matter for the
same reason as the reciprocal mixing of a local oscillator (Chapter~\ref{ch:sdr}): a strong
blocker's skirt can bury a weak neighbour.

The total jitter combines the clock's own jitter and the converter's internal aperture
uncertainty in root-sum-square, $\sigma_t=\sqrt{\sigma_{\text{clk}}^2+\sigma_{\text{ap}}^2}$, and the
jitter noise adds to the quantization and thermal noise:
$\text{SNR}^{-1}=\text{SNR}_{\text{q}}^{-1}+\text{SNR}_{\text{jitter}}^{-1}+\text{SNR}_{\text{th}}^{-1}$.
Clock jitter is computed from the clock's single-sideband phase noise $\mathcal{L}(f)$ by
integrating over the offset range that matters,
\begin{equation}
\sigma_t=\frac{1}{2\pi f_{\text{clk}}}\sqrt{2\int_{f_1}^{f_2}10^{\mathcal{L}(f)/10}\,df}.
\label{eq:ch05:pnjitter}
\end{equation}

\mdcfig[0.5]{ch05_pn_jitter}{From a phase-noise plot to a jitter number. A 100\,MHz clock with
close-in noise falling at about 18\,dB per decade onto a $-157$\,dBc/Hz floor; integrating
$\mathcal{L}(f)$ from 10\,kHz to 50\,MHz by~\eqref{eq:ch05:pnjitter} gives about 255\,fs, most of it
from the broad floor, even though the logarithmic axis makes the close-in region look larger.}

\begin{worked}[The jitter budget at 100\,MHz]
A 14-bit ADC (ideal SQNR 86\,dB) digitises a 100\,MHz IF. What jitter can we tolerate, and what
does a real clock deliver?

For the jitter noise alone to equal the quantization noise, $2\pi f\sigma_t=10^{-86/20}=5.0\times10^{-5}$,
so $\sigma_t=5.0\times10^{-5}/(2\pi\times10^8)=80$\,fs. For a 12-bit converter (74\,dB) the
figure relaxes to 320\,fs. If the clock and the converter's aperture contribute 100\,fs and
60\,fs, the total is $\sqrt{100^2+60^2}=117$\,fs and the jitter-limited SNR at 100\,MHz is
$-20\log_{10}(2\pi\times10^8\times1.17\times10^{-13})=82.7$\,dB; combined with 86\,dB of
quantization noise, the SNR is $-10\log_{10}(10^{-8.27}+10^{-8.6})=81.0$\,dB, an ENOB of 13.2 bits.

Where does 100\,fs come from? Take a 100\,MHz clock with a flat phase-noise floor of
$-160$\,dBc/Hz from 10\,kHz to 50\,MHz offset. The single-sideband integral is
$-160+10\log_{10}(5\times10^7)=-83$\,dBc; doubling for both sidebands gives $-80$\,dBc, an RMS
phase of $10^{-4}$\,rad, and by~\eqref{eq:ch05:pnjitter} a jitter of
$10^{-4}/(2\pi\times10^8)=159$\,fs. A floor of $-160$\,dBc/Hz is excellent for a crystal oscillator;
reaching 100\,fs or below requires a low-noise PLL clock cleaner and a band-limiting filter on
the clock line. Notice that the integral is dominated by the wide offsets, which is why
filtering the clock's broadband floor helps so much.
\end{worked}

\paragraph{Nonuniform sampling.} Jitter is an accident; sometimes samples are deliberately
nonuniform. A beautiful generalisation due to Athanasios Papoulis (1977) shows that a signal of
bandwidth $W$ can be recovered from $M$ interleaved sample streams, each at rate $2W/M$, with
known fixed offsets---or from samples of $M$ different filtered versions of the signal---provided
the average rate is at least $2W$. The case of $M$ uniformly offset streams with small timing
errors is exactly the \emph{time-interleaved ADC} of Section~\ref{sec:ch05:adc}, where the
offsets are unwanted and must be calibrated. Sampling a band-limited signal at random times is
also possible on average, and the modern theory of compressed sensing shows that signals that
are \emph{sparse} in frequency can be recovered from far fewer samples than the Nyquist rate
suggests, which has been applied to wideband spectrum sensing.

\mdcfig{ch05_ti_concept}{Deliberately nonuniform sampling made uniform: in a four-way time-interleaved converter each sub-ADC (one colour) samples at a quarter of the rate, and the four staggered streams together sample at the full rate---provided their timing offsets are exactly right (Section~\ref{sec:ch05:adc}).}

\subsection{The sample-and-hold}
\label{sec:ch05:sh}

An ADC needs time to convert, and the input must not change while it does---just as a portrait
painter needs the sitter to hold still. Almost every converter therefore begins with a
\term{track-and-hold} (T/H, often loosely called a sample-and-hold): a switch and a capacitor. In
\emph{track} mode the switch is closed and the capacitor voltage follows the input through the
switch's on-resistance $R_{\text{on}}$; at the sampling instant the switch opens and the capacitor
holds the voltage while the quantizer works (Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_track_hold}). The sampling
theorem's ``instant'' is the moment the switch opens, and everything in the previous subsection is
really a statement about how precisely that moment is defined.

\mdcfig[0.5]{ch05_track_hold}{A track-and-hold in action. While the clock is in its track phase
(shaded) the capacitor follows the input; at each falling edge it freezes the value and holds it
flat while the quantizer works.}

The T/H has a catalogue of impairments, each with its own datasheet line:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Acquisition time and bandwidth.} In track mode the switch and capacitor form a
low-pass filter with time constant $R_{\text{on}}C$. Settling to within half an LSB of a
$b$-bit converter needs $(b+1)\ln2$ time constants, about 10 for 14 bits, and the $-3$\,dB
bandwidth must exceed the highest input frequency, which is what limits undersampling.
\item \emph{Aperture delay and jitter.} The delay between the clock edge and the switch
actually opening is harmless if constant; its random part is the aperture jitter.
\item \emph{Pedestal and charge injection.} When a MOS switch opens, its channel charge is
dumped partly onto the capacitor, a signal-dependent step that causes distortion.
Bottom-plate sampling and differential circuits cancel most of it.
\item \emph{Droop and feedthrough.} Leakage discharges the capacitor during hold, and the
open switch's capacitance couples the input to the held value.
\item \emph{Thermal noise.} The switch resistance generates noise $4kTR_{\text{on}}$ per
hertz, filtered by the $RC$ network whose noise bandwidth is $1/(4R_{\text{on}}C)$. The product
is independent of $R_{\text{on}}$:
\begin{equation}
\overline{v_n^2}=4kTR_{\text{on}}\cdot\frac{1}{4R_{\text{on}}C}=\frac{kT}{C}.
\label{eq:ch05:ktc}
\end{equation}
\end{itemize}
The famous \term{$kT/C$ noise} is frozen onto the capacitor at every sampling instant, and it
sets a lower bound on capacitor size, and therefore on power, for a given resolution. The
surprise in~\eqref{eq:ch05:ktc} is that the resistor cancels: a bigger resistor makes more noise
per hertz but also narrows the bandwidth by exactly the same factor. Only a bigger capacitor helps
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_ktc}).

\mdcfig[0.5]{ch05_ktc}{The $kT/C$ limit. The sampling capacitor at which
thermal noise equals quantization noise for a 2\,V peak-to-peak converter: every extra bit
multiplies it by four.}

\begin{worked}[How big must the sampling capacitor be?]
A 14-bit converter with a 2\,V peak-to-peak differential full scale has a step
$\Delta=2/2^{14}=122\,\mu$V and quantization noise power
$\Delta^2/12=1.24\times10^{-9}\,\text{V}^2$ (35\,$\mu$V RMS). At room temperature,
$kT=4.14\times10^{-21}$\,J. Making the $kT/C$ noise equal to the quantization noise needs
\[
C=\frac{kT}{\Delta^2/12}=\frac{4.14\times10^{-21}}{1.24\times10^{-9}}=3.3\,\text{pF},
\]
and in practice designers allow the thermal noise to dominate slightly and use a few
picofarads. Each extra bit quadruples the required capacitance: 16 bits needs about 53\,pF and
18 bits about 850\,pF. Driving such capacitors at hundreds of megahertz takes current, which is
why high resolution and high speed together cost so much power, and why the figures of merit
of Section~\ref{sec:ch05:adc} reward converters that avoid large sampling capacitors, as
oversampling converters do.
\end{worked}

\subsection{Reconstruction: the zero-order hold and its correction}
\label{sec:ch05:recon}

Now run the film backwards. A digital-to-analog converter must turn the list of numbers back into
a voltage. The sinc interpolator of~\eqref{eq:ch05:interp} is the perfect answer, but it is not
causal---every output instant depends on samples that have not arrived yet---and it cannot be built.
Real DACs do the simplest possible thing: they hold each sample constant for one period, like a
staircase, a \term{zero-order hold} (ZOH), and follow it with an analog \term{reconstruction filter}
(or \emph{image filter}) that sands off the corners. The hold is a convolution of the impulse train
with a rectangle of width $T_s$, whose transform is $T_s\sinc(fT_s)e^{-j\pi fT_s}$, so the output
spectrum is the image-replicated spectrum~\eqref{eq:ch05:replicas} multiplied by
\begin{equation}
|H_{\text{ZOH}}(f)|=\left|\sinc\!\left(\frac{f}{f_s}\right)\right|
=\left|\frac{\sin(\pi f/f_s)}{\pi f/f_s}\right|.
\label{eq:ch05:zoh}
\end{equation}
This has two effects (Figures~\ref{fig:ch05_reconstruction} and~\ref{fig:ch05_zoh_comp}). In
the wanted band it causes \emph{droop}: $-0.14$\,dB at $0.1f_s$, $-0.91$\,dB at $0.25f_s$,
$-2.42$\,dB at $0.4f_s$ and $20\log_{10}(\pi/2)=3.92$\,dB at $f_s/2$. Beyond $f_s/2$ it
\emph{attenuates the images}, with nulls at every multiple of $f_s$, so the first image of a
signal at $f$ appears at $f_s-f$ reduced by $20\log_{10}[(f_s-f)/f]$\,dB relative to the
in-band component (only 3.5\,dB for $f=0.4f_s$). The analog filter must do the rest. The
delay term $e^{-j\pi fT_s}$ is half a sample of pure delay, which matters only inside
feedback loops.

\mdcfig{ch05_reconstruction}{Left: ideal sinc interpolation passes smoothly through the
samples, while a zero-order hold produces a staircase. Right: the staircase's frequency
response $\sinc(f/f_s)$ droops by 3.9\,dB at the Nyquist frequency.}

There are three standard remedies, usually combined:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Inverse-sinc pre-emphasis.} A short digital FIR filter before the DAC boosts
high frequencies by $1/\sinc(f/f_s)$. A symmetric three-tap filter $[-a,\,c,\,-a]$ has
response $c-2a\cos(2\pi f/f_s)$; a least-squares fit to $1/\sinc$ over $0$--$0.4f_s$ gives
$a\approx0.077$, $c\approx1.135$ and shrinks the 2.4\,dB droop to a residual of about $-0.4$ to
$+0.2$\,dB, and nine taps reduce
the residual to a few hundredths of a decibel (Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_zoh_comp}, right). Most
RF DACs include such a filter as an option.
\item \emph{Interpolation (oversampling DAC).} Raise the sample rate digitally by a factor
$L$ before the DAC, inserting zeros and low-pass filtering (Chapter~\ref{ch:dsp}). The signal
now occupies only $0$--$0.4f_s/L$ of the faster DAC's band, so the droop is tiny
($-0.14$\,dB for $L=4$) and the first image moves out to near $Lf_s$, where a gentle analog
filter removes it (Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_zoh_comp}, left).
\item \emph{Analog equalisation.} Build the inverse droop into the reconstruction filter.
\end{enumerate}

High-speed RF DACs add tricks of their own. A \emph{return-to-zero} DAC holds each value for
only half a period, which halves the in-band power but moves the first null of the envelope to
$2f_s$, leaving more energy in the second Nyquist zone. A \emph{mix-mode} DAC (or ``RF
mode'') inverts the output in the middle of each period, which places a null at DC and a
peak in the second zone; it lets a 6\,GS/s DAC synthesise a 5\,GHz carrier directly from
its first image. The choice of mode is simply a choice of pulse shape $p(t)$ in the
pulse-amplitude modulation model of Chapter~\ref{ch:baseband}.

\mdcfig{ch05_zoh_comp}{Left: the sinc envelope of a ZOH DAC and the images of a signal
occupying $0$--$0.4f_s$ (shaded). At the original rate (orange) the first image starts only
$0.2f_s$ above the band; after $4\times$ digital interpolation (navy) the first image moves to
$4f_s\pm0.4f_s$ and the in-band droop nearly vanishes. Right: in-band droop and its correction by
least-squares inverse-sinc FIR filters of three and nine taps.}

\begin{inpractice}[The transmit chain of the B200]
On transmit, the AD9364 in the USRP B200 takes complex samples from the FPGA at up to
61.44\,MS/s, passes them through a programmable FIR filter (which can include inverse-sinc
correction) and a chain of half-band and third-band interpolators that raise the rate by up to
48, and drives a 12-bit DAC at several hundred megasamples per second. A low-order analog
low-pass filter then removes the distant images before the I/Q modulator. In GNU Radio or
UHD you never see these stages, but you see their consequences: requesting a sample rate
much lower than the analog filter bandwidth leaves the digital filters to do all the work,
and choosing a rate that makes the interpolation ratio odd or awkward can raise the image
and noise levels measurably.
\end{inpractice}

\subsection{A first look at multirate processing}

The remedies above share one idea: let the analog filter be gentle and do the hard filtering
digitally at a different rate. Changing the sample rate of a signal that is already digital
is the subject of \emph{multirate} signal processing. \emph{Decimation} by $M$ keeps every
$M$th sample after a digital low-pass filter that removes everything that would alias into
the new, narrower band; \emph{interpolation} by $L$ inserts $L-1$ zeros between samples, which
creates $L-1$ images, and removes them with a digital low-pass filter. Every statement in
this section about analog sampling has an exact digital twin: decimation \emph{is} sampling
of a discrete-time signal, and~\eqref{eq:ch05:replicas} and~\eqref{eq:ch05:fold} apply with
$f_s$ replaced by the new rate. Chapter~\ref{ch:dsp} develops the efficient structures
(polyphase filters, half-band filters, cascaded integrator--comb filters) that make conversion
by large factors affordable, and Section~\ref{sec:ch05:decim} below shows how they complete a
sigma-delta converter.

\begin{tryit}[Alias a signal that is already digital]

\section{Uniform quantization}
\label{sec:ch05:quant}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Rounding everyone's height to the nearest inch}

A school nurse measures the heights of a thousand children and writes each one down to the
nearest inch. A child of 52.3 inches becomes 52; one of 52.8 becomes 53. Every entry is wrong by
somewhere between $-\frac12$ and $+\frac12$ inch, and the errors have three convenient habits. They
are \emph{small} (never more than half a step). They are \emph{evenly spread}: an error of
$+0.4$ is as likely as one of $-0.1$, because heights do not cluster at whole inches. And they
are \emph{unrelated} to the height itself: tall children are no more likely to be rounded up than
short ones. Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_height_rounding} checks this with 20\,000 simulated heights; the
errors fill the interval as flatly as a sheet of paper, and their RMS value is $1/\sqrt{12}$ of an
inch, about 0.29 inch.

That is quantization, and those three habits are the whole of the standard model of quantization
noise. The rest of this section uses them, finds out when they fail, and learns to restore them
when they do.

\subsection{The uniform quantizer}

A $b$-bit quantizer maps each input sample to one of $L=2^b$ levels. The uniform quantizer
spaces the levels by a step $\Delta=2X_{\max}/L$ over a full-scale range $\pm X_{\max}$. Two
arrangements are common. A \emph{mid-rise} quantizer has a decision threshold at zero and
output levels at $\pm\Delta/2,\pm3\Delta/2,\dots$, which uses all $2^b$ codes symmetrically
but has no zero output. A \emph{mid-tread} quantizer has an output level at zero, so that
silence or a quiet idle channel produces exactly zero rather than toggling between
$\pm\Delta/2$; it has an odd number of useful levels, and two's-complement converters are
mid-tread with one extra negative code. The difference between the output and the input is
the \term{quantization error} $e=Q(x)-x$, bounded by $\pm\Delta/2$ as long as the input does not
exceed full scale (Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_quantizer}). Outside that range the quantizer
\term{clips} or \emph{overloads}, and the error grows without bound---the nurse's ruler is only
six feet long, and a seven-foot basketball player is recorded as six. These are two fundamentally
different kinds of error, \emph{granular} noise inside the range and \emph{overload} distortion
outside it, and the central problem of quantizer design is balancing them.

\mdcfig{ch05_quantizer}{A 3-bit mid-rise quantizer, its error sawtooth, and the SQNR of a
full-scale sine versus word length. The simulated values match the $6.02b+1.76$\,dB rule to a
fraction of a decibel.}

\subsection{The additive-noise model and when it holds}

The error $e=Q(x)-x$ is a deterministic function of $x$: the sawtooth of
Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_quantizer}. Yet engineers routinely model it as random noise, uniformly
distributed on $[-\Delta/2,\Delta/2]$, independent of the signal and white---the nurse's three
habits. Under that model,
\begin{equation}
\sigma_e^2=\int_{-\Delta/2}^{\Delta/2}e^2\,\frac{de}{\Delta}=\frac{\Delta^2}{12}.
\label{eq:ch05:d212}
\end{equation}
In words: the quantization noise power is the square of the step divided by twelve. Halve the
step and the noise power falls by four.

When is the model justified? Bernard Widrow answered the question in 1956 with an elegant
argument that treats quantization as \emph{sampling of the probability density}, and the answer
mirrors the sampling theorem so neatly that it deserves a box of its own.

\mdcfig{ch05_model_fails}{When the additive-noise model holds and when it fails. Left: the
quantization error of a busy Gaussian signal is perfectly flat between $\pm\frac12$ LSB. Right: a
sine smaller than one step, synchronised with the sample clock, produces only a handful of error
values---not noise at all, but a periodic, signal-dependent pattern.}

\begin{deeper}[Widrow's quantization theorem]
Quantizing $x$ with step $\Delta$ is equivalent to sampling the density $p_x$ in the amplitude
domain, so the characteristic function of the output is periodic with period $2\pi/\Delta$, exactly
as sampling a waveform makes its spectrum periodic. Widrow's \term{quantization theorem} states
that if the characteristic function $\Phi_x(u)=\E[e^{jux}]$ is band-limited, vanishing for
$|u|\ge2\pi/\Delta$, the moments of the quantized variable can be recovered exactly and the
error behaves exactly as uniform noise uncorrelated with the signal. A weaker condition
(Sripad and Snyder, 1977), $\Phi_x(2\pi k/\Delta)=0$ for all integers $k\ne0$, is necessary
and sufficient for the error to be uniform and uncorrelated with $x$.

No real density is exactly band-limited, but many are close. For a Gaussian input of
standard deviation $\sigma$, $|\Phi_x(2\pi/\Delta)|=\exp(-2\pi^2\sigma^2/\Delta^2)$, which is
$2.7\times10^{-9}$ for $\sigma=\Delta$.
\end{deeper}

So the additive model is excellent whenever the signal is \emph{busy}---it spans many quantization
steps and is not synchronised with the sampling clock, like the children's heights that do not
cluster at whole inches. It fails in four common situations:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Small signals}, a few LSBs or less, where the error is obviously a function of
the signal; a signal smaller than $\Delta/2$ can vanish entirely in a mid-tread quantizer.
\item \emph{Slowly varying or constant inputs}, where the error is a constant offset rather
than noise.
\item \emph{Periodic inputs coherent with the sample rate}, such as a sine at exactly
$f_s/16$, which make the error periodic: its power appears as discrete spurs at harmonics
of the input rather than as a white floor. ADC test engineers deliberately choose
``incommensurate'' test frequencies to avoid this, and the SFDR of an ideal quantizer can
depend on the input frequency.
\item \emph{Coarse quantizers}, one to three bits, where the error is large and strongly
signal-dependent. The one-bit quantizer inside a sigma-delta modulator is the extreme case,
and the linear model of Section~\ref{sec:ch05:sigmadelta} is a useful but imperfect
approximation there.
\end{itemize}
Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_model_fails} shows the difference at a glance. Section~\ref{sec:ch05:dither}
shows how to \emph{force} the additive model to hold.

\subsection{Six decibels per bit}

For a full-scale sine of amplitude $X_{\max}=2^{b-1}\Delta$, the power is
$X_{\max}^2/2=2^{2b}\Delta^2/8$, and the \term{signal-to-quantization-noise ratio} is
\begin{equation}
\text{SQNR}=\frac{X_{\max}^2/2}{\Delta^2/12}=\frac32\,2^{2b}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{SQNR}_{\text{dB}}=6.02\,b+1.76.
\label{eq:ch05:sqnr}
\end{equation}
This is the most frequently quoted formula in data conversion: \emph{each bit is worth
6\,dB}, because each bit halves $\Delta$ and quarters the noise power. A 12-bit converter,
such as the B200's, offers 74\,dB; a 16-bit audio converter 98\,dB; a 24-bit audio
converter a theoretical 146\,dB, far below the analog noise floor of any real
circuit (Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_bits_ladder}).\footnote{The 1.76\,dB is $10\log_{10}1.5$ and belongs to the sine wave, not to the
quantizer. Data sheets that quote ``$6.02N+1.76$'' implicitly assume a full-scale sinusoidal
test signal.}

\mdcfig{ch05_bits_ladder}{Six decibels per bit: the ideal full-scale-sine SQNR of~\eqref{eq:ch05:sqnr}
for some famous word lengths. Real converters fall short of the top bars; the best audio
ADCs deliver roughly 20 effective bits, whatever their output word length.}

\subsection{Loading, crest factor and back-off}

Real signals do not sit at full scale, and many are far from sinusoidal. Write the full-scale
level in terms of the signal's RMS value, $X_{\max}=\Gamma\sigma_x$, where $\Gamma$ is the
\term{loading factor} (the \emph{crest factor} of the largest peak the quantizer will pass
without clipping). Then
\begin{equation}
\text{SQNR}=\frac{\sigma_x^2}{\Delta^2/12}=\frac{3\cdot2^{2b}}{\Gamma^2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{SQNR}_{\text{dB}}=6.02\,b+4.77-20\log_{10}\Gamma.
\label{eq:ch05:loading}
\end{equation}
For a sine, $\Gamma=\sqrt2$ and~\eqref{eq:ch05:loading} reduces to~\eqref{eq:ch05:sqnr}. For a
Gaussian-like signal---speech over many talkers, the sum of many OFDM subcarriers, thermal
noise---there is no hard peak, and $\Gamma$ becomes a design choice, like choosing how tall a door
to build when you do not know the height of the tallest visitor. Every decibel of
\emph{back-off} costs a decibel of granular SQNR; too little back-off lets the tails clip.
With $\Gamma=4$ (``four-sigma loading'') the SQNR is $6.02b-7.27$\,dB and the overload noise,
$2\int_{\Gamma\sigma}^{\infty}(x-\Gamma\sigma)^2p(x)\,dx$, is 52\,dB below the signal, so for
more than about 10 bits the overload dominates and four sigma is too little. Minimising the
sum of granular and overload noise for a Gaussian gives an optimum loading that grows slowly
with resolution (Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_loading}): about $2.6\sigma$ for 4 bits (19.8\,dB), $3.9\sigma$
for 8 bits (40.6\,dB), $5.0\sigma$ for 12 bits (62.7\,dB) and $5.9\sigma$ for 16 bits (85.4\,dB).

\mdcfig[0.5]{ch05_loading}{Choosing the loading factor for a Gaussian input. To the left of each
peak, clipping noise dominates; to the right, the step is needlessly coarse. The optimum moves
outwards slowly as bits are added.}

The same equation explains the second refinement that matters in a receiver. If the signal is
narrowband compared with $f_s/2$, the quantization noise, spread uniformly over $0$ to $f_s/2$,
is mostly out of band, and a digital filter that keeps only the signal band $B$ removes it.
The in-band SQNR improves by the \term{processing gain}
\begin{equation}
G_{\text{proc}}=10\log_{10}\frac{f_s}{2B}\ \text{dB}.
\label{eq:ch05:procgain}
\end{equation}
An SDR that samples at 56\,MS/s (real-equivalent bandwidth 28\,MHz) and filters to a 200\,kHz
channel gains $10\log_{10}(28\times10^6/2\times10^5)=21.5$\,dB, which is why a 12-bit radio can
receive a signal 90\,dB below the converter's full scale. This processing gain is the seed of
oversampling and of the sigma-delta converter (Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_noise_moving}).

\begin{worked}[An OFDM signal in a 12-bit ADC]
A 20\,MHz LTE or Wi-Fi carrier is digitised by a 12-bit ADC. OFDM is close to Gaussian; the
standard practice is to clip at about 12\,dB above RMS ($\Gamma=4$), accepting rare clipping
that the error-correcting code absorbs. Equation~\eqref{eq:ch05:loading} gives
$6.02\times12+4.77-12.04=65.0$\,dB. If the ADC samples complex baseband at 30.72\,MS/s, the
20\,MHz signal occupies two thirds of the band, so the processing gain is only 1.8\,dB. Now
leave 6\,dB of headroom for the automatic gain control to respond to fading and another 10\,dB
for an adjacent-channel blocker that must also not clip, and the SQNR available to the wanted
signal falls to about $65+1.8-16\approx51$\,dB. That is still far more than the 25 to 30\,dB a
256-QAM constellation needs (Chapter~\ref{ch:modulation}). The lesson is that in a radio
receiver the bits are spent not on the wanted signal's SNR but on \emph{dynamic range}:
headroom for the signals you do not want.
\end{worked}

\begin{pitfall}[title=Clipping is worse than noise]
Quantization noise degrades gracefully; clipping does not. A signal that clips even
occasionally generates broadband distortion and, in a transmitter, spectral regrowth into
adjacent channels. For OFDM signals with 10\,dB or more of peak-to-average ratio, the rule of
thumb is to keep the RMS level 10 to 12\,dB below full scale in the ADC and DAC, and to budget
the lost SQNR accordingly. In the B200, setting the digital TX amplitude above about 0.7 is a
common way to generate a very clean spectral splatter. And because an ADC that clips gives
no warning in the samples themselves---a clipped sample is a perfectly valid number---every
serious receiver monitors the fraction of samples at the extreme codes and feeds it to the
gain control.
\end{pitfall}

\mdcfig{ch05_clipping}{What clipping does to an OFDM signal (600 occupied subcarriers of 1024,
QPSK). Clipping 12\,dB above the RMS level leaves the spectrum clean; clipping at 6\,dB and 3\,dB
throws distortion into the adjacent bands at only about 30\,dB and 20\,dB below the signal---far
too much for any spectrum mask.}

\subsection{Dither: adding noise to hide the staircase}
\label{sec:ch05:dither}

Photographers know a strange fact: a picture of a clear blue sky printed with too few shades of
blue shows ugly bands, like contour lines on a map, but the same picture with a little film grain
looks smooth. The grain is noise, and it makes the picture \emph{better}. Quantizers behave the same
way. When the input is small or slowly varying, the error is no longer noise-like; it is a
deterministic function of the signal, heard as harmonic distortion on quiet music, seen as
contour bands in a smoothly shaded image (Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_dither_image}), and measured as spurs
in an ADC's spectrum. The cure is counter-intuitive: add a small random signal, \term{dither},
before quantization. Lawrence Roberts proposed it for television pictures in 1962, and the theory
was completed by Leonard Schuchman (1964) and, for audio, by Stanley Lipshitz, Robert Wannamaker
and John Vanderkooy around 1992.

\mdcfig{ch05_dither_image}{Dither as film grain. A smoothly shaded image (left) quantized to six
grey levels shows hard contour bands (centre). Adding triangular dither of about one step before the
same quantizer (right) replaces the bands with fine grain, and the eye recovers the smooth shading.}

\begin{analogy}[Film grain]
Your eye averages over neighbouring grains, and your ear averages over neighbouring samples. A
staircase that is the same at every step is a pattern the eye or ear locks onto; random grain is
a pattern it ignores. Dither trades a structured error that we notice for an unstructured one that
we do not. The analogy's limit: dither does not make the noise smaller---it makes it slightly
larger---it only changes its character from distortion to hiss.
\end{analogy}

There are two kinds. In \emph{subtractive} dither the same pseudo-random sequence $d$ is added
before the quantizer and subtracted after it, so the output is $Q(x+d)-d$. If $d$ is uniform on
$[-\Delta/2,\Delta/2]$ (rectangular probability density, \emph{RPDF}), Schuchman's condition
holds for any input and the total error is \emph{exactly} uniform, of variance $\Delta^2/12$,
and statistically independent of the signal. This is the ideal, but it requires the receiver
to know the dither sequence, which is possible in a converter or a codec but not, for example,
on a CD where the playback machine has no idea what dither the mastering engineer used.

In \emph{non-subtractive} dither the dither stays in the output, $Q(x+d)$. The total error then
cannot be independent of the signal, but its first $m$ moments can be made independent of
the signal. The results, which Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_dither} verifies numerically, are:
\begin{itemize}
\item With RPDF dither of one LSB peak-to-peak, the \emph{mean} of the error is zero for every
input---the staircase is linearised on average, and a slowly varying signal smaller than one
step is preserved in the average of the output. But the error \emph{variance} still depends on
the signal, varying from 0 to $\Delta^2/4$ (noise modulation), which the ear hears as a noise
floor that breathes with the music.
\item With \term{TPDF dither}, the sum of two independent RPDF variables, triangular on
$[-\Delta,\Delta]$, both the mean and the variance of the error are independent of the signal. The
variance is $\Delta^2/12$ (quantizer) plus $\Delta^2/6$ (dither) $=\Delta^2/4$, three times
(4.8\,dB) the undithered noise, and it is constant. Higher moments still vary, but the ear and
most measurements are insensitive to them.
\end{itemize}
TPDF dither of 2\,LSB peak-to-peak is therefore the standard for audio word-length reduction,
for example when a 24-bit studio master is reduced to 16 bits for CD. Its 4.8\,dB noise penalty
can be hidden by \emph{noise-shaped} dither, which uses the error-feedback structure of
Section~\ref{sec:ch05:sigmadelta} to push the requantization noise into the frequency range
where the ear is least sensitive (above about 15\,kHz), making a 16-bit CD perceptually quieter
than its 98\,dB arithmetic suggests (Chapter~\ref{ch:sourcecoding} discusses the auditory
masking that makes this work).

\mdcfig{ch05_dither}{Dither for a mid-tread quantizer with a 1\,LSB step. Left and centre: the
mean and variance of the total error $Q(x+d)-x$ as the input $x$ is swept over three steps.
Without dither the mean is a sawtooth (the error is a deterministic function of $x$). RPDF
dither makes the mean zero but the variance still depends on $x$; TPDF dither makes both
constant, at $\Delta^2/4$; subtractive dither achieves the minimum $\Delta^2/12$. Right: a
0.9\,LSB sine quantized without dither produces a comb of odd harmonics; with TPDF dither the
harmonics disappear into a flat noise floor.}

High-speed ADCs use dither for a related purpose. The spurs of a pipeline or SAR converter come
mostly from the differential nonlinearity of its internal DAC at particular codes
(Section~\ref{sec:ch05:adc}). Adding a large pseudo-random dither---several hundred LSBs, often
outside the signal band so that it can be filtered off, or subtracted digitally---spreads each
code transition over many codes and averages their errors, trading a little SNR for a large
improvement in SFDR. Several commercial RF ADCs offer it as a register setting.

\begin{keyidea}[Noise is better than distortion]
Quantization error can take the form of noise (uniform, white, independent of the signal) or of
distortion (harmonics, spurs, offsets correlated with the signal). The total error power is
similar, but noise is benign: it masks nothing, it averages away, and the ear, the eye and the
demodulator all tolerate it better. Dither, sigma-delta modulation and the ``incommensurate''
test frequencies of ADC characterisation are all ways of making sure the error is noise.
\end{keyidea}

\section{Non-uniform quantization and companding}
\label{sec:ch05:companding}
%======================================================================

\subsection{The problem with speech}

A telephone network must serve a shouting salesman on a short line and a whispering grandmother at
the end of thirty miles of copper, through the same codec. Speech has a dynamic range of about
40\,dB between loud and soft talkers on different telephone lines, plus the large syllabic variation
within one utterance, and its amplitude distribution is sharply peaked near zero (approximately
Laplacian, or gamma-distributed). A uniform quantizer wastes its fine levels on large amplitudes
that rarely occur and gives quiet talkers a poor SQNR: by~\eqref{eq:ch05:loading}, every decibel of
level lost is a decibel of SQNR lost. Giving a talker 30\,dB below full scale an SQNR of 40\,dB takes
12 to 13 uniform bits, most of which are wasted on the loud talkers.
Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_quiet_talker} shows what a quiet talker sounds like to an 8-bit uniform
quantizer: a crude staircase of three or four levels.

\mdcfig{ch05_quiet_talker}{A quiet talker (peaks 36\,dB below full scale) through two 8-bit codecs.
The uniform quantizer has only a few levels in this range and mangles the waveform; the $\mu$-law
codec, whose steps near zero are some 30 times finer, follows it closely.}

The ideal is a quantizer whose step grows in proportion to the signal amplitude, giving a
roughly constant \emph{relative} accuracy and hence a constant SQNR regardless of level: fine
steps near zero, where speech spends most of its time, and coarse steps out in the rarely visited
extremes (Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_speech_levels}). A logarithmic characteristic does exactly that.

\mdcfig[0.5]{ch05_speech_levels}{Where speech lives. The amplitude histogram of a speech-like
(Laplacian) signal is sharply peaked at zero (note the logarithmic scale). A 5-bit uniform
quantizer spreads its levels evenly (orange); a 5-bit $\mu$-law quantizer crowds them where the
samples are (navy).}

\begin{analogy}[A camera's auto-exposure for voice]
A camera pointed at a dim room opens its aperture; pointed at a sunny beach, it closes it, so
both pictures use the full range of the sensor. Companding does the same for every single sample:
small samples are magnified before quantization and large ones are squeezed, and the receiver
undoes it. Unlike a camera, which adjusts slowly for the whole scene, a compander adjusts
\emph{instantaneously}, sample by sample, by a fixed curve---so it needs no side information and
never lags behind a sudden shout.
\end{analogy}

\subsection{Companding and Bennett's integral}

\term{Companding} (compressing then expanding) implements a non-uniform quantizer as a
nonlinear compressor $F(x)$, a uniform quantizer of step $\Delta$ on the compressed variable
$y=F(x)$, and the inverse expander $F^{-1}$ at the receiver. Near an input $x$, a step $\Delta$
in $y$ corresponds to a step $\Delta/F'(x)$ in $x$, so the local granular noise is
$(\Delta/F'(x))^2/12$. Averaging over the input density gives \emph{Bennett's integral}
(W.~R.~Bennett, 1948):
\begin{equation}
\sigma_e^2\approx\frac{\Delta^2}{12}\int\frac{p(x)}{F'(x)^2}\,dx
=\frac{\Delta^2}{12}\,\E\!\left[\frac{1}{F'(x)^2}\right].
\label{eq:ch05:bennett}
\end{equation}
In words: wherever the compressor is steep, the effective step is small and the noise is low, and
the total noise is the average of the local noise weighted by how often the signal visits each
neighbourhood. If we want $\text{SQNR}=\sigma_x^2/\sigma_e^2$ to be independent of the input level,
the local step must be proportional to $|x|$, so $F'(x)=c/|x|$ and $F(x)=c\ln|x|+\text{const}$: a
logarithm. A pure logarithm is unusable because it diverges at $x=0$; the practical laws are
logarithmic for large inputs and linear for small ones.

\subsection{The \texorpdfstring{$\mu$}{mu}-law and the A-law}

North America and Japan standardised the \term{$\mu$-law}, with inputs normalised to
$|x|\le1$,
\begin{equation}
F_\mu(x)=\operatorname{sgn}(x)\,\frac{\ln(1+\mu|x|)}{\ln(1+\mu)},\qquad \mu=255,
\label{eq:ch05:mulaw}
\end{equation}
and Europe and most of the rest of the world the \term{A-law},
\begin{equation}
F_A(x)=\operatorname{sgn}(x)\begin{cases}\dfrac{A|x|}{1+\ln A}, & |x|<1/A,\\[2ex]
\dfrac{1+\ln(A|x|)}{1+\ln A}, & 1/A\le|x|\le1,\end{cases}\qquad A=87.6.
\label{eq:ch05:alaw}
\end{equation}
The $\mu$-law is approximately linear for $|x|\ll1/\mu$ and logarithmic for $|x|\gg1/\mu$;
the A-law is exactly linear below $1/A$ and exactly logarithmic above it.

Substituting $F_\mu'(x)=\mu/[(1+\mu|x|)\ln(1+\mu)]$ into~\eqref{eq:ch05:bennett} with
$\Delta=2/2^b$ gives a closed form for the $\mu$-law SQNR:
\begin{equation}
\text{SQNR}_\mu=\frac{3\cdot2^{2b}}{\ln^2(1+\mu)}\cdot
\frac{1}{1+\dfrac{2\,\E|x|}{\mu\,\sigma_x^2}+\dfrac{1}{\mu^2\sigma_x^2}}.
\label{eq:ch05:musqnr}
\end{equation}
For loud signals the bracket tends to 1 and the SQNR becomes independent of level and of the
signal's distribution:
$6.02b+4.77-20\log_{10}\ln(1+\mu)$, which for $b=8$ and $\mu=255$ is
$48.16+4.77-14.88=38.0$\,dB. For quiet signals ($\sigma_x\ll1/\mu$) the law is linear and the
SQNR falls 1\,dB per dB, like a uniform quantizer whose step is $\ln(1+\mu)/\mu$ times smaller
than the 8-bit step: about 46 times, or 5.5 extra bits. The constant $\mu$ trades the plateau
height against its width: a larger $\mu$ lowers the plateau (by $20\log_{10}\ln(1+\mu)$) and
extends it to quieter signals. Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_companding} shows the result for a
speech-like input. Eight-bit $\mu$-law holds an SQNR of 35 to 38\,dB over a 40\,dB range of input
levels, while an 8-bit uniform quantizer degrades 1\,dB per decibel of level; matching $\mu$-law
across that range with a uniform quantizer would need about 12 to 13 bits.

\mdcfig{ch05_companding}{Left: the $\mu$-law compressor. Right: SQNR against input level for a
speech-like (Laplacian) signal. Eight-bit $\mu$-law keeps the SQNR nearly constant over a 40\,dB
range, performing like a 12-bit uniform quantizer for quiet talkers.}

\subsection{Why 255 and 87.6: the G.711 segment approximations}
\label{sec:ch05:g711}

In the 1960s nobody could compute a logarithm 8000 times a second per channel, so the laws were
implemented as piecewise-linear approximations whose segments were chosen to suit binary
logic. That choice explains the strange constants---and turns a $\mu$-law byte into what is, in
effect, the world's most widely used floating-point number.

\paragraph{The $\mu$-law.} Divide the compressed range $[0,1]$ into eight equal \emph{chords}
(segments) of 16 steps each. The smooth law reaches $F_\mu=k/8$ where
$1+\mu x=(1+\mu)^{k/8}$. With $\mu=255$, $(1+\mu)^{k/8}=256^{k/8}=2^k$, so the chord endpoints are
\begin{equation}
x_k=\frac{2^k-1}{255},\qquad k=0,1,\dots,8:
\end{equation}
each chord spans exactly twice the input range of the one before. Within each chord the 16
steps are uniform, so the step size doubles from chord to chord---exactly what a binary
\emph{exponent and mantissa} representation provides. A $\mu$-law code word is a tiny
floating-point number: one polarity bit $P$, three chord (exponent) bits $SSS$ and four step
(mantissa) bits $QQQQ$ (Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_g711_byte}). ITU-T Recommendation G.711 defines it on a
14-bit linear scale (full scale $\pm8159$ units) with a bias of 33: the chords span 0--31, 31--95,
95--223, \dots, 4063--8159 units with steps of 2, 4, 8, \dots, 256 units, and the encoder is simply
``add 33, find the position of the leading one, take the next four bits''. The result is the
\emph{15-segment} law (the two innermost chords of each polarity are collinear through zero).
The choice $\mu=255=2^8-1$ is therefore not a measured optimum but the value that makes the
smooth curve pass through the corners of a binary-friendly chord structure.

\mdcfig{ch05_g711_byte}{Anatomy of a $\mu$-law byte. The 14-bit sample $+300$ becomes polarity 0,
chord 3 and step 4: a sign, a three-bit exponent and a four-bit mantissa. On the line all eight bits
are inverted. Bottom: chord 3 spans 223 to 479 units in 16 steps of 16; the decoder returns the
middle of step 4, 295, an error of under 2\%.}

\paragraph{The A-law.} G.711's A-law uses 13-bit linear samples (full scale $\pm4096$ units).
Its first two chords both have a step of 2 units and lie on one straight line through the
origin, and each later chord doubles the step (4, 8, \dots, 64); counting both polarities and
merging the collinear central chords gives the \emph{13-segment} A-law. The central slope of
the segmented curve is 32 codes per 1/64 of the range per polarity, a slope of $(32/128)/(1/64)=16$
relative to a uniform 8-bit quantizer: the A-law gives small signals four extra bits of
resolution, the equivalent of a 12-bit uniform quantizer. The smooth law~\eqref{eq:ch05:alaw}
has central slope $A/(1+\ln A)$, and setting this equal to 16 gives $A=87.56$, rounded to
87.6. Again the constant is chosen to match a binary segment structure.

Two further details of G.711 come from the transmission line rather than the quantizer. On T1
lines a long run of zero bits starves the repeaters' clock recovery
(Chapter~\ref{ch:baseband}); since the most common samples are small, the $\mu$-law code
\emph{inverts all eight bits} before transmission, so that silence (code 0 for chord 0, step 0,
positive) is sent as \texttt{11111111}. The A-law code inverts every \emph{even} bit (an XOR with
\texttt{01010101}) for the same reason. And because a channel bank must calibrate levels,
G.711 also defines the \term{dBm0} scale, in which 0\,dBm0 is the level of a sine that would
deliver 1\,mW at a zero-level reference point of the analog network. A full-scale sine is
$+3.17$\,dBm0 for $\mu$-law and $+3.14$\,dBm0 for A-law, and the ``digital milliwatt'', a
repeating eight-sample $\mu$-law sequence that represents a 1\,kHz sine at 0\,dBm0, was used
throughout North America as a test tone.

\mdcfig{ch05_g711}{Left: the G.711 $\mu$-law (eight chords of 16 steps, staircase in navy)

6\,dB each time the step doubles.}

\begin{worked}[$\mu$-law SQNR at $-30$\,dBm0]
A $-30$\,dBm0 sine is a quiet but normal speech level on a long-distance call. On the
$\mu$-law scale, full scale is $+3.17$\,dBm0, so the sine's amplitude is
$10^{-33.17/20}=0.0220$ of full scale. Then $\sigma_x^2=0.022^2/2=2.42\times10^{-4}$ and
$\E|x|=2(0.022)/\pi=0.0140$. In~\eqref{eq:ch05:musqnr},
\[
\frac{2\E|x|}{\mu\sigma_x^2}=\frac{0.0280}{255\times2.42\times10^{-4}}=0.454,\qquad
\frac{1}{\mu^2\sigma_x^2}=\frac{1}{65025\times2.42\times10^{-4}}=0.064,
\]
so the bracket is $1.518$ and the SQNR is $38.0-10\log_{10}1.518=36.2$\,dB. A bit-exact
simulation of the segmented G.711 codec (Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_g711}) gives 36.3\,dB. An 8-bit
uniform quantizer on the same scale gives about 17\,dB at this level, and a 13-bit uniform
quantizer about 47\,dB. The companded 8-bit code therefore saves about five bits per sample
for quiet talkers---40\,kb/s per channel---at the cost of about 10\,dB at high levels, where the
ear does not notice because the speech itself masks the noise. That trade is the reason the
world's telephone network runs at 64\,kb/s and not 104\,kb/s.
\end{worked}

\begin{pitfall}[$\mu$-law meets A-law]
A call between a North American (or Japanese) network and a European one must convert between
the two laws, and the conversion is a table lookup from one 8-bit code to another. Because the
two quantizers have different decision levels, each conversion adds roughly the noise of
another quantization; G.711 therefore specifies the conversion tables exactly, and by
convention the conversion is performed in the $\mu$-law country. The practical lesson extends far beyond telephony: every tandem
transcoding---between companding laws, between speech codecs, between video formats---adds
noise, and a well-designed network avoids it.
\end{pitfall}

\subsection{Optimal quantizers}

For a known input density, which quantizer minimises the mean-square error for a fixed number
of levels $L$? Stuart Lloyd (1957, published only in 1982) and Joel Max (1960) found two
necessary conditions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item each decision threshold lies midway between adjacent output levels (nearest-neighbour
condition), $t_i=(y_{i-1}+y_i)/2$;
\item each output level is the centroid (conditional mean) of its decision interval,
$y_i=\E[x\mid t_i\le x<t_{i+1}]$.
\end{enumerate}
Iterating the two conditions from an initial guess is the \term{Lloyd--Max algorithm}; each
iteration cannot increase the distortion, so it converges, though possibly to a local optimum
for multimodal densities. Picture eight post offices serving a long valley whose population is
densest in the middle: each household goes to its nearest office, each office moves to the centre
of its customers, and the offices drift together where the people are
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_lloyd_max}). For a 3-bit Gaussian quantizer the optimum gives 14.62\,dB, only
0.35\,dB better than the best uniform quantizer---the gain is modest for a bell curve, larger for
peaky densities such as speech. Its multidimensional generalisation, the $k$-means algorithm of
machine learning (Linde, Buzo and Gray's LBG algorithm in coding), designs the vector quantizers
of speech and image codecs (Chapter~\ref{ch:sourcecoding}).

\mdcfig[0.5]{ch05_lloyd_max}{The optimal 3-bit quantizer for a Gaussian input, from the
Lloyd--Max algorithm (navy), against the best uniform quantizer (orange). Thresholds (dotted) sit
midway between levels; each level is the centre of mass of its cell.}

At high resolution the optimum is easy to state through Bennett's integral. Minimising
$\int p(x)/F'(x)^2\,dx$ subject to $\int F'(x)\,dx$ being fixed (the range of the compressed
variable) gives $F'(x)\propto p(x)^{1/3}$, and the minimum distortion is the
\emph{Panter--Dite} formula (1951),
\begin{equation}
\sigma_e^2\approx\frac{1}{12L^2}\left(\int p(x)^{1/3}\,dx\right)^3.
\label{eq:ch05:panterdite}
\end{equation}
For a Gaussian this is $\frac{\sqrt3\pi}{2}\sigma^2L^{-2}$, about 4.35\,dB worse than the
rate--distortion bound of information theory (Chapter~\ref{ch:sourcecoding}). An important
companion result: at high resolution, a \emph{uniform} quantizer followed by an ideal entropy
coder is within 0.255\,bit per sample (1.53\,dB) of the rate--distortion bound for any smooth
density, so uniform quantization plus entropy coding is nearly optimal---the design behind
almost every modern image, audio and video codec. The logarithmic laws of G.711 are not
optimal for any one density; they are \emph{robust}, giving acceptable performance across a
40\,dB range of densities, which matters more in a telephone network than the last decibel.

8\,kHz. Bottom: the error left by an order-10 linear predictor, on the same scale. Its variance is
about 10\,dB smaller, but what is left is concentrated in sharp pulses at the pitch period, so
its crest factor nearly triples---a fact with consequences.}

\subsection{Prediction gain: tomorrow's weather is like today's}

The laziest weather forecast in the world---``tomorrow will be like today''---is right surprisingly
often. A forecaster who used it would only ever need to report the \emph{surprise}: ``two degrees
warmer than predicted''. Surprises are small numbers, and small numbers need fewer digits.

PCM quantizes each sample as if it knew nothing about the previous ones. But adjacent speech
samples at 8\,kHz are strongly correlated---the normalised autocorrelation at lag one,
$\rho_1=R_x[1]/R_x[0]$, is typically 0.8 to 0.95 for voiced speech---and image pixels even more so.
\term{Differential PCM} (DPCM), patented by C.~Chapin Cutler of Bell Labs in 1952, predicts the
next sample from past samples and quantizes only the \emph{prediction error}, which has much
smaller variance (Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_dpcm_residual}).

Consider the simplest predictor, $\hat x[n]=a\,x[n-1]$. The prediction error
$d[n]=x[n]-ax[n-1]$ has variance
\begin{equation}
\sigma_d^2=\E\big[(x[n]-ax[n-1])^2\big]=\sigma_x^2\,(1-2a\rho_1+a^2),
\end{equation}
which is minimised by $a=\rho_1$, giving $\sigma_d^2=\sigma_x^2(1-\rho_1^2)$. For a fixed number
of bits, the quantization noise is proportional to the variance of what is quantized, so DPCM
improves the SNR by the \term{prediction gain}
\begin{equation}
G_p=\frac{\sigma_x^2}{\sigma_d^2}=\frac{1}{1-\rho_1^2}\quad\text{(first order)}.
\label{eq:ch05:gp}
\end{equation}
With $\rho_1=0.85$, $G_p=3.6$, or 5.6\,dB---nearly one bit per sample. With a $p$th-order
linear predictor $\hat x[n]=\sum_{k=1}^pa_kx[n-k]$, the optimum coefficients solve the
\emph{normal equations} $\sum_ka_kR_x[i-k]=R_x[i]$, $i=1,\dots,p$, the same Wiener--Hopf
equations that appear in equalisation (Chapter~\ref{ch:equalization}), and the
Levinson--Durbin recursion solves them in $O(p^2)$ operations, giving
$G_p=1/\prod_{i=1}^p(1-k_i^2)$ in terms of the reflection coefficients $k_i$
(Chapter~\ref{ch:sourcecoding}). The prediction gain grows quickly up to the order that
captures the spectral envelope---about 8 to 12 for speech at 8\,kHz, two coefficients per
formant---and then saturates, as Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_prediction} (left) shows for a synthetic
vowel. For real speech, a fixed long-term predictor gives roughly 6 to 10\,dB and a predictor
adapted every 10 to 20\,ms a few decibels more.

\subsection{Closed-loop prediction}

There is a trap. The decoder does not have the original samples $x[n-k]$, only its own
reconstructions $\tilde x[n-k]$, which contain quantization noise. If the encoder predicted from
the originals (\emph{open-loop} prediction), the decoder's predictions would differ and the
quantization errors would accumulate in the decoder's integrating loop---like two hikers who agree
to navigate by ``ten paces past the last landmark'' but disagree about where the last landmark was.
DPCM therefore places a copy of the decoder inside the encoder and predicts from the
\emph{reconstructed} samples (Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_dpcm_loop}):
\begin{equation}
\hat x[n]=\sum_{k=1}^pa_k\tilde x[n-k],\qquad d[n]=x[n]-\hat x[n],\qquad
\tilde x[n]=\hat x[n]+Q(d[n]).
\label{eq:ch05:dpcm}
\end{equation}
Then $x[n]-\tilde x[n]=d[n]-Q(d[n])$: the reconstruction error equals the quantization error of
the residual, exactly, with no accumulation. This \emph{closed-loop} or
\emph{analysis-by-synthesis} principle, with the decoder inside the encoder, recurs throughout
the book: in the CELP speech coders of Chapter~\ref{ch:sourcecoding}, in every motion-compensated
video coder, in the decision-feedback equaliser of Chapter~\ref{ch:equalization} and, as we shall
see, in the sigma-delta modulator.

\mdcfig{ch05_prediction}{Left: open-loop prediction gain of a synthetic vowel (with a little
background noise) against predictor order. Most of the gain comes from the first two
coefficients; little is gained beyond order 10. Right: closed-loop DPCM with a fixed predictor and
a fixed uniform quantizer (loading optimised at each rate) against PCM on the same signal. The
dashed line is PCM plus the order-10 prediction gain. DPCM gains about 6\,dB at 2 to 3 bits per
sample, but at 7 bits only about 1.4\,dB, because the prediction residual is far peakier than the
signal.}

Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_prediction} (right) reveals a subtlety that textbooks often skip. The
prediction gain is a ratio of \emph{variances}, but the quantizer's range must be set by
\emph{peaks}. For voiced speech the residual is close to the glottal excitation, a train of
sharp pulses at the pitch period (Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_dpcm_residual}); its crest factor here is 8
to 10, against about 3.7 for the signal itself, so the residual needs about 8\,dB more back-off. At
low rates the optimum loading accepts heavy clipping anyway, so most of the prediction gain is
realised; at high rates the loading penalty eats nearly all of it. The remedy is to make the
quantizer step follow the local signal level, which brings us to adaptive DPCM.

\subsection{Adaptive DPCM}

\term{Adaptive DPCM} (ADPCM) adapts the quantizer step, and usually the predictor, to the
local statistics. Adaptation can be \emph{forward} (the encoder measures the level of each
block and sends the step size as side information) or \emph{backward} (encoder and decoder
both compute the step from past transmitted codes, so no side information is needed but both
must stay in lock-step). Nuggehally Jayant's 1973 adaptive quantizer is the classic backward
scheme: after each sample the step is multiplied by a factor that depends only on the code just
sent, $\Delta_{n+1}=M(|I_n|)\,\Delta_n$, with $M<1$ for the inner codes and $M>1$ for the outer
codes (for a 2-bit quantizer, multipliers of about 0.8 and 1.6). The step grows quickly at the
onset of a pitch pulse or a loud syllable and decays during quiet intervals, so the loading
tracks the signal---a driver who brakes hard when the road surprises him and eases off when it is
straight (Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_adpcm_step}).

\mdcfig{ch05_adpcm_step}{A Jayant adaptive quantizer at work. The 2-bit quantizer's step (red)
swells within a few samples of each loud syllable and shrinks in the quiet, roughly tracking the
envelope (dashed). The decoder computes the same step from the same codes, so no side
information is sent.}

\begin{inpractice}[ADPCM in your cordless phone]
ITU-T G.726 combines a backward-adaptive quantizer with a backward-adaptive pole--zero predictor
(two poles and six zeros, updated by a simplified sign--sign gradient algorithm) and runs at 16,
24, 32 or 40\,kb/s. At 32\,kb/s (four bits per sample) it delivers quality close to 64\,kb/s PCM,
which doubled the capacity of international circuits in the 1980s and is still the voice codec
of DECT cordless telephones. G.722 applies ADPCM in two subbands to 7\,kHz wideband speech.
Chapter~\ref{ch:sourcecoding} (Section~\ref{sec:ch16:speech}) places these waveform coders in the
landscape of speech coding, where analysis-by-synthesis coders reach toll quality at 8\,kb/s.
\end{inpractice}

\subsection{Delta modulation}

Take DPCM to the extreme: sample much faster than Nyquist, predict each sample as the previous
reconstructed value ($p=1$, $a=1$), and quantize the error with a single bit. A \term{delta
modulator} (DM) transmits 1 if the input is above the staircase approximation and 0 if below,
and the staircase steps by $\pm\delta$ accordingly (Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_delta_mod})---a child
playing ``warmer, colder'' with the signal, one word per sample. The decoder is an integrator (an
up/down counter, or an RC network) followed by a low-pass filter. Delta modulation is trivially
simple, needs no word framing and is robust to bit errors, since a single error displaces the
staircase by only $2\delta$. It suffers two impairments:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Slope overload} occurs when the input changes faster than the staircase can follow,
$|x'(t)|>\delta f_s$. For a sine of amplitude $A$ and frequency $f_m$ the condition for no
overload is
\begin{equation}
A\le\frac{\delta f_s}{2\pi f_m}.
\label{eq:ch05:dmslope}
\end{equation}
\item \emph{Granular noise} appears when the input is flat and the staircase hunts around it
in steps of $\delta$. The error is roughly uniform on $[-\delta,\delta]$, of variance $\delta^2/3$.
\end{itemize}
A large step avoids overload but increases granular noise; a small step does the reverse. To
estimate the best SNR, take the largest sine allowed by~\eqref{eq:ch05:dmslope} (power
$\delta^2f_s^2/8\pi^2f_m^2$) and assume the granular noise is white over $0$ to $f_s/2$, so that
a low-pass filter of bandwidth $B$ passes the fraction $2B/f_s$ of it. Then
\begin{equation}
\text{SNR}_{\text{DM}}\approx\frac{\delta^2f_s^2/(8\pi^2f_m^2)}{(\delta^2/3)(2B/f_s)}
=\frac{3}{16\pi^2}\,\frac{f_s^3}{f_m^2B}.
\label{eq:ch05:dmsnr}
\end{equation}
(The constant depends on the assumed noise spectrum; the scaling is what matters.) The SNR grows
as $f_s^3$, 9\,dB per doubling of the bit rate, compared with 6\,dB per extra bit for PCM, which
adds $f_s$ bits per second for each bit of resolution. For speech with $f_m=800$\,Hz and
$B=3.4$\,kHz, a 32\,kb/s delta modulator gives about 25\,dB and a 64\,kb/s one about 34\,dB.
At low bit rates DM is competitive with PCM; at high quality it is not.

\mdcfig{ch05_delta_mod}{Delta modulation: the staircase follows a slow input closely (with
granular noise) but cannot follow the steep segment on the right (slope overload).}

\paragraph{Adaptive delta modulation and CVSD.} Speech alternates between quiet and loud
passages, so a fixed step is always wrong. \term{Continuously variable slope delta
modulation} (CVSD), described by Jan Greefkes and Karel Riemens of Philips in 1970, watches the
output bits: a run of three or four identical bits means the staircase is falling behind
(slope overload is imminent), so the step is increased; otherwise the step decays slowly
towards a minimum (Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_cvsd}). Because the step is computed from the transmitted
bits, the decoder tracks it exactly. CVSD at 16 and 32\,kb/s was the standard digital voice coding
of US and NATO tactical military radio and switching equipment from the 1970s (US MIL-STD-188-113
defines it), valued for its graceful degradation at bit error rates of 1\% or more, where PCM is
unintelligible. It survives in the \emph{Bluetooth} core specification, where synchronous (SCO)
voice links can use 64\,kb/s CVSD as an alternative to $\mu$-law or A-law PCM, precisely because
of that robustness on a noisy 2.4\,GHz link (Chapter~\ref{ch:wifi}).

\mdcfig{ch05_cvsd}{Fixed-step delta modulation against CVSD at 32\,kb/s on a signal that is quiet,
then loud, then medium. The fixed step is too coarse for the quiet passage and far too slow for
the loud one; CVSD's step swells within a few samples when three equal bits appear in a row and
shrinks again afterwards.}

\begin{history}[Delta modulation, Cutler and the road to sigma-delta]
Delta modulation was patented in France in 1946 by E.~M.~Deloraine, S.~van Mierlo and
B.~Derjavitch of the ITT laboratories, and analysed in depth by F.~de Jager of Philips in 1952
(``Delta modulation, a method of PCM transmission using the one-unit code''). At Bell Labs,
C.~Chapin Cutler filed in 1950 the patent on \emph{differential quantization} that introduced
DPCM, and in 1954 a second patent (issued in 1960) that quantized a signal with a coarse
quantizer inside a loop which fed the quantization error back---the idea now called
\emph{error feedback} or \emph{noise shaping}. The decisive step came from Japan. In 1962
Hiroshi Inose, Yasuhiko Yasuda and Jun Murakami of the University of Tokyo published ``A
telemetering system by code modulation: $\Delta$--$\Sigma$ modulation'', which moved the
integrator of a delta modulator from the decoder to the \emph{front} of the encoder, so that the
loop integrated the difference between the input and the fed-back output. The modulator now
coded the signal's amplitude rather than its slope, eliminated slope overload, and needed only
a low-pass filter to decode. They called it delta-sigma; most of the industry later said
sigma-delta. The idea waited two decades for VLSI to supply the cheap digital filters it needs,
and in the 1980s, through work by James Candy at Bell Labs, Bernhard Boser and Bruce Wooley at
Stanford, NTT's MASH team and many others, it took over audio and precision conversion.
\end{history}

\section{Oversampling and sigma-delta conversion}
\label{sec:ch05:sigmadelta}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Oversampling alone}

Here is a converter puzzle. Your phone's audio chip delivers 24-bit samples, yet at its heart sits
a comparator---a one-bit quantizer---and no precision resistor ladder at all. How do you get 24 bits
out of one? The first idea is to sample faster and average.

Sample at $f_s=2B\cdot\mathrm{OSR}$ instead of $2B$, quantize with $N$ bits, and filter the
result digitally to the band $B$. If the quantization noise is white, the filter removes the
fraction $1-1/\mathrm{OSR}$ of it, and by~\eqref{eq:ch05:procgain}
\begin{equation}
\text{SQNR}=6.02N+1.76+10\log_{10}\mathrm{OSR}\ \text{dB}.
\end{equation}
Each doubling of the sample rate buys 3\,dB, half a bit. To turn a 1-bit quantizer into a
16-bit one this way would need an OSR of $4^{15}\approx10^9$. Oversampling alone is far too
slow a lever; it needs help.

\mdcfig{ch05_sd_painter}{A first-order one-bit sigma-delta modulator at work. The output is only
ever $+1$ or $-1$ (grey bars), but where the input (red) is high the $+1$s crowd together, and a
simple moving average of the bits (navy) traces the input closely.}

\subsection{The first-order sigma-delta modulator}

The help is feedback. Imagine a fast, sloppy painter who may only dab pure black or pure white,
but who steps back after every stroke, compares the canvas with the scene, and makes the next
stroke whichever colour reduces the accumulated error. Up close the canvas is a chaos of black and
white dots; from across the room it is a perfect grey photograph. Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_sd_loop}
shows the electronic version, the \term{sigma-delta} ($\Sigma\Delta$) modulator: an integrator
accumulates the difference between the input and the fed-back output, and a coarse quantizer
(often one bit, a single comparator) digitises the integrator output. If the output is too large
on average the integrator ramps down, if too small it ramps up; the loop therefore forces the
\emph{local average} of the coarse output to track the input (Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_sd_painter}).
A DC input of 0.25 on a $\pm1$ scale, for example, produces a pattern with five $+1$s for every
three $-1$s.

Model the quantizer as the addition of an error, $V=U+E$. The integrator output is
$U=\frac{z^{-1}}{1-z^{-1}}(X-V)$. Substituting and solving,
\begin{equation}
V(z)=z^{-1}X(z)+(1-z^{-1})E(z).
\label{eq:ch05:sd1}
\end{equation}
The signal passes with a one-sample delay---the \emph{signal transfer function} is
$\mathrm{STF}=z^{-1}$---while the quantization error is filtered by the \term{noise transfer
function} $\mathrm{NTF}(z)=1-z^{-1}$, a differentiator, whose magnitude
$|\mathrm{NTF}(e^{j2\pi f/f_s})|=2\sin(\pi f/f_s)$ is zero at DC and 2 at $f_s/2$. The loop
does not reduce the total quantization noise; it \emph{moves} it from low frequencies, where
the signal is, to high frequencies, where the decimation filter will remove it
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_noise_moving}). This is \term{noise shaping}---sweeping the dust out of the
room you live in and into the attic. Note the closed-loop principle again: the quantizer's error is
computed from what was actually transmitted, and the loop compensates for it in the next sample.

\begin{analogy}[A fast, sloppy painter]
The one-bit modulator is a painter allowed only black or white dabs, working very fast and
checking the accumulated error after every stroke. Each dab is badly wrong; the running total is
almost exactly right, and seen from a distance (through the decimation filter) the canvas is
smooth. The analogy's limit: the painter's errors are not random speckle but are pushed
systematically to fine detail---high frequencies---which is why a sigma-delta stream must always be
low-pass filtered, and why a careless amplifier that passes the out-of-band noise can be damaged
by it.
\end{analogy}

evenly over $0$ to $f_s/2$ and filtering to a narrow band removes most of it; a sigma-delta loop
(Section~\ref{sec:ch05:sigmadelta}) goes further and pushes the same total noise out of the band.}

\subsection{Higher order and the noise-shaping formula}

Replacing the single integrator by a cascade of $L$ integrators (with suitable feedback paths)
gives the $L$th-order modulator, whose ideal NTF is
\begin{equation}
V(z)=z^{-L}X(z)+(1-z^{-1})^L E(z),\qquad |\mathrm{NTF}(f)|^2=\left(2\sin\frac{\pi f}{f_s}\right)^{2L}.
\label{eq:ch05:sdL}
\end{equation}
The in-band noise follows by integration. With the quantization noise white, of power
$\sigma_e^2$ spread over $0$ to $f_s/2$, and using $2\sin(\pi f/f_s)\approx2\pi f/f_s$ in the
narrow signal band $0\le f\le B=f_s/(2\,\mathrm{OSR})$,
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\text{in-band}}^2=\int_0^B\frac{2\sigma_e^2}{f_s}\left(\frac{2\pi f}{f_s}\right)^{2L}df
=\sigma_e^2\,\frac{\pi^{2L}}{(2L+1)\,\mathrm{OSR}^{2L+1}}.
\label{eq:ch05:sdnoise}
\end{equation}
For an $N$-bit quantizer and a full-scale sine, the peak SQNR is therefore
\begin{equation}
\text{SQNR}=6.02N+1.76-10\log_{10}\frac{\pi^{2L}}{2L+1}+(20L+10)\log_{10}\mathrm{OSR}\ \text{dB}.
\label{eq:ch05:sdsqnr}
\end{equation}
The last term is the payoff: $(6L+3)$\,dB per octave of oversampling, 9\,dB for a first-order loop,
15\,dB for second order, 27\,dB for fourth order (Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_ntf}). In other words, every
doubling of the clock now buys one and a half to four and a half bits instead of half a bit. The
second term is the price: the shaped noise is amplified near $f_s/2$ by up to $2^L$, so the total
noise power rises even as the in-band noise falls.

\mdcfig{ch05_ntf}{Left: noise transfer functions $(1-z^{-1})^L$ for orders 1 to 4; the dashed line
marks the band edge for an OSR of 64. Right: the peak SQNR of~\eqref{eq:ch05:sdsqnr} for a one-bit
quantizer against OSR. The horizontal line is the 98\,dB of a 16-bit converter.}

\mdcfig{ch05_sigma_delta}{Spectra of first- and second-order one-bit sigma-delta modulators
with a tone input. Quantization noise is shaped away from low frequencies; the second-order
loop leaves far less noise in the signal band.}

Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_sigma_delta} shows real spectra of one-bit first- and second-order
modulators: the noise rises at 20 and 40\,dB per decade, exactly as~\eqref{eq:ch05:sdL} predicts.
Because a one-bit quantizer has only two output levels, and two points always define a straight
line, the feedback DAC of a one-bit modulator is \emph{inherently linear}: no component matching
is needed to reach 20-bit linearity. This, more than anything, is why sigma-delta converters
conquered audio and precision measurement.

\begin{worked}[How much oversampling for 16 bits with a second-order loop?]
We want 98\,dB, the SQNR of an ideal 16-bit converter, from a one-bit second-order modulator.
With $N=1$ and $L=2$, equation~\eqref{eq:ch05:sdsqnr} gives
\[
\text{SQNR}=7.78-10\log_{10}\frac{\pi^4}{5}+50\log_{10}\mathrm{OSR}
=7.78-12.90+50\log_{10}\mathrm{OSR}.
\]
Setting this to 98\,dB gives $\log_{10}\mathrm{OSR}=103.1/50=2.06$, an OSR of 115; the next power
of two is 128, for which the formula promises 100.2\,dB. For 48\,kHz audio (a 24\,kHz band) that
means a modulator clock of $128\times48=6.144$\,MHz.

Now the reality check. A simulation of the actual nonlinear loop (lab 16 and
Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_sd_stability}) gives an in-band SQNR of about 70\,dB at OSR 64 and 85\,dB at
OSR 128 for a $-6$\,dBFS input, roughly 10\,dB below the linear model's prediction at the same
input level, and the modulator overloads for inputs above about $-2$\,dBFS. The one-bit quantizer
is not a small additive noise source: its input swings well beyond $\pm1$, its effective gain is
less than one and its error variance exceeds $\Delta^2/12$. To guarantee 16 bits, then, a designer
would choose an OSR of 256 (simulated: about 100\,dB), a higher-order loop, or a multi-bit
quantizer---a 9-level (about 3-bit) quantizer in the same second-order loop gives about 96\,dB at
OSR 64 in simulation. Commercial audio converters use all three tools.
\end{worked}

\subsection{Stability of higher-order loops}

If fourth order gives 27\,dB per octave, why not tenth order? Because a loop with a one-bit
quantizer and a high-order NTF is \emph{unstable}---the painter, told to correct the last ten
strokes at once, overreacts and smears the whole canvas. The ideal NTF $(1-z^{-1})^L$ has a gain of
$2^L$ at $f_s/2$---16 for $L=4$. The quantizer error, amplified that much, drives the
integrators far beyond the range the one-bit quantizer can correct, the internal states grow
without bound, and the output degenerates into long runs of identical bits. In
Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_sd_stability} the ``pure'' fourth-order modulator fails at every input
level.

\mdcfig{ch05_sd_stability}{Left: simulated in-band SQNR of one-bit modulators at OSR 64 against
input amplitude. The second-order loop overloads near full scale; the pure fourth-order loop
$(1-z^{-1})^4$ is unstable at every amplitude and does not appear; fourth- and fifth-order loops
whose NTFs are designed for $\|\mathrm{NTF}\|_\infty=1.5$ are stable up to about $-6$\,dBFS and
reach 92 to 97\,dB. Right: the two fourth-order NTFs. Limiting the out-of-band gain to 1.5 costs
in-band attenuation but buys stability.}

The cure is to give up some in-band attenuation in exchange for a smaller out-of-band gain. The
NTF becomes $N(z)/D(z)$ with the same zeros (at or near DC) and poles that flatten the
high-frequency response. An empirical criterion, \term{Lee's rule} (Wai Laing Lee, 1987), says
that a one-bit modulator is usually stable if the peak out-of-band gain satisfies
$\|\mathrm{NTF}\|_\infty\lesssim1.5$. Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_sd_stability} uses a high-pass
Butterworth NTF scaled to meet it: the fourth-order loop is now stable up to about $-6$\,dBFS
and reaches 92\,dB at OSR 64, and a fifth-order loop about 97\,dB, against 70\,dB for second
order. Two further refinements are standard. Spreading the NTF zeros across the signal band
instead of stacking them at DC (\emph{optimised zeros}) lowers the in-band noise by roughly 10
to 20\,dB for fourth and fifth order. And because no simple criterion guarantees stability, every
design is verified by long simulations, with reset circuitry that clears the integrators if the
loop ever does overload. Richard Schreier's freely available \emph{Delta-Sigma Toolbox} automates
this design process and is the industry's common language.

\paragraph{Idle tones.} Low-order modulators with constant or slowly varying inputs produce
periodic bit patterns. A first-order loop with a DC input of $p/q$ (in lowest terms) outputs a
pattern of period $q$ or $2q$, a tone that can fall in the audio band and be heard as a faint
whistle that changes pitch with the input. Higher-order loops and a little dither (added before
the quantizer, where the loop shapes it like the quantization noise) break up the patterns
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_idle_tones}).

\mdcfig{ch05_idle_tones}{Idle tones. A first-order one-bit modulator with a constant input of 0.01
produces a periodic bit pattern whose spectrum is a comb of lines (red), the lowest of which falls
inside the audio band. With a little dither added before the quantizer (navy) the lines dissolve
into a smooth, shaped noise floor.}

\mdcfig{ch05_decim_chain}{The first decimation stage. The shaped noise of a second-order modulator
(grey) rises steeply above the signal band; a three-stage CIC decimating by 16 (navy) places deep

\subsection{Multi-bit quantizers and MASH}

A quantizer with $2^N$ levels lowers the quantization noise by $6N$\,dB and, more importantly,
makes the loop much more stable, allowing aggressive NTFs and inputs close to full scale. The
price is that the feedback DAC now has several levels whose values depend on component matching;
a mismatch error in the DAC enters the loop at the same point as the input and is \emph{not}
shaped, so a 0.1\% element mismatch limits linearity to roughly 60\,dB. \emph{Dynamic element
matching} solves this: the DAC is built from identical unit elements, and the logic rotates
which elements are used (in \emph{data-weighted averaging}, the elements are used in strict
rotation), so that the mismatch error itself is first-order noise-shaped out of band---like a
team of slightly unequal rowers who swap seats every stroke so that no one's weakness pulls the
boat consistently to one side.

Another route to high order without instability is the \term{MASH} (multi-stage noise shaping)
or cascade architecture, introduced by NTT in 1987. Two stable first-order modulators are
cascaded: the second digitises the first one's quantization error $E_1$. With
$V_1=z^{-1}X+(1-z^{-1})E_1$ and $V_2=z^{-1}E_1+(1-z^{-1})E_2$ (sign conventions as in~\eqref{eq:ch05:sd1}), the digital
combination
\begin{equation}
Y=z^{-1}V_1-(1-z^{-1})V_2=z^{-2}X-(1-z^{-1})^2E_2
\end{equation}
cancels $E_1$ completely and leaves second-order shaping of $E_2$, with each loop individually
stable. Cascades of 2-1, 2-2 and 2-1-1 stages give third- and fourth-order shaping. The catch is
that the cancellation relies on the digital combining filters matching the analog loop filters;
any mismatch (finite op-amp gain, capacitor errors) leaks first-order-shaped $E_1$ into the
output. MASH structures are therefore favoured where the loop is digital---the fractional-$N$
frequency synthesisers of Chapter~\ref{ch:sdr} use a digital MASH 1-1-1 modulator to dither the
divide ratio---or where the analog circuits are very accurate.


\subsection{Decimation filters}
\label{sec:ch05:decim}

The modulator output is a high-rate stream of short words containing the signal plus a large
amount of shaped noise above the band. The \emph{decimation filter} must remove that noise before
it aliases and reduce the rate by the OSR, usually in stages:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A \term{cascaded integrator--comb} (CIC) filter decimates by a large factor, typically 8 to
32, using only adders and registers. Its response is $\big|\sin(\pi fR/f_s)/(R\sin(\pi f/f_s))\big|^K$
for decimation $R$ and $K$ stages, with nulls at every multiple of the output rate---exactly
where the noise would alias onto the signal band (Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_decim_chain}). Because the
shaped noise rises at $20L$\,dB per decade, the CIC needs $K=L+1$ stages to attenuate it faster
than it grows.
\item One or two \emph{half-band} FIR filters, each decimating by 2, in which every other
coefficient is zero (Chapter~\ref{ch:dsp}).
\item A final sharp FIR filter that sets the passband ripple and the transition band,
operating at only two or four times the output rate.
\end{enumerate}
A 48\,kHz audio ADC whose modulator runs at $64\times48\,\text{kHz}=3.072$\,MHz might use a
fifth-order CIC decimating by 16, then two half-band stages. The same chain, reversed, forms the
\emph{interpolation} filter of a sigma-delta DAC, which upsamples 16- or 24-bit words to a few
megahertz, noise-shapes them to one or a few bits and drives a simple, inherently linear
switched-capacitor or current-steering output stage.

\end{tryit}

\subsection{Continuous-time and bandpass sigma-delta}

The modulators above are \emph{discrete-time}: their integrators are switched-capacitor
circuits that operate on samples. In a \emph{continuous-time} (CT) modulator the loop filter is
built from active-RC or $g_m$-$C$ integrators that operate on the continuous input, and the
sampler sits inside the loop just before the quantizer. This has a remarkable consequence: any
signal that would alias at the sampler is suppressed by the loop filter before sampling, so a
CT modulator has \emph{implicit anti-aliasing}, its STF having nulls near every multiple of
$f_s$. CT modulators also avoid the settling requirements of switched capacitors, so they reach
higher clock rates at lower power. Their weaknesses are sensitivity to the feedback DAC's clock
jitter (the DAC pulse width varies with the jitter, so the jitter error enters at the input
unshaped), to \emph{excess loop delay} in the quantizer and DAC, and to RC time-constant
variation, all of which must be calibrated.

\begin{figure}[H]\centering\includegraphics[width=0.45\linewidth]{figs/ch05_bp_sd}
\caption{A second-order bandpass sigma-delta modulator, $\mathrm{NTF}=1+z^{-2}$,
digitising a tone near $f_s/4$ with a one-bit quantizer. The noise is pushed away from a notch at
$f_s/4$ instead of from DC.}\label{fig:ch05_bp_sd}
\end{figure}

Replacing the integrators (poles at DC) by resonators (poles at, say, $f_s/4$) moves the NTF
zeros to the resonance and gives a \emph{bandpass} $\Sigma\Delta$ modulator that digitises a
narrow band around an IF with the same noise-shaping benefits (Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_bp_sd}).
CT lowpass and bandpass
$\Sigma\Delta$ ADCs with signal bandwidths of tens to hundreds of megahertz, clocked at several
gigahertz, are used in cellular handsets, base stations and wideband receivers.

\begin{inpractice}[Where sigma-delta lives]
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Audio.} Every phone, laptop and headset audio codec, and nearly every studio
converter, is a sigma-delta design, typically a third- to fifth-order loop with a multi-bit
quantizer and dynamic element matching, running at 64 to 256 times the output rate. The best
reach dynamic ranges of approximately 120 to 130\,dB, well beyond what any microphone can use.
\item \emph{Precision measurement.} Weigh scales, thermocouple and strain-gauge interfaces and
digital multimeters use high-order $\Sigma\Delta$ ADCs with 24-bit output words and very high
OSR at output rates of a few hertz to a few kilohertz; their decimation filters double as 50/60\,Hz
mains-rejection notches.
\item \emph{Radio.} The receive ADCs of the AD9361 family, including the AD9364 in the USRP
B200, are described by Analog Devices as high-speed sigma-delta converters clocked at several
hundred megahertz, followed by the half-band and FIR decimation chain described in
Section~\ref{sec:ch05:aa}. This is why the B200's dynamic range improves as the channel bandwidth
is reduced: the decimation filters remove more shaped noise (lab 16 suggests measuring it).
\item \emph{Synthesisers and power stages.} Fractional-$N$ PLLs (Chapter~\ref{ch:sdr}), class-D
audio amplifiers and the one-bit DSD audio format of Section~\ref{sec:ch05:audio} are sigma-delta
modulators whose ``quantizer'' is a divider, a power switch or a storage format.
\end{itemize}
\end{inpractice}

\mdcphoto[0.42]{ch05_adc_die}{The die of an Analog Devices AD574, a classic 12-bit
successive-approximation ADC of the late 1970s, packaged as two chips: a precision analog die and
a digital die. The analog die carries laser-trimmed thin-film resistors, adjusted at the factory

\begin{keyidea}[Trade speed for resolution, and put the noise where it does no harm]
Sigma-delta conversion turns the precision problem of data conversion into a speed problem,
which CMOS solves cheaply. A crude quantizer inside a feedback loop, clocked fast, produces an
output whose error is small only where we look---in the signal band---and large where we do not.
The same idea, under different names, is error feedback in audio word-length reduction,
noise-shaped dither, Tomlinson--Harashima precoding (Chapter~\ref{ch:equalization}) and the
fractional-$N$ synthesiser: whenever an error is unavoidable, shape its spectrum away from the
frequencies that matter.
\end{keyidea}

%======================================================================
\section{Real data converters}
\label{sec:ch05:adc}
%======================================================================

An ideal $b$-bit converter is a uniform quantizer preceded by a perfect sampler. A real one has
thermal noise, nonlinearity, jitter, limited bandwidth and a power budget---and it is a physical
object you can photograph (Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_adc_die}). This section explains how converters
are specified, how the main architectures work and where each one fits. Learn to read the data
sheet here and you can read any converter data sheet you will ever meet.

\subsection{Static specifications: offset, gain, DNL and INL}

The \emph{transfer characteristic} of an ADC is the staircase that maps input voltage to output
code. Its departures from the ideal staircase are measured with a slowly swept input (or a
histogram of a sine-wave input). Offset and gain errors shift and tilt the staircase as a whole
and are usually calibrated away. What remains is described by two functions of the code $k$
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_dnl_inl}):
\begin{itemize}
\item \term{Differential nonlinearity} (DNL): the error in the width $W_k$ of each code step,
$\mathrm{DNL}_k=(W_k-\Delta)/\Delta$ in LSBs. A DNL of $-1$ means the code is never produced, a
\emph{missing code}. A converter is guaranteed monotonic and free of missing codes if
$|\mathrm{DNL}|<1$ everywhere.
\item \term{Integral nonlinearity} (INL): the deviation of each code transition from the ideal
straight line, which is the running sum of the DNL, $\mathrm{INL}_k=\sum_{i\le k}\mathrm{DNL}_i$.
\end{itemize}
Think of a staircase built by a careless carpenter: DNL is how much each tread differs from the
standard depth, INL is how far you have drifted from where you should be after climbing to that
step. INL determines harmonic distortion: a smooth bow in the INL curve produces second and third
harmonics, while sharp DNL errors at particular codes (typically the major carry transitions of
a binary-weighted DAC inside the converter, such as code 2047 to 2048 of a 12-bit part) produce
spurs that depend on signal amplitude. These are the spurs that large-scale dither
(Section~\ref{sec:ch05:dither}) averages away.

\mdcfig{ch05_dnl_inl}{Static errors of a 4-bit converter. Left: the real staircase (navy) against
the ideal (dashed); one step has zero width, so code 7 never appears. Right: the DNL of each code
(bars) and the INL (line), its running sum.}

\subsection{Dynamic specifications}

Dynamic performance is measured with a pure sine input and an FFT of the output, using coherent
sampling or a good window (Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_adc_fft}). The key figures are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{SNR}: signal power to the total noise power excluding the first few harmonics.
\item \emph{SINAD} or \emph{SNDR}: signal to noise \emph{and} distortion, all non-signal power.
\item \term{Effective number of bits} (ENOB): inverting~\eqref{eq:ch05:sqnr},
\begin{equation}
\text{ENOB}=\frac{\text{SINAD}_{\text{dB}}-1.76}{6.02},
\label{eq:ch05:enob}
\end{equation}
with SINAD referred to full scale (if the test tone is at $-1$\,dBFS, add 1\,dB first). A 14-bit
RF ADC might deliver 11 to 11.5 effective bits; a 24-bit audio ADC perhaps 20.
\item \term{Spurious-free dynamic range} (SFDR): the ratio of the signal to the largest spur,
in dBc (relative to the carrier) or dBFS. In a receiver with a strong blocker it often matters
more than noise, because a spur can land on a weak wanted signal and no amount of filtering
can remove it. SFDR is frequently 10 to 20\,dB better than SINAD, because SINAD sums all the
noise while SFDR sees only the single worst spur.
\item \emph{Noise spectral density} (NSD): for wideband RF converters, noise per hertz relative to
full scale, $\mathrm{NSD}=-\mathrm{SNR}_{\text{dBFS}}-10\log_{10}(f_s/2)$\,dBFS/Hz. A 14-bit ADC with
72\,dBFS SNR at 250\,MS/s has an NSD of $-72-81=-153$\,dBFS/Hz, the number a radio engineer
plugs into a cascaded noise-figure calculation (Chapter~\ref{ch:sdr}).
\end{itemize}

transfer curve digitises a $-1$\,dBFS tone. The noise floor sets the SNR (71.7\,dBFS), the second

\paragraph{Flash.} A \emph{flash} converter compares the input simultaneously with $2^b-1$
thresholds from a resistor ladder---a row of judges each holding up a card saying ``higher'' or
``lower''. The comparator outputs form a \emph{thermometer code} (all ones below the input level,
all zeros above), which logic converts to binary. It is the fastest architecture, one comparator
delay per sample, but its size and power double with every bit: an 8-bit flash needs 255
comparators. Its characteristic faults are \emph{bubbles} (an isolated wrong comparator in the
thermometer code) and \emph{metastability} (a comparator that cannot decide in time), which create
rare, large ``sparkle'' errors and are suppressed by careful encoding logic.

\paragraph{Successive approximation (SAR).} A SAR converter plays twenty questions with a single
judge (Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_sar_search}). In the first cycle it asks whether the input is above
half scale, in the second whether it is above one or three quarters, and so on, resolving one bit
per cycle in $b$ cycles. The DAC is usually an array of binary-weighted capacitors that also serves
as the sampling capacitor (the \emph{charge-redistribution} SAR of McCreary and Gray, 1975), so the
converter contains almost no static power consumers: just a comparator, switches and logic,
all of which scale beautifully with CMOS technology. SAR converters have become the most
energy-efficient architecture at moderate rates, and the building block of most gigasample
converters.

\mdcfig[0.5]{ch05_sar_search}{A 6-bit SAR conversion of an input at 0.637 of full scale. Each
cycle tries the next bit; the comparator keeps it (green) if the DAC level is still below the
input. After six questions the code is 101000, or 40/64.}

\paragraph{Pipeline.} A \emph{pipeline} converter splits the conversion across a chain of
stages, like a bucket brigade in which each worker estimates a little and passes on what is left.
Each stage samples its input, resolves a few bits with a coarse flash, subtracts the
corresponding DAC value, amplifies the residue (by 2 per resolved bit) and passes it to the next
stage, which works on it while the first stage takes the next sample. Throughput is one sample
per clock; latency is several clocks. The standard trick is \emph{redundancy}: each stage
resolves ``1.5 bits'' (three levels) so that comparator offsets up to a quarter of the stage's
range are corrected digitally by the following stages (Lewis and Gray, 1987). The residue
amplifiers, which must settle accurately within half a clock period, consume most of the power.
Pipelines dominated IF sampling and base stations in the 2000s and are still common.

\paragraph{Sigma-delta.} Section~\ref{sec:ch05:sigmadelta}. Highest resolution, inherent
linearity with one-bit or element-matched DACs, easy anti-aliasing, but bandwidth limited to a
small fraction of the clock rate and latency from the decimation filter.

\paragraph{Time-interleaved.} To exceed what one converter can do, run $M$ identical converters
in rotation, each sampling at $f_s/M$ with clock phases offset by $T_s$ (Ken Poulton's team at
Hewlett-Packard built 1--8\,GS/s interleaved oscilloscope ADCs in the 1980s and 1990s, following
Black and Hodges' 1980 analysis). The rate multiplies by $M$, but so do the problems: if the
sub-converters differ in offset, gain or sampling instant, the output contains periodic errors
with period $M$ samples---like a relay of photographers whose cameras are not quite identical,
so that every fourth picture is a little brighter. Offset differences form a periodic sequence that
appears as fixed spurs at $kf_s/M$. Gain and timing differences \emph{modulate} the signal by a
periodic sequence, which creates images at $kf_s/M\pm f_{\text{in}}$; a timing skew of standard
deviation $\sigma_\tau$ behaves like jitter, contributing an image power of about
$(2\pi f_{\text{in}}\sigma_\tau)^2$ relative to the signal, so skew matters more and more as the input
frequency rises (Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_ti_spurs}). Matching to 0.1\% of gain and 100\,fs of timing is
not achievable by layout alone, so interleaved converters calibrate continuously in the background,
estimating the mismatches from the signal's own statistics and correcting them digitally. With
this, the industry builds 64-way interleaved SAR converters running at 100 to 200\,GS/s for
800\,Gb/s coherent optical transceivers (Chapter~\ref{ch:wireline}).

\mdcfig{ch05_ti_spurs}{Spectrum of a simulated four-way time-interleaved 12-bit ADC with small
mismatches between the sub-converters. Offsets produce spurs at $f_s/4$ and $f_s/2$ whatever
the input; gain and timing mismatches produce images of the input at $f_s/4\pm f_{\text{in}}$ and
$f_s/2-f_{\text{in}}$, 60\,dB below the signal here. The grey spectrum is the same converter
with matched channels.}

\paragraph{The converter landscape.} Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_adc_landscape} summarises where each
architecture lives in the plane of sample rate and effective resolution, with the jitter wall of
Section~\ref{sec:ch05:jitter} superimposed for a Nyquist-frequency input. Two physical limits
bound the landscape: thermal ($kT/C$) noise at the top left, where higher resolution demands
quadratically larger capacitors and power, and aperture jitter at the right, where even 100\,fs
limits a gigahertz input to about 10 bits.

\subsection{Figures of merit}

How good is a converter for its power? Robert Walden's 1999 survey introduced the most widely
quoted figure of merit, the energy per \emph{conversion step},
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{FOM}_{\text{W}}=\frac{P}{2^{\text{ENOB}}\,f_s}\ \ \text{(J per conversion step)},
\label{eq:ch05:walden}
\end{equation}
where $f_s$ is the Nyquist sample rate (twice the signal bandwidth for oversampling converters).
It assumes that power doubles with each extra effective bit, which is roughly true for low and
moderate resolution. At high resolution, where thermal noise dominates and each extra bit costs a
factor of four in power (Section~\ref{sec:ch05:sh}), the \emph{Schreier figure of merit} is fairer:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{FOM}_{\text{S}}=\text{SNDR}_{\text{dB}}+10\log_{10}\frac{B}{P}\ \ \text{(dB)},
\label{eq:ch05:schreier}
\end{equation}
with bandwidth $B$ in hertz and power $P$ in watts. Boris Murmann's continuously updated survey of
converters published at the main circuits conferences shows the best designs at around one to a few
femtojoules per conversion step and Schreier FOMs around 180 to 185\,dB, improving by roughly a factor of two
every few years.

\mdcfig{ch05_adc_landscape}{The landscape of ADC
architectures: approximate regions occupied by commercial and published converters in the 2020s,
in effective resolution against sample rate. The diagonal lines are the jitter
limits~\eqref{eq:ch05:jitter} for an input at the Nyquist frequency $f_s/2$. The regions are
indicative only; see the surveys in the further reading for measured data.}

\begin{worked}[Comparing two converters]
Converter A is a 14-bit, 250\,MS/s pipeline ADC with an SNDR of 71\,dB at a 100\,MHz input,
dissipating 400\,mW. Converter B is a $\Sigma\Delta$ audio ADC with a 20\,kHz bandwidth, 110\,dB
SNDR and 10\,mW. (Both are illustrative, not specific parts.)

For A, $\text{ENOB}=(71-1.76)/6.02=11.5$ and $2^{11.5}=2896$, so
$\mathrm{FOM}_{\text{W}}=0.4/(2896\times2.5\times10^8)=5.5\times10^{-13}$\,J, or 550\,fJ per step.
Its Schreier FOM is $71+10\log_{10}(1.25\times10^8/0.4)=71+84.9=155.9$\,dB.

For B, $\text{ENOB}=17.98$, $2^{17.98}=2.6\times10^5$ and the Nyquist rate is 40\,kS/s, so
$\mathrm{FOM}_{\text{W}}=0.01/(2.6\times10^5\times4\times10^4)=0.96$\,pJ per step: by Walden's
measure B looks \emph{worse} than A. Its Schreier FOM is $110+10\log_{10}(2\times10^4/0.01)=110+63=173$\,dB,
17\,dB \emph{better}. The disagreement is the point: at 18 bits the thermal-noise limit makes
power grow fourfold per bit, which Walden's FOM penalises unfairly. Use $\mathrm{FOM}_{\text{W}}$
to compare moderate-resolution converters and $\mathrm{FOM}_{\text{S}}$ for high-resolution ones.
\end{worked}

\begin{inpractice}[The converters in a software-defined radio]
The USRP B200's AD9364 integrates two 12-bit converters per channel (I and Q) for receive and two
for transmit, with a maximum data rate of 61.44\,MS/s and an RF bandwidth adjustable from about
200\,kHz to 56\,MHz. Because the receive converter is an oversampled sigma-delta design followed by
decimation, its usable dynamic range at narrow bandwidths exceeds what ``12 bits'' suggests. At
the other extreme, AMD's Zynq UltraScale+ RFSoC family integrates up to 16 RF ADCs of 12 or 14
bits at a few gigasamples per second (and higher-rate parts with fewer channels), built as
time-interleaved arrays with background calibration of the interleaving spurs, together with
digital down-converters and the FPGA. A single chip of this kind digitises an entire 5G band
directly at RF. Between them sit the 14- and 16-bit pipeline and SAR converters of most
laboratory receivers and base stations. In every case, the numbers that decide whether the radio
works are the NSD, the SFDR in the presence of blockers and the clock jitter, rather than the
nominal bit count.
\end{inpractice}

\section{Digital telephony}
\label{sec:ch05:telephony}
%======================================================================

The telephone network was the first large system to digitise, and its design choices---8000
samples per second, 8 bits per sample, a 125\,$\mu$s frame---still set the timing of much of the
world's communication infrastructure. Chapter~\ref{ch:history} tells how the T1 carrier came about;
this section explains how it, and the network that grew from it, actually work.

\subsection{The 64\,kb/s channel}

Why 8000 samples per second? The analog long-distance network carried voice channels in
frequency-division multiplex at 4\,kHz spacing, and the channel filters of the time passed
about 300 to 3400\,Hz. Sampling must exceed $2\times3400=6800$\,Hz, and the anti-aliasing filter
in the channel bank needs a transition band; 8000\,Hz, twice the FDM channel spacing, leaves 1.2\,kHz
between the passband edge and the Nyquist frequency and allows a modest filter
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_why_8k}). Why 8 bits?
Because 8-bit companded PCM gives the 35 to 38\,dB of SQNR over a 40\,dB range of talker levels
that matched the quality of the analog toll network (Section~\ref{sec:ch05:companding}). The
product is the \term{DS0} (digital signal level 0) channel of 64\,kb/s, the atom of the digital
telephone network and of ISDN.

\begin{history}[title=The 8 kHz heartbeat]
The number 8000 appears everywhere in communications because telephone speech was
band-limited to 3.4\,kHz and the designers of T1 chose a comfortable margin above the
6.8\,kHz Nyquist rate. From that choice follow the 125\,$\mu$s frame, the 64\,kb/s DS0, the
1.544 and 2.048\,Mb/s primary rates, SONET's 51.84\,Mb/s, the 8\,kHz frame clock of
ISDN and GSM's 20\,ms speech frames. A sampling-rate decision made for a vacuum-tube
filter in the 1950s still sets the timing of the global network.
\end{history}

\subsection{Channel banks and the T1 frame: 24 calls taking turns}

Picture 24 people who must share one narrow footbridge. They could not cross side by side, but if
each takes one quick step in turn---person 1, person 2, \dots, person 24, then person 1 again---and
the whole cycle repeats 8000 times a second, every one of them makes steady progress and nobody
notices the queue. That is \term{time-division multiplexing} (TDM), and it is how the T1 line
carries 24 telephone calls on two twisted pairs (Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_tdm_interleave}).

The device that converts 24 analog voice circuits into a T1 stream and back is a \term{channel
bank}. The first, Bell's D1 bank of 1962, sampled each channel, companded it with an analog diode
compressor ($\mu=100$) and coded it with 7 bits, using the eighth bit of each channel's time
slot for signalling. Later banks (D2 from the late 1960s, then D3, D4 and D5) moved to the
8-bit $\mu=255$ segmented code of G.711, implemented digitally so that every bank in the network
had identical characteristics---essential once calls passed through many digital links in
tandem.

\begin{history}[T1: digital telephony goes to work, 1962]
T1 was not built to be revolutionary; it was built to save copper. By the late 1950s the cable
ducts between telephone offices in American cities were full, and digging new ones was expensive.
Bell Labs engineers realised that the existing pairs could carry many calls each if the calls were
digitised and the pairs were fitted with regenerative repeaters. Conveniently, the cables already
had manholes about every 6000\,ft, where loading coils were installed for voice; the coils were
removed and the T1 repeaters put in their place. The first system entered commercial service in
the Chicago area in 1962, carrying 24 calls on two pairs that had previously carried two. Transistors
made it practical where Reeves's vacuum tubes had not, and within two decades T1 and its successors
had turned the interoffice network digital.
\end{history}

\mdcfig{ch05_tdm_interleave}{Time-division multiplexing. Each call is sampled in turn (left, with
the sampling instants staggered), and its samples are slotted one after another into a repeating
frame (right). Every 125\,$\mu$s each call gets one 8-bit slot; a framing bit (grey) marks where each
frame begins.}


Each channel gets a time slot of eight bits in a repeating frame. The T1 (DS1) frame
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_t1_frame}) contains 24 slots plus one framing bit, 193 bits every 125\,$\mu$s,
giving $193\times8000=1.544$\,Mb/s. One framing bit per frame is very little, so the framing pattern
is spread across groups of frames:
\begin{itemize}
\item The \emph{superframe} (SF, or D4 framing) groups 12 frames. The framing bits of the odd
frames carry the terminal-framing pattern 101010 that the receiver locks to; those of the even
frames carry 001110, which marks frames 6 and 12.
\item The \emph{extended superframe} (ESF, ANSI T1.403) groups 24 frames and divides the
8\,kb/s of framing bits three ways: a 2\,kb/s framing pattern (001011 in frames 4, 8, \dots, 24),
a 2\,kb/s CRC-6 computed over the previous extended superframe for in-service error monitoring,
and a 4\,kb/s \emph{facility data link} for performance reports and maintenance messages.
\end{itemize}

\mdcfig{ch05_t1_frame}{The T1 (DS1) frame: one framing bit and 24 eight-bit $\mu$-law samples
every 125\,$\mu$s, for 1.544\,Mb/s.}

Signalling (on-hook, off-hook, dial pulses) is carried by stealing the least significant bit of
every channel in frames 6 and 12 of the superframe (the A and B bits), or frames 6, 12, 18 and 24
of the ESF (A, B, C, D): \term{robbed-bit signalling} (Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_robbed_bit}). For voice
the damage is negligible, an average of $8-\frac16$ bits per sample. For data it is fatal to the
LSB, and because a terminal cannot know which frames will be robbed (a call may cross several T1
spans with unrelated superframe alignments), data over a robbed-bit trunk can trust only 7 bits per
sample, $7\times8000=56$\,kb/s. That is why the ``56K'' modems of the late 1990s (ITU-T V.90) and
the switched data services of North America stopped at 56\,kb/s, while Europe's E1 carried clear
64\,kb/s channels.

\mdcfig{ch05_robbed_bit}{The D4 superframe: twelve T1 frames (rows) of 24 channels (columns). The
framing bits down the left spell 100011011100; the change in the even-frame pattern marks frames 6
and 12, in which the least significant bit of every channel is borrowed for signalling bits A and B.}

\begin{worked}[T1 overhead and capacity]
A T1 carries $24\times64=1536$\,kb/s of channel payload out of 1544\,kb/s, an overhead of only
8\,kb/s or 0.52\%. With robbed-bit signalling, each channel loses one bit in six frames, so the
signalling capacity per channel is $8000/6=1333$\,b/s (two signalling bits, A and B, alternating);
the voice channel carries $8000\times(8-1/6)=62.7$\,kb/s on average, and a data channel is
limited to 56\,kb/s. When the signalling is instead carried in a separate channel
(\emph{common-channel} signalling, as in ISDN's primary rate interface below), the T1 carries 23
clear 64\,kb/s channels plus a 64\,kb/s signalling channel. For comparison, the E1 frame spends
two of 32 slots on framing and signalling: 6.25\% overhead, but 30 clean 64\,kb/s channels.
\end{worked}

On the wire, T1 uses the bipolar \emph{alternate mark inversion} (AMI) line code at 1.544\,Mb/s
on two twisted pairs (one for each direction), with regenerative repeaters about every 6000\,ft
(1.8\,km). AMI has no DC component and provides a built-in error check (two marks of the same
polarity are a \emph{bipolar violation}), but a long run of zeros carries no transitions for the
repeaters' clock recovery. Early T1 therefore required a minimum density of ones (which is why
$\mu$-law inverts its bits), and later T1 adopted \emph{B8ZS}, which replaces eight consecutive
zeros by a pattern containing two deliberate bipolar violations that the receiver recognises and
removes; E1 uses the similar HDB3, which replaces four zeros. Chapter~\ref{ch:baseband}
(Section~\ref{sec:ch08:linecodes}) analyses these codes and their spectra.

\subsection{The E1 frame}

The CEPT (European) primary rate, standardised in ITU-T G.704 and G.732, organises 32 time slots
of 8 bits into a 256-bit frame every 125\,$\mu$s: $256\times8000=2.048$\,Mb/s
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_e1_frame}). Slot 0 carries framing: in alternate frames a frame-alignment
signal (the pattern 0011011 in bits 2--8), and in the others a ``1'' in bit 2 (so that the
alignment pattern cannot be imitated), a remote-alarm bit and national-use bits. Bit 1 of slot 0
can carry a CRC-4 check computed over a 16-frame multiframe, the counterpart of ESF's CRC-6. Slot 16
carries \emph{channel-associated signalling} (CAS): over a 16-frame multiframe it carries four
signalling bits (a, b, c, d) for each of the 30 voice channels, two channels per frame, after a
multiframe alignment pattern in frame 0. Alternatively, slot 16 carries a common-channel signalling
link (Signalling System No.\,7 or ISDN's D channel). The remaining 30 slots are clear 64\,kb/s
channels. Where T1 squeezed its housekeeping into stolen bits, E1 gave it rooms of its own.

\subsection{Digital switching: a post office for time slots}

Once voice is a sequence of 8-bit samples in time slots, a telephone exchange can switch calls by
moving samples from one slot to another. Think of a sorting office. Letters arrive in a sack in
one order; a clerk files each into a numbered pigeonhole as it arrives, and a second clerk, reading
from a list, collects them in a different order for the outgoing sack. Nobody moves a wire; the
``connection'' is just the order in which the pigeonholes are emptied.

\begin{analogy}[A post office for time slots]
In the sorting office, each letter waits in its pigeonhole for at most one round of collection;
in a time-slot interchange, each sample waits at most one 125\,$\mu$s frame. The analogy's limit:
a real post office can fall behind on a busy day, but a TSI never does---every slot is written and
read exactly once per frame, so its capacity is fixed by memory speed, and a call is either given
a path or refused (blocked) at set-up time.
\end{analogy}

The electronic version is the \term{time-slot interchange} (TSI): a memory with one word per time
slot. Samples from the incoming frame are written into the memory sequentially, slot by slot;
samples for the outgoing frame are read out in an order given by a \emph{control memory} that the
call-processing software sets up when a call is connected (Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_tst}). If the
control memory says that outgoing slot 7 should read word 3, the caller in incoming slot 3 is
connected to the listener on outgoing slot 7. Each connection is delayed by up to one frame,
125\,$\mu$s, which is inaudible.

A single TSI is limited by memory speed: each slot needs one write and one read every 125\,$\mu$s,
so a TSI serving $N$ channels needs a memory cycle time below $125\,\mu\text{s}/2N$, 61\,ns for
1024 channels. Large exchanges therefore combine time and space switching. In a
\emph{time--space--time} (TST) switch, each incoming TDM stream enters its own TSI, which moves
each sample into a free internal time slot; a \emph{space switch}, a crossbar of logic gates
reconfigured in every time slot, connects the TSI outputs to the outgoing TSIs; and a second
stage of TSIs moves each sample into the outgoing slot it needs. Because the internal slot can be
chosen freely, the switch can be made non-blocking with a modest expansion of the internal rate.

\mdcpairany{ch05_crossbar}{Before time-slot switching: line-finder and selector frames of an NTT
Model C400 crossbar exchange in Tokyo. Every call through such an office held a physical path of
relay contacts for its whole duration.}{ch05_5ess}{After: a Lucent 5ESS, the digital switch that
succeeded the 4ESS family, here serving as a GSM mobile switching centre. Calls are now numbers
moved between time slots in memory.}

\begin{inpractice}[The digital exchange]
France put one of the first time-division digital exchanges, the E10 system developed by CNET
and Alcatel, into service in Brittany in 1970. In North America, AT\&T's \textbf{4ESS}, a TST
toll switch able to terminate on the order of 100\,000 trunks, entered service in Chicago in
January 1976; it was the first digital switch in the long-distance network, and switching digital
trunks directly removed the need to convert each call back to analog at every office. Northern
Telecom's DMS-100 (1979) and AT\&T's 5ESS (1982) brought digital switching to local exchanges
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_5ess}), with line cards that contained the codec (a single-chip $\mu$-law or
A-law coder--decoder and its filters, one of the first mass-market mixed-signal integrated circuits)
for each subscriber. By the 1990s the analog network survived only in the copper loop between the
subscriber and the line card. Today the same functions run as software on servers, and the TSI has
become a packet switch, but many carriers still operate TDM switches for legacy services.
\end{inpractice}

\subsection{Echo and delay in the digital network}

Shout into a canyon and your voice comes back a moment later. If the echo returns quickly it merges
with your own voice and you never notice it; if it comes back a quarter of a second later it is
maddening. Digital transmission removed noise and loss from the telephone network but exposed
exactly this problem. The \emph{hybrid} that converts the two-wire subscriber loop to the four-wire
transmission network leaks part of the far-end talker's voice back towards them, typically 6 to
20\,dB down (Chapter~\ref{ch:wireline}, Section~\ref{sec:ch24:hybrid}). On a short analog call the
echo returns within a few milliseconds and merges with sidetone; but digital equipment adds
delay---125\,$\mu$s per TSI stage, several milliseconds for each codec and buffer, 270\,ms one way
through a geostationary satellite (Chapter~\ref{ch:satellite}), 20 to 100\,ms for packet
voice---and echo that returns more than about 25\,ms after it was spoken must be controlled
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_delay_budget}).

\mdcfig{ch05_delay_budget}{Where the delay comes from, on a logarithmic scale. A time-slot
interchange adds a mere 125\,$\mu$s; packet voice and satellites add tens to hundreds of
milliseconds, well past the point at which a talker's echo becomes objectionable and must be
cancelled.}

Because the digital network is linear between the two hybrids, the echo path is a fixed linear
filter that an adaptive FIR filter can learn from the far-end signal and subtract. These
\term{echo cancellers}, specified in ITU-T G.168 with echo tails of 64 to 128\,ms, were among the
first mass applications of adaptive signal processing (Chapter~\ref{ch:equalization},
Section~\ref{sec:ch12:echo}). Note the dependence on the quantization of this chapter: a canceller
working on $\mu$-law samples must first expand them to linear form, and the residual echo can be no
smaller than the quantization noise of the echo itself.

\subsection{The digital hierarchy}

Primary-rate frames are multiplexed again into higher rates, like local trains feeding an express.
Table~\ref{tab:ch05:pdh} lists the \term{plesiochronous digital hierarchy} (PDH) levels.
``Plesiochronous'' means \emph{nearly} synchronous: each tributary has its own clock, accurate to
some tens of parts per million, so the multiplexer runs slightly faster than the sum of its inputs
and inserts \emph{justification} (stuffing) bits to absorb the rate differences, with control bits
telling the demultiplexer which bits to discard. That is why a DS2 at 6.312\,Mb/s carries four DS1s
totalling only 6.176\,Mb/s. The consequence was operational pain: a DS1 was buried at an
unpredictable position inside a DS3, and extracting it meant demultiplexing everything.

replaced this with byte-synchronous frames repeating every 125\,$\mu$s---the sampling period of

\subsection{ISDN: the digital line to the subscriber}

The \term{Integrated Services Digital Network} (ISDN), standardised by the CCITT in the 1980s
(the I-series recommendations), extended the 64\,kb/s channel to the customer. The \emph{basic
rate interface} (BRI) provides two 64\,kb/s B (bearer) channels and a 16\,kb/s D (signalling and
packet) channel, 2B+D or 144\,kb/s, over the existing copper loop. In North America the loop
interface (the U reference point) carries it with the four-level 2B1Q line code at 80\,kbaud,
160\,kb/s including framing and maintenance, using echo cancellation for full-duplex
transmission on one pair (Chapter~\ref{ch:wireline}); inside the customer premises the S/T
interface runs at 192\,kb/s on two pairs. The \emph{primary rate interface} (PRI) is a T1 or E1
with common-channel signalling: 23B+D in North America and Japan, 30B+D in Europe, with a
64\,kb/s D channel.

ISDN never became the universal residential service its designers expected; it was overtaken in
the late 1990s by DSL and cable modems, which offered far more than 128\,kb/s. But it mattered:
its D-channel protocol (Q.931) became the call-control basis of later systems, PRI trunks
connected private exchanges and call centres to the network for decades, and BRI carried
broadcast-quality audio links and videoconferences in the 1990s (Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_isdn_ta}).

\subsection{Voice over IP: G.711 lives on}

From the 2000s, voice moved from circuits to packets. In voice over IP, samples are collected
into packets, typically every 20\,ms, and carried in the \emph{Real-time Transport Protocol}
(RTP) over UDP and IP. Modern codecs such as AMR-WB, EVS and Opus (Chapter~\ref{ch:sourcecoding})
compress speech to 6 to 64\,kb/s, but G.711 remains the universal fallback that every VoIP phone,
gateway and SIP trunk must support (Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_ipphone}), because it is simple, has no
algorithmic delay, carries DTMF tones and fax and modem signals tolerably, and transcodes to the
legacy network losslessly. In RTP it has its own static payload types, 0 for $\mu$-law (PCMU) and
8 for A-law (PCMA) (IETF RFC 3551). A curiosity of the same RFC is that G.722, which samples at
16\,kHz, is signalled with an RTP clock rate of 8000\,Hz, an error in an early version of the
specification that had to be preserved for compatibility.

\begin{worked}[The real bit rate of a G.711 VoIP call]
With 20\,ms packetisation, each packet carries $0.02\times8000=160$ samples of one byte. The
headers add 12 bytes of RTP, 8 of UDP and 20 of IPv4, 40 bytes in all, so each packet is 200 bytes
and there are 50 packets per second: $200\times8\times50=80$\,kb/s at the IP layer, 25\% more than
the 64\,kb/s payload. On Ethernet, each frame adds 18 bytes of header and frame check sequence plus
20 bytes of preamble and inter-frame gap, so the wire rate is $238\times8\times50=95.2$\,kb/s per
direction (Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_voip_packet}). Shortening the packets to 10\,ms to reduce delay
doubles the header overhead (the Ethernet wire rate becomes 126.4\,kb/s); lengthening them to 40\,ms
saves bandwidth but adds delay and makes each lost packet more audible. This is why header
compression (RoHC) matters on cellular links, where VoLTE squeezes the 40-byte header to a few bytes.
\end{worked}

\mdcfig{ch05_voip_packet}{The anatomy of a G.711 packet on Ethernet. With 20\,ms of speech per
packet the headers add half as much again to the 160-byte payload; with 10\,ms packets they nearly
equal it.}

The circuit network's 125\,$\mu$s frame is invisible in VoIP, but its fingerprints are not: the
8\,kHz sampling rate, the 64\,kb/s rate, the $\mu$-law and A-law codes and the echo cancellers of the
previous subsections all survive in the media gateways that still connect packet voice to the
remaining TDM world, and in the mobile networks' fallback modes.

\section{Digital audio: sampling rates and formats}
\label{sec:ch05:audio}
%======================================================================

The telephone network chose its parameters for intelligible speech. Consumer and professional
audio chose theirs for high fidelity, and the reasons for the numbers are a small history of
technology. An LP record stores sound as a wiggling groove, a direct analog of the waveform
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_vinyl}); a compact disc stores it as a spiral of pits encoding 16-bit numbers.
The rest of this section is the story of how the numbers were chosen.

\mdcpairany{ch05_vinyl}{Analog storage: the grooves of an LP, magnified. The waveform is cut
directly into the vinyl, so every speck of dust and every wobble of the turntable becomes part of
the sound.}{ch05_cdp101}{Digital storage: the Sony CDP-101, the first compact-disc player sold to
the public, launched in Japan in October 1982.}

\paragraph{The compact disc: 44.1\,kHz and 16 bits.} The CD standard (the Philips--Sony ``Red
Book'' of 1980, launched commercially in 1982) stores two channels of 16-bit linear PCM at
44.1\,kHz, 1.4112\,Mb/s of audio. Sixteen bits gives 98\,dB of theoretical SQNR, more than
analog tape or vinyl, and 44.1\,kHz places the Nyquist frequency at 22.05\,kHz, just above the
roughly 20\,kHz limit of human hearing, leaving the narrow transition band whose filter we
designed in Section~\ref{sec:ch05:aa}. On the disc, the audio is protected by the cross-interleaved
Reed--Solomon code and modulated with eight-to-fourteen modulation, raising the channel bit rate to
4.3218\,Mb/s (Chapter~\ref{ch:classiccodes}).

\begin{history}[Why 44.1\,kHz? Digital audio on videotape]
In the late 1970s, the only affordable recorder that could store a megabit per second was a video
cassette recorder. Sony's PCM adaptors (the PCM-1600 used for CD mastering, and consumer units such
as the PCM-F1) encoded digital audio as black-and-white dots in the active lines of a television
signal, three samples per channel per video line. In the NTSC system, with 245 usable lines per field
and 60 fields per second, that gives $3\times245\times60=44\,100$ samples per second. In PAL, with
294 usable lines and 50 fields per second, $3\times294\times50=44\,100$ as well. A rate that worked
with both television standards, high enough for a 20\,kHz bandwidth and convenient for mastering
on video equipment, was the natural choice when the CD was defined. (Strictly, NTSC colour video runs
at 59.94 fields per second, and some NTSC-based equipment recorded at 44.056\,kHz.) The professional
world, meanwhile, settled on 48\,kHz, which divides more conveniently into film and video frame rates
and allows a wider transition band; DAT (1987) recorded at 48\,kHz, and could play 44.1\,kHz
recordings, while a long-play mode used 32\,kHz, the rate also used in broadcast distribution.
\end{history}

\mdcphoto[0.6]{ch05_pcm1630}{A Sony PCM-1630 digital audio processor, successor to the PCM-1600.
Paired with a professional video recorder, units like this stored the 16-bit, 44.1\,kHz masters
from which compact discs were pressed---the reason the CD's sample rate fits a television line.}

\begin{history}[The compact disc goes on sale, 1982]
Philips and Sony, who had each been developing optical digital audio discs, agreed in 1979 to
pool their work, and the joint ``Red Book'' standard followed in 1980. Broadly, Philips brought
its optical-disc and laser-pickup experience and Sony its experience with digital audio and error
correction. The first players reached Japanese shops on 1 October
1982, led by Sony's CDP-101 (Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_cdp101}), with Philips' CD100 following in Europe
early in 1983. The disc's 12\,cm diameter and roughly 74-minute playing time are often said to have
been chosen so that one disc could hold Beethoven's Ninth Symphony; the story is widely repeated
and probably at least partly true, though the participants' accounts differ. Within a decade the
CD had overtaken the LP, and within two it had been overtaken in turn by files---which still use
its 44.1\,kHz and 16 bits.
\end{history}

\mdcphoto[0.45]{ch05_cd100}{A Philips CD100, Philips' first compact-disc player, on display at
Amberley Museum in England. Inside, a 14-bit DAC driven at four times the sample rate did the work
that would otherwise have needed an 11th-order analog filter.}

\paragraph{Oversampling and the CD player.} The CD's tight transition band would have demanded an
elliptic reconstruction filter of order 9 to 11. Philips' first players (1982) instead used a
14-bit DAC, the TDA1540, driven at four times the sample rate by a digital interpolation filter,
with first-order noise shaping recovering most of the lost resolution in the audio band. Within a
decade, nearly every player used a one-bit or few-bit sigma-delta DAC running at 64 to 256 times
44.1\,kHz, the architecture that persists in every phone and headphone amplifier
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_cdp101_inside}).

\mdcpairany{ch05_cdp101_inside}{Inside a CDP-101. Much of the first player's circuitry was
digital: error correction, interpolation and control logic, decoding the cross-interleaved
Reed--Solomon code in real time.}{ch05_dat}{A DAT cassette (left) beside a compact cassette.
DAT recorded 16-bit PCM at 48\,kHz on a helical-scan tape only about half the size.}

\paragraph{High resolution.} Studio production uses 24-bit words at 48, 96 or 192\,kHz. The extra
bits provide headroom during mixing (a recording made with 20\,dB of safety margin still has 16 bits
of resolution) and the higher rates relax the anti-alias filters and simplify processing. Whether
playback at more than 48\,kHz and 16 to 20 bits is audible to anyone is a contested question; the
best converters achieve approximately 120 to 130\,dB of dynamic range, the equivalent of 20 to 21
effective bits, which already exceeds the dynamic range of any listening room.
Figure~\ref{fig:ch05_audio_formats} maps the formats.

\paragraph{DSD: sigma-delta as a format.} The Super Audio CD (1999) stored the one-bit output of a
sigma-delta modulator directly, at $64\times44.1\,\text{kHz}=2.8224$\,MHz: \emph{Direct Stream
Digital} (DSD). Playback needs only a low-pass filter. Its noise is shaped (rising steeply above
about 20\,kHz), so the format's performance is exactly that of Section~\ref{sec:ch05:sigmadelta}:
high in-band SQNR and a large amount of out-of-band noise that the playback chain must not
amplify. Professional editing of DSD is awkward---a one-bit stream cannot be mixed or equalised
without conversion to multi-bit PCM---which limited its adoption, but DSD files persist as a
niche high-resolution download format.

the 8\,kHz, 8-bit telephone channel to 24-bit studio formats and the 2.8\,MHz one-bit DSD stream.

multi-bit sigma-delta designs running at many times the output rate, delivering 24-bit words at up

\section{Summary}
%======================================================================

\begin{itemize}
\item PCM is sampling, quantization and coding. Sampling a band-limited signal above twice its
bandwidth loses nothing; the sampled spectrum is the original replicated every $f_s$, and the
signal is recovered by ideal low-pass (sinc) interpolation. Aliasing---the wagon wheel turning
backwards---cannot be undone once it has happened, so the anti-alias filter comes first.
\item Oversampling trades analog filter complexity for digital processing: a CD's anti-alias
filter drops from an elliptic 11th-order design to a third-order RC filter at $64\times$.
Bandpass sampling digitises a band of width $B$ in a higher Nyquist zone at about $2B$, if the
rate lies in one of the allowed wedges; even zones are inverted.
\item Sampling-clock jitter limits the SNR to $-20\log_{10}(2\pi f\sigma_t)$, independent of
resolution and sample rate: about 100\,fs is needed for 14-bit performance at 100\,MHz.
$kT/C$ noise sets the size, and hence power, of the sampling capacitor.
\item A zero-order-hold DAC multiplies the spectrum by $\sinc(f/f_s)$, with 3.92\,dB of droop at
$f_s/2$; inverse-sinc filters and digital interpolation correct it.
\item Uniform quantization noise is $\Delta^2/12$ when the signal is busy (Widrow's theorem says
when), giving $6.02b+1.76$\,dB for a full-scale sine and $6.02b+4.77-20\log_{10}\Gamma$ for a
loading factor $\Gamma$. Dither makes the additive model exact: subtractive RPDF dither, or
non-subtractive TPDF dither at the cost of 4.8\,dB.
\item Logarithmic companding keeps the SQNR constant over a wide range of levels. G.711's
$\mu$-law ($\mu=255$) and A-law ($A=87.6$) are binary-friendly chord approximations that give
8-bit codes the small-signal resolution of 13- and 12-bit uniform quantizers, about 36--38\,dB of
SQNR over 40\,dB of level, and define the 64\,kb/s channel.
\item Prediction reduces the variance to be quantized by the prediction gain, but the encoder must
predict from reconstructed samples; peaky residuals need adaptive quantization (ADPCM). Delta
modulation and CVSD are one-bit DPCM, robust but limited by slope overload and granular noise.
\item Sigma-delta modulators shape quantization noise out of the signal band by
$(1-z^{-1})^L$, gaining $(6L+3)$\,dB per octave of oversampling. High-order one-bit loops need
NTFs with limited out-of-band gain (Lee's rule) to stay stable; multi-bit quantizers with
element matching and MASH cascades are the alternatives. A CIC-plus-half-band decimator completes
the converter.
\item Converters are judged by SNR, SINAD, ENOB, SFDR, DNL/INL and NSD, and compared by Walden or
Schreier figures of merit. Flash, SAR, pipeline, sigma-delta and time-interleaved architectures
cover the landscape between the thermal-noise and jitter limits; interleaving adds spurs at
$kf_s/M$ and $kf_s/M\pm f_{\text{in}}$ that must be calibrated.
\item The digital telephone network is built on the 8\,kHz, 8-bit, 64\,kb/s DS0; T1 (1.544\,Mb/s,
193-bit frames, SF/ESF, robbed-bit signalling) and E1 (2.048\,Mb/s, 32 slots); time-slot
interchange switching; echo cancellation; the PDH and SONET/SDH hierarchies; and ISDN. G.711 lives
on in VoIP. Audio formats chose 44.1 and 48\,kHz for reasons of video tape and film, and DSD
stores a sigma-delta bit stream directly.
\end{itemize}

\item S.~P.~Lipshitz, R.~A.~Wannamaker and J.~Vanderkooy, ``Quantization and dither: a theoretical
survey,'' \emph{Journal of the Audio Engineering Society}, vol.~40, no.~5, 1992. The definitive
treatment of subtractive and non-subtractive dither, including why TPDF is the right choice for audio.
\item R.~H.~Walden, ``Analog-to-digital converter survey and analysis,'' \emph{IEEE Journal on

performance stands.
\item H.~Inose, Y.~Yasuda and J.~Murakami, ``A telemetering system by code modulation:

\item ITU-T Recommendations G.711 (PCM of voice frequencies: $\mu$-law and A-law), G.704 (frame
structures for 1544 and 2048\,kbit/s, including SF, ESF, CRC-4 and CAS), G.726 (ADPCM), G.168

authority on every number in Section~\ref{sec:ch05:telephony}.
\item IETF RFC 3550 and RFC 3551, the RTP protocol and its audio/video profile, which assign the
PCMU and PCMA payload types and define how G.711 travels in VoIP.
